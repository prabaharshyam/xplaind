## What it is

Three cost levers that work together:

- **Model tiering** (or routing). Not every request needs the most capable model. Classify, extract or answer simple questions with a small, cheap, fast model, and send only the hard ones to a large one.
- **Reasoning effort.** Current models think before they answer, and those thinking tokens are billed as output tokens, even when you never see the text. An effort setting controls how much the model thinks. Easy, high-volume work rarely needs the default.
- **Batching.** Work that does not need an answer in seconds goes through a provider's **batch API**: you upload many requests at once and collect results later at a large discount. Examples are nightly document tagging, summarising yesterday's tickets and re-scoring an archive.

A note on words. In this lesson "batching" means the provider's discounted, asynchronous product. **Continuous batching** is something else: it is how a serving engine such as vLLM packs many requests onto your own GPUs at once. The lesson "Inside LLM inference: prefill, decode and the KV cache" covers that.

All three levers depend on knowing your traffic: what share of requests is easy, and what share can wait.

```flow
title: Where each request goes
Incoming work -> Can it wait hours?
Can it wait hours? -[yes]-> Batch API at half price | match results by your id, retry failures
Can it wait hours? -[no]-> Small model, low effort -> Validator passes?
Validator passes? -[yes]-> Answer
Validator passes? -[no, about 7%]-> Large model -> Answer
Hard tasks such as fit summaries -[directly]-> Large model
```

This lesson follows a resume-screening service at an HR-tech company in Pune processing 400,000 resumes a month.

## How it works

### Three tiering patterns

- **Fixed by task.** Each feature uses the cheapest model that passes its evals. Extraction from a standard form may need only a small model; a legal summary may need a large one. This is the simplest and most common pattern. Start here.
- **Cascade.** Try the small model first, check its output, and escalate to the large model only when the check fails. It suits tasks where a cheap check can tell good output from bad, such as structured extraction.
- **Router.** A classifier, or a small model, predicts difficulty and picks the model up front. Research systems such as RouteLLM learn this from preference data. A router suits chat, where you cannot afford two calls in a row and there is no output to check yet.

Every route needs its own eval. The real question is not "is the small model good?" but "is it good enough on the requests we send it?"

### The cascade cost formula

For each item, a cascade costs the small call, plus the large call for the share that escalates:

`cost per item = small cost + escalation rate × large cost`

Escalated items pay for both calls. So a small model at one tenth of the price with 40% escalation costs 0.1 + 0.4 = 0.5 of the large model alone: half price, if quality holds. With a price gap of 20 times or more, as between Haiku 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5, the cascade stays cheaper until almost every item escalates. The money is rarely the risk. Two things are: the wrong answers the check lets through, and the extra wait for escalated requests.

### Validators and their miss rate

The check decides what escalates, so test it like any other model. Use cheap checks in layers:

1. **Format.** Does the output parse against the schema?
2. **Range.** Are values plausible? For example, years of experience between 0 and 50, and at least one skill.
3. **Grounding.** Does the answer appear in the source? For example, the candidate's name, most listed skills and the last employer should appear in the resume text. This catches invented values that are perfectly well formed.
4. **Signals from the call.** A response that stopped because it hit `max_tokens`, or a refusal, is a failure, not an answer.

Then run the small model on a labelled golden set and count the **miss rate**: of the outputs that were wrong, how many passed every check? Format checks catch loud failures. Quiet errors (a plausible but wrong employer) pass them. A self-reported "unsure" or a cheap judge model can add a signal for free text, but measure its miss rate the same way.

### Reasoning effort and thinking tokens

On Claude models, `output_config.effort` takes `low`, `medium`, `high`, `xhigh` or `max`. It shapes all output tokens, including thinking. As of November 2026, the default is `medium` on Claude Haiku 5.5 and Claude Opus 5.5 and `high` on Claude Sonnet 5.5. Anthropic suggests `low` for simple, high-volume work.

Three gotchas:

- **Thinking counts toward `max_tokens`.** A tight `max_tokens` can cut a reply off mid-answer. Leave room, and treat `stop_reason == "max_tokens"` as a failure.
- **Thinking switches differ by model.** On Haiku 5.5 you may send `thinking: {"type": "disabled"}` at `high` effort or below. Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5 reject it with an error, so lower effort instead.
- **Effort is a fourth tier.** Before building a cascade, also test the large model at lower effort. Sometimes that holds quality with one model and one cache.

Read `usage.output_tokens` per route. If output tokens far exceed the visible reply, thinking is where your money goes.

### Batching: how it behaves

These are Anthropic's Message Batches rules as of 28 November 2026; other providers differ in detail.

- **Price:** 50% off input and output tokens. OpenAI and Google also offer discounted batch modes; check their current terms.
- **Speed:** most batches finish within an hour, but any request not processed within 24 hours expires. Never batch work someone is waiting for.
- **Size:** up to 100,000 requests or 256 MB per batch. Results stay available for 29 days.
- **Results:** they can arrive in any order. Match by your own `custom_id`. Each item is `succeeded`, `errored`, `canceled` or `expired`, and you are not billed for the last three.
- **Limits:** batches have their own rate limits, shared across models: requests per minute plus a cap on requests waiting in the queue (300,000 at the Build tier). They are separate from the real-time limits, and batches are not subject to ramp limits. A large overnight job therefore does not use up the per-minute token limits that daytime recruiters rely on.
- **Not allowed in a batch:** streaming, fast mode and server-side fallbacks.

### Caching inside the picture

Prompt caching stacks with tiering and batching. Put the stable part first (instructions, then the job description) and mark it with `cache_control`. Claude Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 cache prefixes of at least 512 tokens; a shorter prefix is processed normally with no error. A 5-minute cache write costs 1.25 times the input price and a 1-hour write 2 times. Reads are far cheaper. In batches, cache hits are best effort because requests run in any order. Use the 1-hour cache and, as Anthropic suggests, send one request first to write the prefix before you submit the rest.

### Prices to plan with

Anthropic list prices in US dollars per million tokens, checked 28 November 2026 (Haiku prices apply to prompts up to 100,000 tokens):

| Model | Input | Output | Batch input | Batch output | Cache read |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Claude Haiku 5.5 | $0.10 | $0.50 | $0.05 | $0.25 | $0.01 |
| Claude Sonnet 5.5 | $2 | $10 | $1 | $5 | $0.10 |
| Claude Opus 5.5 | $4 | $20 | $2 | $10 | $0.20 |

Sonnet 5.5 costs 20 times as much as Haiku 5.5 per token. Re-check prices before every cost review and write the check date next to them.

### A cascade in code

Small model at low effort, layered checks, the large model only when a check fails:

```python
import anthropic
from pydantic import BaseModel, ValidationError

client = anthropic.Anthropic()
SMALL, LARGE = "claude-haiku-5-5", "claude-sonnet-5-5"   # check the models page for current names

PROMPT = ("Return only JSON with name, years_experience, skills and "
          "last_employer (null if absent). Copy names exactly as written.\n\n")

class Resume(BaseModel):
    name: str
    years_experience: float
    skills: list[str]
    last_employer: str | None = None

def extract(model, effort, text):
    r = client.messages.create(
        model=model, max_tokens=2000,              # thinking counts toward max_tokens
        output_config={"effort": effort},
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": PROMPT + text}])
    if r.stop_reason != "end_turn":                # cut off or refused: treat as a failure
        return None, r.usage
    return "".join(b.text for b in r.content if b.type == "text"), r.usage

def valid(raw, text):
    if raw is None:
        return None
    try:
        item = Resume.model_validate_json(raw)
    except ValidationError:
        return None
    page = text.lower()
    words = item.name.lower().split()
    if not words or not all(w in page for w in words):          # name must be in the resume
        return None
    if not 0 <= item.years_experience <= 50 or not item.skills:
        return None
    found = sum(s.lower() in page for s in item.skills)
    if found < 0.8 * len(item.skills):                          # most skills must be in the text
        return None
    if item.last_employer and item.last_employer.lower() not in page:
        return None
    return item

def cascade(text, stats):
    raw, _ = extract(SMALL, "low", text)
    stats["small"] += 1
    if item := valid(raw, text):
        return item
    stats["escalated"] += 1
    raw, _ = extract(LARGE, "high", text)
    return valid(raw, text)                                      # None goes to a person
```

An empty name fails the `if not words` check and escalates instead of crashing. Track the escalation rate daily. If it climbs, a new kind of input has arrived and the savings are shrinking.

### The fit summary, cached and batched

The job description is identical for every resume in a hiring drive, so it goes first and is marked for caching:

```python
import time

def run_batch(items, job_description):
    batch = client.messages.batches.create(requests=[
        {"custom_id": it["id"],                                  # your id: 1-64 letters, digits, - or _
         "params": {"model": LARGE, "max_tokens": 4000,
                    "output_config": {"effort": "medium"},
                    "system": [{"type": "text",
                                "text": "You screen resumes for this role.\n\n" + job_description,
                                "cache_control": {"type": "ephemeral", "ttl": "1h"}}],
                    "messages": [{"role": "user",
                                  "content": "Write a five-line fit summary.\n\n" + it["text"]}]}}
        for it in items])
    while client.messages.batches.retrieve(batch.id).processing_status != "ended":
        time.sleep(60)
    done, retry = {}, []
    for r in client.messages.batches.results(batch.id):         # any order: key by custom_id
        if r.result.type == "succeeded":
            m = r.result.message
            done[r.custom_id] = "".join(b.text for b in m.content if b.type == "text")
        else:                                                    # errored, expired or canceled
            retry.append(r.custom_id)
    return done, retry
```

Send `retry` to a new, smaller batch, or to the real-time path if recruiters need it by morning. Validate batch outputs exactly as you would real-time ones. Check `usage.cache_read_input_tokens` on a sample: if it stays at zero, something in the prefix is changing.

## A worked example

A Pune HR-tech company screens resumes for its clients. Each resume gets structured extraction (name, experience, skills, last employer) and a fit summary against the job description. Volume is 400,000 resumes a month, about 80% uploaded in bulk the evening before a hiring drive.

All figures below are an illustration with assumed token counts, at, for example, ₹90 to the dollar and the 28 November 2026 prices above. Your own numbers come from `usage` logs.

**Before.** Everything runs on Claude Sonnet 5.5 in real time at its default `high` effort. Per resume:

- Extraction: 3,400 input tokens ($0.0068) and 900 output tokens, of which about 650 are thinking ($0.009). Total $0.0158.
- Fit summary: 5,000 input tokens ($0.010) and 1,400 output tokens, of which about 1,050 are thinking ($0.014). Total $0.024.

That is $0.0398 a resume, $15,920 a month, about ₹14.3 lakh. Notice that thinking alone is over 40% of each call.

**Step 1: choose the validator on a golden set.** The team hand-labels 500 resumes and counts resumes with every field correct:

| Path | Escalated | Wrong outputs that passed checks | Resumes fully correct |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sonnet 5.5 alone | Not applicable | Not applicable | 489 (97.8%) |
| Haiku 5.5 alone | Not applicable | Not applicable | 466 (93.2%) |
| Cascade, format and range checks | 15 (3%) | 22 of 34 | 476 (95.2%) |
| Cascade, plus grounding checks | 35 (7%) | 7 of 34 | 489 (97.8%) |

The format-only validator looked attractive at 3% escalation, but it missed 22 of the 34 wrong outputs. The grounding checks more than double the escalation rate and match the large model's quality. The team picks them.

**Step 2: apply the levers one at a time.**

| Lever | What changes | Monthly spend after | Saving |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Start | Sonnet 5.5 for everything, real time | ₹14.33 lakh | None |
| 1. Cascade for extraction | Haiku 5.5 at low effort: $0.0005 a resume, plus 7% × $0.0158 for escalations | ₹9.22 lakh | ₹5.11 lakh |
| 2. Lower effort on fit summaries | Medium effort: thinking falls to about 400 tokens, summary cost $0.0175 | ₹6.88 lakh | ₹2.34 lakh |
| 3. Batch the bulk uploads | 80% of resumes at half price | ₹4.13 lakh | ₹2.75 lakh |
| 4. Cache the job description | 2,000-token prefix, 90% of calls read it from cache (1-hour writes in batches, 5-minute in real time) | ₹3.45 lakh | ₹0.68 lakh |

Read the table the way a reviewer would:

- **Lever 1:** escalations cost more than twice as much as all the small-model calls together. Each point of escalation rate is visible on the bill.
- **Lever 2:** the effort change needed its own eval. Recruiters blind-rated 100 summaries at both settings and saw no difference. Without that check, it would be a guess.
- **Lever 3:** batching multiplies everything before it by 0.6 (80% at half price, 20% at full price).
- **Lever 4:** caching is small here because the prefix is only 2,000 of 5,000 input tokens. The 400-token extraction instructions do not cache at all: they are under the 512-token minimum.

The bill falls from about ₹14.3 lakh to about ₹3.45 lakh a month, roughly 76% lower, with golden-set accuracy and recruiter ratings unchanged. Daytime uploads stay in real time. There, the 7% of resumes that escalate wait for two calls, so the team tracks p95 latency for that path separately.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing the small model by vibe.** Run the evals for each route. Cheap and wrong is expensive.
- **Trusting a low escalation rate.** A validator that checks only format passes well-formed but wrong answers. Measure its miss rate on a golden set.
- **Counting cost per call instead of cost per successful task.** Include escalations, retries and human fixes.
- **Ignoring thinking tokens.** Default effort on a large model can make reasoning the biggest line on the bill. Set effort per route and check it against evals.
- **Setting `max_tokens` too tight.** Thinking counts toward it, so replies get cut off and look like model failures.
- **Batching user-facing work.** Batches usually finish within an hour but can take up to 24.
- **Losing failed batch items.** Match by `custom_id`, retry errored and expired items, and keep writes idempotent so reruns create no duplicates.
- **Forgetting the latency of escalation.** In chat, a cascade makes the hardest questions slowest. A router or a single model may serve users better.
- **Caching a prefix that cannot cache.** It is too short, it changes on every request, or the 5-minute cache expires between daytime uploads.

## When tiering and batching are not worth it

- **Low volume.** The savings are smaller than the engineering time.
- **Every request is urgent**, so batching does not fit.
- **The small model fails your evals** on the traffic you would send it, and a lower effort setting on the large model does not help either.
- **There is no reliable check** for a cascade to escalate on, and no clear signal for a router.

## From demo to production

- **Track escalation rate, validator miss rate and cost per successful task** per feature, every week.
- **Re-run the golden set** on every model, prompt or effort change. Re-sweep effort when you change models, because levels behave differently between models.
- **Handle batch failures** by id, with retries, a report and a real-time fallback before the deadline.
- **Keep urgent and batch work apart.** Batch has its own limits; use workspace limits so a runaway job cannot drain the real-time quota.
- **Record prices with dates** in every cost comparison, and re-check them before each review.
