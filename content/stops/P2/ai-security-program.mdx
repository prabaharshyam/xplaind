## What it is

An **AI security programme** is the set of habits, documents and owners that keep a company's use of AI secure, across every team, not only one product. Single fixes, a guardrail here or a red-team round there, do not add up to security if nobody knows which AI systems exist, who owns them and which ones were never reviewed.

A working programme has seven parts:

1. **AI inventory**, with an AI bill of materials for systems you build.
2. **Shadow AI** handling: finding and bringing unapproved use into approved tools.
3. **Acceptable-use policy** for staff.
4. **Vendor reviews** for AI tools and model providers.
5. **Security reviews** for new AI features, scaled by risk.
6. **Metrics**, so leadership can see whether it works.
7. **Training and owners**: who does what.

Several earlier lessons cover parts of this, and this lesson links them rather than repeating them: "Your company's AI policy" is the employee's view of a policy; "AI risk frameworks" covers the EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001 and India's guidelines and the risk register; "Incident response for AI" covers what to do when things go wrong; "Security questionnaires" covers answering clients' reviews of you. Here you are on the other side: the person who runs the programme.

This lesson follows Neha, the first AI security engineer at a Pune fintech with about 900 staff.

## How it works

```flow
title: A new AI tool or feature, from request to review
Team wants an AI tool or feature -> Intake form | purpose, data, users, vendor, tools
Intake form -> Added to AI inventory
Added to AI inventory -> Risk tier?
Risk tier? -[low: internal, no personal data]-> Checklist review | 1 to 3 days
Risk tier? -[medium: personal data or vendor]-> Vendor review and design review
Risk tier? -[high: customers, tools, money]-> Threat model, red team, sign-off
Checklist review -> Approved with conditions
Vendor review and design review -> Approved with conditions
Threat model, red team, sign-off -> Approved with conditions
Approved with conditions -> Monitored | metrics, yearly re-review
```

**1. The AI inventory.** List every AI system and tool: what the company builds (chatbots, agents, ML models), what it buys (an AI meeting-notes tool, an AI feature inside the CRM) and what staff use (chat assistants, coding assistants). For each, record the owner, purpose, users, data it touches, vendor and model, where data is stored, integrations and tools, risk tier, last review date and status. A spreadsheet is fine at first; what matters is that one team owns it and every entry has a named owner.

For systems you build, add an **AI bill of materials (AI-BOM)**: the models and their versions, datasets, libraries, prompts, retrieval sources and external tools or MCP servers. When a model file or library turns out to be compromised, the AI-BOM tells you in minutes which systems use it. The "AI supply chain" lesson covers AI-BOM formats.

**2. Shadow AI.** Shadow AI is AI use the company does not know about or has not approved: an analyst pasting a customer list into a personal chatbot account, a browser extension that reads every page, a team paying for an AI tool on a credit card. The risks are data leaving the company, possible use of that data for training, and breaking client contracts or the DPDP Act.

Find it with data you already have: web proxy and DNS logs, single sign-on and OAuth app grants ("Sign in with Google" to an AI tool), browser extension inventories, expense claims for AI subscriptions, and plain conversations with teams. Then respond in order:

- **Offer an approved alternative** that does the job well, with enterprise terms (no training on your data, admin controls, logs).
- **Publish simple rules** on what data may go where.
- **Then restrict**: block or warn on unapproved tools, and use data loss prevention rules for obvious patterns like Aadhaar, PAN or card numbers.

Blocking without an alternative does not stop use; it moves it to personal phones, where you see nothing.

**3. Acceptable-use policy.** A short policy for staff: approved tools, data categories and where each may go, review and disclosure rules, how to request a new tool and how to report a mistake. The "Your company's AI policy" lesson shows what a good one contains. Your job is to make it short, findable, enforced by tool settings where possible, and updated when the approved list changes.

**4. Vendor reviews.** Before an AI vendor gets company data, check:

| Area | Question to ask | Good answer looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Training | Is our data used to train or improve models? | No, by contract, for all tiers we use |
| Retention | How long are prompts, files and outputs kept? | A short, stated period, or zero retention, and deletion on request |
| Location | Where is data stored and processed? | Named regions; meets our contracts and DPDP Act needs |
| Sub-processors | Which model providers and other parties see our data? | A published list, with notice of changes |
| Security | Which certifications and audits? | SOC 2 Type II or ISO 27001 report available; ISO/IEC 42001 is a plus |
| Incidents | How fast will they tell us about a breach? | A contractual deadline short enough for our own reporting duties |
| Controls | Admin console, SSO, audit logs, data export? | Yes, all available on our plan |
| AI-specific | How do they test for prompt injection and misuse in their own product? | A described process, not just "our model is safe" |

Reuse the questionnaire you would answer yourself; the "Security questionnaires" lesson shows that side.

**5. Security reviews for new AI features.** Every new AI feature fills a one-page intake form, which also adds it to the inventory. Tier it. Low risk gets a checklist. Medium risk gets a design review and, for vendors, a vendor review. High risk, anything customer-facing with tools, money or sensitive personal data, gets a threat model, a red-team round and attack tests in CI before launch, then a sign-off by named people.

**6. Metrics.** Pick a few that leadership can follow month to month:

| Metric | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| Inventory coverage: AI systems reviewed out of known | Whether the programme reaches everything |
| High-severity AI findings open, and their age | Whether problems get fixed |
| Attack success rate in CI suites, per product | Whether defences hold over time |
| Unapproved AI traffic trend in proxy logs | Whether shadow AI is shrinking |
| Review turnaround time | Whether security is a bottleneck, which drives more shadow AI |
| Staff trained this year | Reach of training |

**7. Training and owners.** Train all staff once a year on the data rules with real examples, and give engineers deeper training on AI threats. Then make ownership explicit:

| Activity | Owner | Involved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AI inventory and AI-BOM standard | AI security | Every system owner keeps their entry current |
| Acceptable-use policy | AI security with legal | HR for training, IT for tool settings |
| Vendor reviews | Security and procurement | Legal for contract terms |
| Feature security reviews | AI security | Product and engineering teams |
| Fixing findings | The system's engineering owner | AI security checks the fix |
| Incident response for AI | Security operations | System owner, legal, communications |
| Metrics report | AI security | Leadership reviews monthly |

## A worked example

A Pune fintech (a made-up example) with about 900 staff hires Neha as its first AI security engineer. In her first month she finds:

- two customer-facing LLM features in production and four planned;
- an AI meeting-notes tool bought by sales on a credit card, recording client calls;
- proxy logs showing about 300 staff a week using five public chatbots, some pasting spreadsheets;
- no AI policy and no list of AI systems.

**Month 1: see everything.** She builds the inventory from proxy logs, SSO grants, expense claims and a short form sent to every team lead. It ends with 23 entries. She tiers them: 5 high, 8 medium, 10 low.

**Month 2: give a better path.** With IT, the company licenses one enterprise chat assistant with no training on company data and single sign-on. A one-page acceptable-use policy goes out with a 20-minute training session using real, anonymised examples from the proxy logs. Two weeks later, unapproved chatbots get a warning page pointing to the approved tool, and data loss prevention rules block uploads containing card numbers or Aadhaar numbers.

**Month 3: review what matters.** The meeting-notes vendor review finds recordings kept indefinitely and used to improve the vendor's models on the plan sales bought. Neha negotiates the business plan with no training and 30-day retention, and the tool stays. The two high-risk production features get threat models; one has its first red-team round, and its findings go into a CI suite.

Her first quarterly report shows: inventory coverage up from 0 to 18 of 23 reviewed, high findings open falling from 6 to 2, unapproved chatbot traffic down by about two thirds, and median review time of four working days. Leadership approves a second hire.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with a long policy instead of an inventory.** You cannot secure what you have not listed.
- **Blocking every AI tool.** Without a good approved option, use moves to personal devices.
- **One review process for everything.** Heavy reviews for low-risk tools create a queue and more shadow AI.
- **Trusting vendor marketing.** Read the terms for your plan; free and paid tiers often differ on training and retention.
- **No owners.** "Security owns AI" means nobody fixes findings in product code.
- **Metrics nobody acts on.** Report a few numbers that lead to decisions.
- **An inventory that goes stale.** Tie it to the intake form and purchasing, so new tools are added automatically.
