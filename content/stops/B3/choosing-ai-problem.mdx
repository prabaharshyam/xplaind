## What it is

Choosing a problem for AI means checking a candidate use case against a few questions before committing a team to it:

- **Is the problem real and valuable?** Frequent, painful, measurable.
- **Is AI the right tool?** Would a rule, a form, a better search or a process change solve it more simply?
- **What happens when AI is wrong?** Can users notice and fix mistakes cheaply? What is the cost of each kind of error?
- **Is there data and context?** Does the AI have access to the information it needs?
- **Can success be measured?** Is there a baseline and a clear metric?

## How it works

**Start from users, not from AI.** Talk to users about the task (see discovery). Find where time, money or satisfaction is lost. Only then ask whether AI helps.

**AI tends to fit when:** inputs are varied and unstructured (free text, documents, images, speech); the task is reading, writing, summarising, classifying or searching; a "good enough" answer that a person checks still saves a lot of time; or personalisation at scale matters.

**AI tends not to fit when:** a simple rule is accurate enough; every answer must be exactly right with no review (calculating tax due, legal filings); the needed information is not available; or users cannot tell when the AI is wrong and errors are costly.

**Errors have two sides.** For a fraud alert, a false alarm annoys a customer; a missed fraud costs money. For a resume screener, a false rejection loses a good candidate unfairly. Decide which error is worse, because it shapes the design: thresholds, human review and messaging.

**Four big risks.** Product teams judge ideas on value (will users want it), usability (can they use it), feasibility (can we build it at acceptable quality and cost) and viability (does it work for the business, including legal and brand risk). AI adds uncertainty to feasibility; test it early with a quick prototype on real data.

**Score and compare.** Put candidates in a simple table: value, frequency, error tolerance, data availability, feasibility, risk. Pick one to two to prototype.

## A worked example

A Pune ed-tech company's leadership asks its PM, Nikhil, for "AI everywhere". He lists five candidates and scores them:

1. **Doubt-solving chat for students**: high value and frequency; errors visible to students and risky for learning; data available (course content). Feasible with grounding and teacher review of reported answers. **Prototype.**
2. **AI grading of descriptive answers**: valuable for teachers; errors directly affect marks and fairness; parents will dispute. **Later, with teachers reviewing every grade.**
3. **Auto-generated weekly progress emails to parents**: frequent, low risk, data in the system; a template with a few AI-written lines works. **Quick win.**
4. **Predicting which students will drop out**: valuable, but data on reasons is weak and errors could stigmatise students. **Not now.**
5. **AI to set fees by demand**: legal and trust risk; a pricing analyst with a spreadsheet does fine. **No.**

Leadership agrees to two prototypes and one quick win, with reasons documented.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting from the technology.** Start from user pain.
- **Ignoring simpler solutions.** Rules and forms are cheaper and more reliable.
- **Not asking what happens when it is wrong.** Error cost decides the design.
- **Assuming the data exists.** Check access and quality early.
- **Saying yes to everything.** A clear "no, because" is valuable.

## Practise

Read the PAIR Guidebook chapter on user needs and SVPG's "Four Big Risks".

1. List five AI ideas for a product or team you know.
2. Score each on value, frequency, error tolerance, data, feasibility and risk, and note which error type is worse.
3. Write a half-page recommendation: which to prototype, which to reject and why.

**You are done when you can** reject an attractive AI idea with clear reasons and pick one where AI clearly helps.
