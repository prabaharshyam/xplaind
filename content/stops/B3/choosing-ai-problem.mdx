## What it is

A common reason AI projects fail is not a bad model. It is choosing the wrong problem. Teams build impressive demos for problems that were rare, that a simple rule would have solved, or where a single mistake was unacceptable.

**Choosing a problem for AI** means checking a candidate use case against a few questions before you commit a team to it:

| Question | What you are checking |
| --- | --- |
| **Is the problem real and valuable?** | It is frequent, painful and measurable |
| **Is AI the right tool?** | A rule, a form, a better search or a process change would not solve it more simply |
| **What happens when AI is wrong?** | Users can notice and fix mistakes cheaply; you know the cost of each kind of error |
| **Is there data and context?** | The AI can access the information it needs |
| **Can success be measured?** | There is a baseline today and a clear metric |

```text
              Good AI problems sit where all three overlap
          +--------------------+
          |  Valuable and      |
          |  frequent          |
          |        +-----------+--------+
          |        |   GOOD    |        |
          +--------+-----------+  AI is |
                   |  AI fits  |  the   |
         Mistakes  |  here     |  right |
         are cheap +-----------+  tool  |
         or easy   |           +--------+
         to check  +-----------+
```

This lesson follows Nikhil, a product manager at an ed-tech company in Pune, whose leadership asks for "AI everywhere". By the end you will be able to reject an attractive AI idea with clear reasons, and pick one where AI clearly helps.

## How it works

### Start from users, not from AI

Talk to users about their work. Find where time, money or satisfaction is lost: the task that takes an hour every day, the question support answers 200 times a week, the report nobody reads because it arrives too late. Only then ask whether AI helps.

### When AI fits and when it does not

| AI tends to fit when | AI tends not to fit when |
| --- | --- |
| Inputs are varied and unstructured: free text, documents, images, speech | A simple rule is accurate enough |
| The task is reading, writing, summarising, classifying or searching | Every answer must be exactly right with no review, like calculating tax due or filing a legal document |
| A "good enough" answer that a person checks still saves a lot of time | The needed information is not available to the system |
| Personalisation at scale matters | Users cannot tell when the AI is wrong, and errors are costly |

### Errors have two sides

Every AI decision can go wrong in two ways, and they rarely cost the same:

| Use case | False alarm costs | Miss costs | Worse error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fraud alert on a payment | An annoyed customer, an OTP | Lost money | Usually the miss |
| Resume screener | Time spent on a weak candidate | A good candidate rejected unfairly | The false rejection |
| Spam filter | A real email in spam | Spam in the inbox | Often the false alarm |
| Medical triage | An unneeded test | A missed serious illness | The miss, by far |

Deciding which error is worse shapes the design: where to set thresholds, when a human reviews, and what the product tells the user.

### The four big risks

Product teams judge every idea on four risks:

1. **Value:** will users want it and choose it?
2. **Usability:** can they figure out how to use it?
3. **Feasibility:** can we build it at acceptable quality, speed and cost?
4. **Viability:** does it work for the business, including legal, brand and cost risk?

AI adds extra uncertainty to **feasibility**: you often cannot know quality until you try. Test it early with a quick prototype on real data.

### Score and compare

Put candidates side by side and score each from 1 (poor) to 5 (strong):

```text
               value  freq  error-tol  data  feasible  low-risk   total
 Idea A          5     5       3        4       4        3         24
 Idea B          4     2       2        2       3        2         15
 Idea C          3     5       5        5       5        5         28  <- quick win
```

Pick one or two to prototype, and write down why you rejected the others.

## A worked example

Nikhil is a product manager at an ed-tech company in Pune that runs online coaching for classes 9 to 12. Leadership asks him for "AI everywhere" by next quarter. He lists five candidate ideas and scores them:

| Idea | Value | Frequency | If wrong | Data | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Doubt-solving chat for students | High | Daily | Visible to students, risky for learning | Course content available | **Prototype**, grounded in course content, with teacher review of reported answers |
| 2. AI grading of descriptive answers | High for teachers | Weekly | Directly affects marks and fairness; parents will dispute | Past graded answers exist | **Later**, with teachers reviewing every grade |
| 3. Auto-written weekly progress emails to parents | Medium | Weekly | Low: easy to spot, little harm | All in the system | **Quick win**: a template plus a few AI-written lines |
| 4. Predicting which students will drop out | High | Monthly | Could stigmatise students | Data on reasons is weak | **Not now** |
| 5. AI to set fees by demand | Unclear | Rare | Legal and trust risk | Some | **No**: a pricing analyst with a spreadsheet does fine |

He presents this one-page table instead of a list of features. Leadership agrees to two prototypes and one quick win, with the reasons for each "no" documented.

Three months later, the progress emails have cut parent calls to teachers by about a third, and the doubt-solving prototype is in a pilot with two batches. The ideas he turned down did not quietly eat the team's time.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting from the technology.** Start from user pain.
- **Ignoring simpler solutions.** Rules, forms and better search are cheaper and more reliable when they are enough.
- **Not asking what happens when it is wrong.** The cost of each error decides the design.
- **Assuming the data exists.** Check access and quality early.
- **No baseline.** If you do not measure the task today, you cannot prove AI helped.
- **Saying yes to everything.** A clear "no, because" is one of a product manager's most valuable outputs.

## Practise

Read the PAIR Guidebook chapter on user needs, linked below, and SVPG's "Four Big Risks".

1. **List.** Write down five AI ideas for a product or team you know.
2. **Score.** Score each on value, frequency, error tolerance, data, feasibility and risk, and note which type of error is worse.
3. **Recommend.** Write a half-page recommendation: which to prototype, which to reject, and why.

**You are done when you can** reject an attractive AI idea with clear reasons and pick one where AI clearly helps.
