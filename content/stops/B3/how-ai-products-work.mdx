## What it is

From the outside, an AI product looks like a chat box that answers questions. Inside, it is a small system of parts, and when it goes wrong, the fix depends on **which part** failed. Product managers who can draw the parts can have useful conversations with engineers. Those who cannot end up asking for "a better model" every time.

Most AI products built on language models share the same parts. Follow one request through them:

```text
  User
   |
   v
 [1 Interface] chat, in-app button, email, WhatsApp, API
   |
   v
 [2 Context assembly] system prompt + user input + history
   |                         ^
   |                         |
   |                  [3 Retrieval] search company documents and data
   v
 [6 Guardrails: input] topic limits, personal data, injection checks
   |
   v
 [4 Model] writes the answer, or asks to use a tool
   |      \
   |       --> [5 Tools] look up an order, create a ticket --> result back
   v
 [6 Guardrails: output] policy rules, format, no promises it can't keep
   |
   v
 [7 Human checkpoint] review or approve, where mistakes are costly
   |
   v
  Answer to user   ------->  [8 Evaluation and monitoring] quality, cost, feedback
```

| Part | What it does | If it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Interface** | Where the user meets the product | Confusing or hidden feature |
| **Context assembly** | Puts together instructions, input and history | Model misunderstands the task |
| **Retrieval (RAG)** | Fetches relevant facts from company data | Wrong or outdated answers |
| **Model** | Produces the answer | Weak reasoning on hard tasks |
| **Tools** | Actions the model can request | Wrong action, or none |
| **Guardrails** | Checks on input and output | Unsafe or off-policy replies |
| **Human checkpoints** | Review or approval for costly cases | Costly mistakes reach customers |
| **Evaluation and monitoring** | Test sets before release; tracking after | Problems found by customers, not you |

This lesson follows Ritu, a product manager at a bank in Mumbai, as she traces why the bank's assistant promised a customer a fee waiver that never happened. By the end you will be able to explain, using the picture, why an answer was wrong and what should change.

## How it works

### Where quality comes from

Wrong answers usually come from one of four places:

1. **Missing or outdated information**: retrieval found the wrong document, or the right one does not exist.
2. **Unclear instructions**: the system prompt does not say what to do in this situation.
3. **A model too weak for the task**: possible, but less common than people think.
4. **No check on the output**: nothing caught the mistake before the user saw it.

When something goes wrong, ask which part failed before asking for a bigger model.

### Where cost comes from

Each request costs:

- **model tokens**, for both the input (prompt, history, retrieved text) and the output;
- retrieval, tools and infrastructure.

Long prompts and long answers cost more. Bigger models cost more per token. Two common savings: **caching** repeated parts of prompts, and **routing** easy requests to smaller, cheaper models.

### Where risk comes from

Confident wrong answers, actions taken without checks, personal data exposed, **prompt injection** (instructions hidden inside documents or emails the model reads), and unfair outcomes. The controls are guardrails, permissions on tools, human review and evaluation.

### Prompting, retrieval or fine-tuning?

| Approach | What it changes | Speed and cost to change | Use when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Prompting** | Instructions and examples | Fast, cheap | Behaviour, tone, format |
| **Retrieval (RAG)** | Facts the model sees per request | Medium | Current or private information |
| **Fine-tuning** | The model's habits through training | Slow, costlier | A narrow style or skill at scale |

Most products start with prompting and retrieval, and many never need fine-tuning.

### Agents

When the model decides a whole sequence of tool calls itself, the product is an **agent**. Agents are flexible but harder to predict, so they need limits on what they can do, logs of every step and approvals for risky actions. Use fixed workflows where they work, and agents where the flexibility pays.

### The product manager's levers

Engineers decide **how**. You decide **what and how good**:

- the user problem and the success metric;
- which actions need approval;
- which failures are acceptable and which are not;
- the budget per request;
- what the evaluation set must cover.

## A worked example

Ritu is the product manager for a feature in a Mumbai bank's mobile app that answers customers' questions about credit card charges.

**The complaint.** The assistant told a customer that their annual fee of ₹2,500 would be waived. It was not, and the customer was charged.

Ritu and the engineers trace the request through the parts:

| Part | What they found | Problem? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interface | In-app chat, working normally | No |
| Context assembly | System prompt said to answer from bank documents only | No |
| Retrieval | Returned last year's fee-waiver offer, which had expired | **Yes: the cause** |
| Model | Answered faithfully from the document it was given | No |
| Guardrails | No rule stopped promises about fees | **Yes: a missing control** |
| Evaluation | No test questions involved expired offers | **Yes: a gap** |

**Fixes, by part:**

- **Retrieval:** filter documents by their valid-from and valid-until dates, so expired offers are never returned.
- **Guardrails:** block the assistant from promising waivers or refunds; it says instead, "You may be eligible. Here is how to check," with a link.
- **Evaluation:** add 30 fee questions, including expired and future offers, to the test set.
- **Monitoring:** a new metric, complaints about wrong fee information per 10,000 chats, reviewed weekly.

Nobody needed a different model. The answer was wrong because of what the model was given and what nobody checked.

## Common mistakes

- **Blaming the model for every error.** Many come from data, retrieval or missing checks.
- **Treating the prompt as secret sauce.** It is one part; evaluation and data matter more.
- **Ignoring cost per request.** A feature users love can lose money at scale.
- **No evaluation set.** Without one, every change is a guess.
- **Agents by default.** Use fixed workflows where they work.
- **No owner for the documents.** Retrieval is only as good as the content behind it.

## Practise

Read Chip Huyen's "Building a Generative AI Platform", linked below, and skim Eugene Yan's patterns for building LLM-based systems.

1. **Draw it.** Pick an AI feature you use, such as a banking chatbot, a shopping assistant or an email writer, and draw its likely parts.
2. **Trace failures.** For three possible failures of that feature, name the part that probably caused each and one fix.
3. **Your decisions.** List the decisions a product manager should make for it: metric, approval points, acceptable errors and budget per request.

**You are done when you can** explain to an engineer, using the picture, why an answer was wrong and what you want changed.
