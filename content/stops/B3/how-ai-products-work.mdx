## What it is

Most AI products built on language models share the same parts. Think of a request flowing through them:

1. **Interface**: chat, a button in an existing app, an email, a WhatsApp message, an API.
2. **Context assembly**: the system prompt (instructions), the user's input, conversation history, and information fetched for this request.
3. **Retrieval**: searching the company's documents or data for relevant facts (RAG).
4. **Model**: the language model that produces the answer, often chosen per task.
5. **Tools**: actions the model can request, such as looking up an order or creating a ticket.
6. **Guardrails**: checks on input and output: topic limits, personal data, policy rules, format.
7. **Human checkpoints**: review or approval where mistakes are costly.
8. **Evaluation and monitoring**: test sets before release, and quality, cost and feedback tracking after.

## How it works

**Where quality comes from.** Wrong answers usually come from one of: missing or outdated information (retrieval and data), unclear instructions (prompt), a model too weak for the task, or no check on the output. When something goes wrong, ask which part failed before asking for "a better model".

**Where cost comes from.** Each request costs model tokens (input and output), plus retrieval, tools and infrastructure. Long prompts and long answers cost more. Bigger models cost more per token. Caching and routing easy requests to small models cut cost.

**Where risk comes from.** Wrong answers stated confidently, actions taken without checks, personal data exposed, prompt injection through documents, and unfair outcomes. Guardrails, permissions, human review and evaluation are the controls.

**Prompting, RAG or fine-tuning.** Instructions and examples change behaviour quickly and cheaply. Retrieval gives the model current, private facts. Fine-tuning teaches a style or narrow skill but is slower and costlier to change. Most products start with prompting and retrieval.

**Agents.** When the model decides a sequence of tool calls itself, the product is an agent. Agents are more flexible and harder to predict, so they need limits, logs and approvals.

**The PM's levers.** You decide the user problem, the success metric, which actions need approval, which failures are acceptable, the budget per request, and what the evaluation set must cover. Engineers decide how; you decide what and how good.

## A worked example

A Mumbai bank's product manager, Ritu, owns a feature that answers customers' questions about credit card charges in the mobile app.

Complaint: the assistant told a customer an annual fee would be waived, but it was not.

Using the picture, Ritu and the engineers trace the request:

- Interface: in-app chat. Fine.
- Retrieval: returned last year's fee-waiver offer document, which had expired. **Cause found.**
- Model: answered faithfully from the wrong document.
- Guardrails: no rule stopped promises about fees.

Fixes, by part: retrieval filters by offer dates; a guardrail blocks the assistant from promising waivers, replacing it with "you may be eligible; here is how to check"; the evaluation set gains 30 fee questions including expired offers. Ritu also adds a metric: complaints about wrong fee information per 10,000 chats.

## Common mistakes

- **Blaming the model for every error.** Most come from data, retrieval or missing checks.
- **Treating the prompt as a secret sauce.** It is one part; evaluation and data matter more.
- **Ignoring cost per request.** A feature loved by users can lose money at scale.
- **No evaluation set.** Without one, every change is a guess.
- **Agents by default.** Use fixed workflows where they work; agents where flexibility pays.

## Practise

Read Chip Huyen's "Building a Generative AI Platform" and skim Eugene Yan's LLM patterns.

1. Pick an AI feature you use (a banking chatbot, a shopping assistant, an email writer) and draw its likely parts.
2. For three possible failures of that feature, name which part probably caused each and one fix.
3. List the decisions a PM should make for it: metric, approval points, acceptable errors, budget per request.

**You are done when you can** explain to an engineer, using the picture, why an answer was wrong and what you want changed.
