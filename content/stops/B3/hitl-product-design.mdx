## What it is

"Keep a human in the loop" is the most common advice in AI product design, and the most often done badly. A person who must click "approve" on 500 AI decisions an hour, with no information, is not oversight. It is a rubber stamp with a name on it.

**Human in the loop** means people are part of how an AI feature works, not just users of its output. There are four common roles:

| Role | What the person does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Review** | Checks AI output before it is used | A support agent reads an AI-drafted reply before sending |
| **Approve** | Authorises an action the AI proposes | A manager approves an AI-suggested refund |
| **Handle exceptions** | Takes the hard or uncertain cases | The AI handles routine claims; adjusters take unusual ones |
| **Audit** | Checks a sample after the fact | A quality lead reviews 5% of automated decisions weekly |

The real product question is not "should there be a human?" but **where, for which cases, with what information, and how fast**.

This lesson follows Arjun, a product manager at a lending app in Bengaluru, as he designs where credit officers sit in an AI-assisted loan review. By the end you will design human roles that are fast and meaningful, and plan how they change as evidence builds.

## How it works

### Decide by risk and confidence

Map your case types on two questions: how costly is a mistake, and how likely is the AI to be right?

```text
                     AI confidence
                low                    high
           +--------------------+--------------------+
   high    |  APPROVE by expert |  APPROVE or REVIEW |
  mistake  |  (or don't use AI) |  every case        |
   cost    +--------------------+--------------------+
   low     |  REVIEW, or route  |  AUTOMATE,         |
           |  as an EXCEPTION   |  AUDIT a sample    |
           +--------------------+--------------------+
```

Unusual cases, the ones unlike anything in your evaluation set, go to experts regardless.

### Design the review moment

A good review screen makes checking quick and real:

1. **The output next to its sources**, so the reviewer does not have to open other systems.
2. **Highlight what needs checking**: amounts, names, dates and any fields the AI was unsure about.
3. **One action each** for approve, edit and reject.

If reviewers must hunt through other systems to decide, they stop checking within a week.

### Watch for automation bias

People tend to accept machine suggestions, especially when busy or tired. This is called **automation bias**. Counter it:

- Show uncertainty clearly, instead of presenting every answer with the same confidence.
- For key fields, require the reviewer to confirm actively, for example tapping the income figure, rather than one "approve all" button.
- Measure **override rates**. An override rate near zero can mean the AI is perfect, or that nobody is really checking.
- Occasionally insert known test cases with a deliberate error, to check attention.

### Plan capacity

Reviews take time. Estimate it:

```text
  daily volume needing review  x  minutes per review  =  minutes a day
        600 applications        x        3 min        =  1,800 min = 30 hours
```

Make sure the team has those hours. Otherwise queues build, people rush, and review quality collapses quietly.

### Clarify accountability

Who is responsible when an approved AI output turns out to be wrong? Usually the approver, which is only fair if they had the information and time they needed. Write it down.

### Use the loop to improve

Every edit and rejection is the best data you have about where the AI fails. Feed them into the evaluation set and into fixes. A loop that does not improve the system is just extra work.

### Evolve oversight with evidence

| Stage | Low-risk cases | High-risk cases |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Launch | Approve or review every one | Approve every one |
| After quality holds for, say, three months | Move to audit of a sample | Still approve every one |
| Ongoing | Audit, with alerts if quality drops | Approve; never fully automate |

Start strict. Relax only with measured evidence, and document each change.

### When the user is the human

In consumer products, the user often is the reviewer: an email draft they edit, a suggestion they accept. Design so that accepting is never the only easy option, and editing is just as quick.

## A worked example

A lending app in Bengaluru uses AI to pre-fill loan application reviews: it extracts income from bank statements, flags risks, and suggests approve, reject or refer. Arjun, the product manager, designs the human role.

**Decisions stay with people.** Credit officers approve or reject every application; the AI only suggests. The lender's credit policy keeps a named person accountable for every lending decision, for fairness and for its regulator.

**The review screen** shows:

- the suggested decision and the three main reasons;
- the extracted monthly income, with the bank statement lines it came from highlighted;
- risk flags in red, such as a recent bounced EMI.

Officers must tap to confirm the income figure before the decision buttons unlock. That one small step stops "approve without looking".

**Overrides are tracked** by officer and reason. In the first month, officers override 14% of AI suggestions. The most common reason, "salary credited in two parts", shows the extraction step was missing split salary credits. Engineers fix it.

**Capacity:** 600 applications a day at 3 minutes each is 30 officer-hours, within the team's capacity of 40.

**Audit:** a senior credit manager reviews 5% of approved files every week.

| Measure | Before AI | Three months after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Review time per application | 9 minutes | 3 minutes |
| Override rate | Not applicable | 14% in month 1, 6% in month 3 |
| Default rate on approvals | Baseline | Same as the manual process |
| Who decides | Officer | Officer |

Review got three times faster, quality held, and decisions stayed with the people accountable for them.

## Common mistakes

- **A human in name only.** Without information and time, review is a rubber stamp.
- **Reviewing everything forever.** Move low-risk cases to audit as evidence builds.
- **Ignoring capacity.** Review queues grow and quality drops.
- **Unclear accountability.** Write down who answers for approved outputs.
- **Wasting overrides.** They are the best signal for improvement.
- **Treating a zero override rate as good news.** It may mean nobody is checking.

## Practise

Read the PAIR Guidebook chapters on feedback and control, linked below, and the Human-AI Interaction guidelines.

1. **Map cases.** For an AI feature of your choice, map case types by mistake cost and AI confidence, and assign each to automate, review, approve, exception or audit.
2. **Sketch the screen.** Draw the review screen with sources, highlighted fields and one-click actions.
3. **Plan capacity and change.** Estimate review capacity, and write the rule for when oversight can be relaxed.

**You are done when you can** show where people sit in your feature, what they see, how long it takes and how it will change with evidence.
