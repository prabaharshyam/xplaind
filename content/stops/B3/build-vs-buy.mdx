## What it is

Every AI product idea hits two questions early, and getting them wrong is expensive:

1. **Feasibility.** Can AI do this task well enough, on our real data, at an acceptable cost and speed?
2. **Build versus buy.** Should we buy a finished product, configure an AI feature in a platform we already use, or build our own?

Teams often skip the first and argue about the second for months. The better order is: test feasibility in days, then choose how to deliver.

```text
   Idea
    |
    v
   Prototype on 20-50 real examples (days, not months)
    |
    +-- far off --> not feasible yet: needs more context, data, or wait
    |
    +-- close   --> feasible: now decide HOW to deliver
                        |
          +-------------+--------------+
          v             v              v
         Buy        Configure         Build
       a product    a platform     your own system
```

This lesson follows Sana, a product manager at a hospital chain in Hyderabad, as she tests whether AI can draft discharge summaries, then decides how to deliver it. By the end you will run a quick feasibility test and defend a build, configure or buy decision.

## How it works

### Prototype in days

Take 20 to 50 **real** examples, masked if they contain personal data. Try the task with a capable model and a simple prompt, by hand or in a notebook. This is a **proof of concept**: small, fast and honest.

Then have a **domain expert** judge the outputs, not the engineers who built the prompt. Rate each output on a simple scale:

| Rating | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Usable | Correct, needs at most small edits |
| Needs work | Right idea, significant edits |
| Unsafe or wrong | Would cause harm if used |

- If results are far off, the task may not be feasible yet, or needs more context or data.
- If results are close, feasibility is likely, and the real work is reliability, integration, safety and evaluation.

### Estimate cost and speed

From the prototype, measure tokens per request and how long each answer takes. Multiply by expected volume. Check that it fits the budget and the user's patience: a doctor will wait ten seconds for a draft, not two minutes.

### The three options

| Option | What it is | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Buy a product** | A ready tool for the use case, such as a meeting-notes tool or a document-processing service | Fastest; vendor handles updates | Limited customisation; vendor's roadmap; your data goes to the vendor |
| **Configure a platform** | An AI feature inside software you already use, such as your CRM, help desk or office suite | Fast; uses existing data and permissions | Limited flexibility; tied to that platform |
| **Build** | Your own system on model APIs or open models | Most control and differentiation | Needs engineers, evaluation and ongoing maintenance |

### Decision factors

1. **Differentiation.** Is this core to how you compete? Build what makes you different; buy commodity functions.
2. **Data and compliance.** Where may the data go? Does the vendor meet your security, privacy and sector rules?
3. **Fit.** Does it handle your languages, document formats and workflows?
4. **Total cost of ownership.** Licences or usage, integration, and the people to run it, over two to three years.
5. **Speed.** How soon do you need value?
6. **Lock-in.** How hard would it be to switch later?

### Hybrid is normal

Most organisations end up mixing: buy for commodity tasks, build where they differentiate, and keep options open with standard interfaces so they can switch models or vendors later.

## A worked example

A hospital chain in Hyderabad wants AI to draft discharge summaries from doctors' notes and lab reports. Doctors spend 20 to 30 minutes on each summary, at the end of long shifts.

**Feasibility.** Sana, the product manager, collects 40 de-identified patient records. Using a capable model and the hospital's summary template, she generates 40 drafts. Three senior doctors rate them:

| Rating | Drafts | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Usable with small edits | 31 | Mostly wording changes |
| Needs significant edits | 7 | Missed context from earlier notes |
| Unsafe | 2 | Both missed a medication change |

Verdict: feasible, but only with mandatory doctor review and an automatic medication cross-check against the pharmacy record.

**The options:**

| Option | What Sana found | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buy product A | Does not integrate with their hospital information system | Ruled out on fit |
| Buy product B | Processes data outside India; the hospital's legal team rejects it | Ruled out on compliance |
| Configure the hospital system vendor's add-on | Cannot be customised to their template or medication checks | Ruled out on fit and safety |
| Build | A small team builds on a model available in the Indian region of their existing cloud, integrated with the hospital system, with the medication cross-check and doctor sign-off | Recommended |

**Cost.** She estimates three months to build, and a two-year total cost lower than the add-on's licences at their volume of about 6,000 discharges a month. The quality of discharge summaries also matters to how the hospital competes on patient care, so building gives real differentiation.

Leadership approves the build, with a review of the market in a year in case a product that meets their rules appears.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping the prototype.** Feasibility guesses are often wrong in both directions.
- **Letting the builders judge their own outputs.** Ask domain experts.
- **Building commodity features.** Buy what does not differentiate you.
- **Buying without checking data handling.** Compliance can rule out otherwise good products.
- **Comparing a licence price with nothing.** Building has people and maintenance costs too.
- **Forgetting lock-in.** Know how you would leave before you sign.

## Practise

Reread SVPG's four big risks, linked below, and Hamel Husain's field guide.

1. **Prototype.** Try an AI task on 20 real examples and have someone who knows the domain rate the outputs.
2. **Compare.** Find two products and one platform feature that claim to solve the same task. Compare them on fit, data handling, cost and lock-in.
3. **Recommend.** Write a one-page build, configure or buy recommendation with a two-year cost estimate.

**You are done when you can** show prototype results rated by an expert and defend your build-versus-buy choice on four factors.
