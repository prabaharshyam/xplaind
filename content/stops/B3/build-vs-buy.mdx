## What it is

Every AI product idea hits two questions early, and getting them wrong is expensive:

1. **Feasibility.** Can AI do this task well enough, on our real data, at an acceptable cost and speed?
2. **Build versus buy.** Should we buy a finished product, configure an AI feature in a platform we already use, or build our own?

Teams often skip the first question and argue about the second for months. The better order is: test feasibility in days, screen out options that cannot work, then compare the ones left on cost and risk.

```flow
title: From idea to a delivery decision
Idea -> Prototype on 20-50 real examples | days, not months
Prototype on 20-50 real examples -[far off]-> Not feasible yet | add context or data, or wait
Prototype on 20-50 real examples -[close]-> Screen the options
Screen the options -[fails fit or compliance]-> Ruled out
Screen the options -[passes]-> Compare two-year cost
Compare two-year cost -> Choose and set a review date
Choose and set a review date -> Large evaluation before go-live
```

This lesson follows Sana, a product manager at a hospital chain in Hyderabad. She tests whether AI can draft discharge summaries, then decides how to deliver it, with numbers. By the end you will be able to run a quick feasibility test, cost both sides of the decision, and defend a buy, configure or build choice.

## How it works

### Step 1: prototype in days

Take 20 to 50 **real** examples, masked if they contain personal data. Try the task with a capable model and a simple prompt, by hand or in a notebook. This is a **proof of concept**: small, fast and honest. Use the hard cases too, such as poor scans, mixed languages and long records, not only the clean ones.

Then have a **domain expert** judge the outputs, not the engineers who wrote the prompt. People who built something see what they meant, not what is on the page. Rate each output on a simple scale:

| Rating | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Usable | Correct, needs at most small edits |
| Needs work | Right idea, significant edits |
| Unsafe or wrong | Would cause harm if used |

- If results are far off, the task may not be feasible yet. It may need more context, better data, or a better model later.
- If results are close, feasibility is likely. The real work is now reliability, integration, safety and evaluation, not whether the model can do it at all.

Keep these 20 to 50 examples and their ratings. They become your first evaluation set, and you will run every vendor and every later model through the same set.

### What a small sample can and cannot tell you

A prototype answers "should we keep going?", not "is it safe to launch?". Small samples hide rare failures.

Suppose 2 of 40 outputs are unsafe. That looks like 5%. But with only 40 examples, the true unsafe rate could be anywhere from under 1% to about 17% (an exact 95% confidence interval). Even 0 unsafe out of 300 only tells you the true rate is probably under about 1.2%.

So the rule is: decide feasibility on a small sample, but decide go-live on a much larger evaluation, with the most serious failure type counted on its own.

### Step 2: estimate cost and speed

From the prototype, note how many tokens each request uses and how long each answer takes. Then multiply by volume. For example, with made-up prices for illustration:

- Suppose one request uses 12,000 input tokens and 1,000 output tokens.
- Suppose the model costs ₹250 per million input tokens and ₹1,000 per million output tokens.
- Then one request costs 12,000 × ₹250 ÷ 1,000,000 + 1,000 × ₹1,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = ₹3 + ₹1 = ₹4.

Real prices change often. Take them from the provider's pricing page and write the date you checked next to them. Add a margin, for example 25%, for retries and evaluation runs.

Check speed against the user's patience too. A doctor will wait ten seconds for a draft, not two minutes.

### Step 3: know the three options

| Option | What it is | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Buy a product** | A ready tool for the use case, such as a document-processing service | Fastest; vendor handles updates and model changes | Limited customisation; the vendor's roadmap; your data goes to the vendor |
| **Configure a platform** | An AI feature inside software you already use, such as your CRM, help desk or hospital system | Fast; uses existing data and permissions | Limited flexibility; tied to that platform |
| **Build** | Your own system on a model API or an open-weight model | Most control over fit, safety checks and data | Needs engineers, evaluation and ongoing maintenance |

"Build" hides a second decision that often settles the case in regulated Indian sectors:

| Build route | Main cost drivers | Skills needed | Data control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hosted model API, ideally in an Indian cloud region | Pay per token; little set-up | Prompting, evaluation, integration | Data goes to the cloud provider under your contract; check region and retention |
| Self-host an open-weight model | GPUs you pay for even when idle, and people to run them | Model serving, scaling, security patching | Data stays in your own account or data centre, but every failure is yours |

A sensible default: start on a hosted API. Self-host only when your rules forbid data leaving your systems, or when volume is high and steady enough that the GPUs are rarely idle. Availability of each model differs by cloud region, so check the region before you design around a model.

### Step 4: screen, then compare

Some factors are pass or fail. Check them first, because no price makes a failing option acceptable:

1. **Fit.** Does it handle your languages, document formats, templates and workflows? Test it on your own evaluation set, not the vendor's demo data.
2. **Data and compliance.** Where may the data go? Does it meet your security, privacy and sector rules?

Then weigh the rest:

3. **Total cost of ownership** over two to three years (below).
4. **Speed.** How soon do you need value? Months of delay have a cost, for example doctor hours not saved.
5. **Differentiation.** Would customers choose you over a competitor because you do this better? If every firm in your sector needs it in the same way, it is a commodity. Saying "it matters to us" is not the test: almost everything matters.
6. **Lock-in.** How hard would it be to leave later?

### The vendor due-diligence checklist

Before you sign with any vendor, get written answers to these seven questions:

1. **Location.** Where is data stored and processed, including backups and support access?
2. **Training.** Do the vendor or its model provider train on your data? Get "no" in the contract.
3. **Retention.** How long is data kept, and will they confirm deletion?
4. **Sub-processors.** Who else touches the data, for example the model provider behind the product?
5. **Security evidence.** Certifications such as ISO 27001 or a SOC 2 report, and a recent penetration test.
6. **Breach notice.** How fast will they tell you? You may have your own duties under the DPDP Act and CERT-In's rules.
7. **Exit.** Can you export your data and outputs in a usable format? How much notice will you get before price or model changes?

### Total cost of ownership

Count both sides fully. Buying is never just the licence fee: there is integration, an internal owner, quality checks and the cost of leaving. Building is never just tokens: there are engineers, evaluation, hosting and maintenance. Costs that are the same in every option, such as the expert time to review each output, cancel out, so you can leave them out of the comparison.

Two gotchas catch experienced teams:

- **People cost more than tokens.** Model usage is often a small part of a build's total. Check your own split before you assume it.
- **Sunk costs change the question.** Once you have built, the money spent is gone. At a later review, compare only future costs: running your build against switching to a vendor.

Also check tax with your finance team. GST on IT and cloud services is 18% (as of November 2026). Most healthcare services are exempt from GST, so a hospital often cannot claim that tax back, which raises the real cost of every vendor and cloud bill.

### Plan for change

Most organisations end up mixing: buy for commodity tasks, build where nothing fits or where they differentiate. Whatever you choose, models and vendors will change under you.

Model providers retire older models. For example, as of November 2026, Anthropic promises at least 60 days' notice before it retires a publicly released model, and after that, requests to it fail. Partner clouds, such as Amazon Bedrock, set their own retirement schedules. So budget for at least one model migration, and keep your evaluation set ready to test the replacement. When you buy, ask the vendor how they handle the same problem.

## A worked example

A hospital chain in Hyderabad wants AI to draft discharge summaries from doctors' notes and lab reports. Doctors spend 20 to 30 minutes on each summary, at the end of long shifts. The chain handles about 6,000 discharges a month. All numbers in this example are illustrative, made up for teaching.

### Feasibility

Sana collects 40 de-identified patient records. Using a capable model and the hospital's summary template, she generates 40 drafts. Three senior doctors rate them:

| Rating | Drafts | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Usable with small edits | 31 | Mostly wording changes |
| Needs significant edits | 7 | Missed context from earlier notes |
| Unsafe | 2 | Both missed a medication change |

Verdict: feasible enough to continue, but only with mandatory doctor sign-off and an automatic check of every medication in the draft against the pharmacy record. The 2 unsafe drafts out of 40 could mean a true rate of anywhere from under 1% to about 17%, so Sana plans a pre-launch evaluation of 400 records, with medication errors counted separately.

### Screening the options

She runs the same 40 records through each option and checks each against the hospital's data policy:

| Option | What Sana found | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buy product A | Good drafts, but does not integrate with their hospital information system | Ruled out on fit |
| Buy product B | Processes data outside India; the hospital's legal team rejects it under the hospital's data policy | Ruled out on compliance |
| Configure the hospital system's add-on | Cannot follow their template or run the medication check | Ruled out on fit and safety |
| Build on a hosted model API in the Indian region of their existing cloud | Integrates with the hospital system, with the medication check and doctor sign-off | The only option left |

Be honest about why build won. Drafting discharge summaries is not what makes this hospital different: every hospital needs it in much the same way. Build won because nothing else passed fit and compliance. That is exactly why the decision gets a review date.

The team also asks regulatory counsel whether the tool could count as medical device software. India's drug regulator, CDSCO, has issued guidance on medical device software under the Medical Devices Rules, 2017, and whether software is a device depends on its intended medical purpose. So the team writes down the intended purpose with care, and gets counsel's view in writing before the build starts.

### The two-year cost of building

Sana estimates three months to build, then 21 months of running within a two-year window: 6,000 × 21 = 126,000 summaries.

| Illustrative cost item | How it is worked out | Two-year cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Build team | 3 people × 3 months × ₹2.5 lakh a month | ₹22.5 lakh |
| Pre-launch clinical evaluation | Doctors rate 400 drafts | ₹3 lakh |
| Security and compliance review | One-off | ₹3 lakh |
| Model usage | ₹5 per summary (₹4 plus 25%) × 126,000 | ₹6.3 lakh |
| Hosting, logging, monitoring | ₹40,000 a month × 21 | ₹8.4 lakh |
| Maintenance | One engineer half time, ₹1.25 lakh a month × 21 | ₹26.25 lakh |
| Ongoing quality sampling | Doctors review a sample, ₹25,000 a month × 21 | ₹5.25 lakh |
| One model migration | Re-test and adjust when the model is retired | ₹4 lakh |
| **Total** | | **₹78.7 lakh, about ₹62 per summary** |

Doctor sign-off time is left out: it is the same whichever option the hospital uses. Figures exclude GST.

### What the numbers tell Sana

- **People dominate.** Model usage is only about 8% of the total. People (building, evaluating, maintaining and migrating) are about 78%. Doubling the token price would add about 8%. Losing the engineer who maintains the system would hurt far more.
- **Volume favours the build.** Most of the cost is fixed. At 12,000 summaries a month, the illustrative total rises only to about ₹85 lakh (with the same team), about ₹34 per summary. A vendor's per-summary fee grows in step with volume.
- **The bar for buying, today.** Suppose a vendor that passed fit and compliance appeared before the build started. Its costs would be integration (₹4 lakh) and an internal owner with quality checks (₹50,000 a month), and it could go live after one month. Over two years it would beat the build at any illustrative fee under about ₹45 per summary: (₹78.7 lakh − ₹4 lakh − ₹12 lakh) ÷ 138,000 summaries in 23 live months = about ₹45.
- **The bar for switching, later.** After the build, its ₹22.5 lakh is sunk. Running it then costs about ₹2.37 lakh a month, with a reserve for future migrations. Switching would cost about ₹7 lakh for integration and re-evaluation, or about ₹0.29 lakh a month spread over 24 months. So a compliant vendor now has to charge under an illustrative ₹26 per summary to be worth the switch: (₹2.37 lakh − ₹0.5 lakh − ₹0.29 lakh) ÷ 6,000 = about ₹26.

### The decision

Leadership approves the build, on a hosted API rather than a self-hosted model, because volume does not justify running GPUs. Sana keeps the prompts, the medication check and the 400-record evaluation set in the hospital's own hands, so the model can be swapped. The yearly market review has a concrete trigger: look again if a product passes fit and compliance at under about ₹26 per summary (the illustrative switching bar), or if volume or rules change.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping the prototype.** Guesses about feasibility are often wrong in both directions.
- **Treating a prototype as launch evidence.** 2 unsafe out of 40 is a reason to evaluate at scale, not a measured 5% rate.
- **Letting the builders judge their own outputs.** Ask domain experts, using a written rubric.
- **Judging vendors on their demo data.** Run every option through your own evaluation set.
- **Calling something differentiating because it matters to you.** Ask whether customers would choose you because of it.
- **Comparing a licence fee with token costs only.** People, evaluation, maintenance and migrations are most of the cost of building.
- **Ignoring sunk costs at review time.** Once built, compare only future costs.
- **Forgetting the exit.** Agree data export and notice terms before you sign, and own your evaluation set.

## Practice

Read SVPG's four big risks, linked below, and Hamel Husain's field guide.

1. **Prototype.** Try an AI task on 20 real examples. Have someone who knows the domain rate the outputs as usable, needs work, or unsafe. Write down what the sample cannot tell you.
2. **Screen.** Find two products and one platform feature that claim to solve the same task. Run your examples through each, and answer the seven due-diligence questions for one of them.
3. **Cost.** Build a two-year cost table for the build route, in the style of Sana's, with every figure marked as an estimate. Work out the vendor fee per unit at which buying would win.
4. **Recommend.** Write a one-page buy, configure or build recommendation with a review date and a concrete trigger for reopening the decision.

**You are done when you can** show expert-rated prototype results, a costed two-year comparison, and a choice you can defend on fit, compliance, cost and lock-in.
