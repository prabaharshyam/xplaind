## What it is

"Can you automate our orders from WhatsApp?" sounds like one question. It hides dozens. Voice notes too? What about a product that is not in the list? Who checks the result? Who may see the messages? Who fixes it in six months? An **automation scope** answers these before work starts, so both sides know what they are getting and paying for.

A scope is a short written agreement, usually one or two pages, not a technical design: the owner must be able to read every line and say yes or no. It has eleven parts:

| Part | Answers the question |
| --- | --- |
| **Process and boundaries** | Where does it start, and where does it end? |
| **Inputs and volumes** | What arrives, in which formats, how many a day? |
| **Steps** | What does the system do, and what do people still do? |
| **Exceptions** | What happens to cases the system cannot handle? |
| **Systems and access** | Which accounts are needed, from whom, by when? |
| **Data and risks** | What personal data goes where? What could go wrong? |
| **Assumptions** | What must stay true for the price to hold? |
| **Success measures** | What improves, against which baseline? |
| **Acceptance and sign-off** | Which test, on which data, and who says "accepted"? |
| **Support and changes** | How long are fixes free, and what counts as a change? |
| **Price, payments and timeline** | How much, paid when, by which dates? |

**In scope** is the work you promise. **Out of scope** is work you have said, in writing, you will not do. A **change request** is a written proposal to alter the scope, with its effect on time and price.

```flow
Discovery | process map, samples -> Draft scope -[client reads]-> Questions, edits
Questions, edits -> Draft scope
Draft scope -> Signed scope | in, out, acceptance, support, price
Signed scope -> Build -> Acceptance test -> Handover -> Support period -> Change requests
```

This lesson follows Ritika, a freelance automation specialist in Indore. A distributor has asked her to "automate orders from WhatsApp". By the end you can write a scope an owner will sign, with a price you can defend.

## How it works

### Start from the process map

Use the map from discovery. Write the start and end as concrete events, as in the worked example below. Then mark each step as **automated**, **assisted** (the system drafts, a person approves) or **manual**. The scope is the map plus these decisions.

A useful default for a first version: if a step is hard to undo, make it assisted, not automated. Removing a human check later, once data shows it is safe, is easy; adding one after a costly mistake is not.

### Collect samples and count them

Ask for about 40 real inputs (20 to 50 works) from normal days, messy ones included, not the client's favourite examples. Then count:

| Input type | Count in 40 samples | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typed text orders | 22 | In scope |
| Photos of handwritten lists | 12 | In scope, with staff review |
| Voice notes | 5 | Out of scope for now; phase 2 |
| Forwarded PDFs from other apps | 1 | Sent to staff as an exception |

At this sample size the counts are rough. Twelve photos in 40 says photos are a large share of the work and risk; one PDF says PDFs exist, not how common they are. So also ask the staff: "What else turns up, even rarely?"

For each exception, choose: handle it automatically, send it to a person, or exclude it, and write the choice down. "Send to a person" needs a named queue and a named person.

Samples hold real customers' details: keep them in a folder the client controls, and delete your copies at the end.

### Agree the human checkpoints

Payments, messages to customers and deletions usually need approval. Name who approves, in which tool. A checkpoint nobody has time for will be skipped within a week, so make it fast: the original message beside the extracted result, and one button to confirm.

### Make access a dependency

List every system, the account needed, who provides it and by when. Access delays often hold projects up more than technical problems do.

WhatsApp is a common example. An automation needs the **WhatsApp Business Platform**, Meta's API, usually through a provider; the free WhatsApp Business app on a phone is not enough. Meta may first ask the business to complete business verification. Providers report that review takes from a few hours to about two weeks, sometimes longer, with no guaranteed time, so the client should start it in week one. Also ask the provider whether staff can keep using the phone app on the same number once it joins the API; Meta allows this in some cases, with conditions.

Write into the scope: "If access arrives late, go-live moves by the same number of days."

### Write down data and risks

Most automations move personal data: names, phone numbers, order history. In India, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (the DPDP Act) covers this. The business that decides why the data is used is the **data fiduciary**. You, the messaging provider and the AI provider process data on its behalf, as **data processors**, and the Act lets a fiduciary use a processor only under a valid contract. The DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025 and most of their duties apply from May 2027, so clients already ask about this. This is not legal advice; for anything unusual, the client should ask a lawyer.

In the scope, answer in plain words:

- what personal data enters the system, and from whom;
- where it is stored and processed, including which AI provider and which region;
- how long raw messages and photos are kept before they are deleted;
- who can see them;
- whether the AI provider trains on business API data (check its current terms and note the date).

List the top three to five risks, each with a mitigation and an owner.

### State the assumptions

An assumption is something you rely on but have not checked, such as "the client keeps the product list current" or "the billing import format does not change". If one fails, the extra work is a change request. This keeps a fixed price fair.

### Measure a baseline, then set targets

You cannot claim a saving you did not measure. Time the current process and count today's errors over a few normal days, then set targets against them (the lesson on measuring ROI turns them into rupees).

### Define acceptance as a test

"The system works well" is not a test. A testable acceptance has five parts:

1. **Data:** which items, from which period, chosen by whom. The client should choose, from in-scope items only.
2. **Answer key:** the correct result for each item, prepared on the client's side before the test.
3. **Definition of correct:** for example, product code and quantity both match the billed invoice.
4. **Threshold:** the number to reach.
5. **Sign-off:** the person with authority to accept, and how long they have to reply.

Two gotchas. First, line accuracy is not order accuracy. If 90% of lines are correct, an order has five lines and errors are spread evenly, only about 59% of orders are fully correct (0.9 to the power 5 is about 0.59). Say which you measure, and what it means for human review. Second, the office manager may run the test, but only the owner can accept it. Name the owner and add a reply window, for example: "If no written list of failures arrives within five working days, the test counts as passed."

### Support and changes

State the support period, for example 30 days of fixes after go-live, and draw the line between a fix and a change:

- **A fix:** the system does not do what the scope says. Free during support.
- **A change:** anything new or different, such as a new input format, field or system, or a change on the client's side like a billing software upgrade. Priced as a change request.

Requests added mid-project without a change to time or price are **scope creep**. Do not absorb them or refuse them: put each on a phase 2 list with a short quote.

### Estimate the effort

Break the work into tasks you can estimate in half-days and add them up. Add a contingency for what you have not seen, for example 15 to 25%, more if samples were thin, and multiply by your day rate. If you cannot estimate a part at all, it is not ready for a fixed price: sell a short paid prototype first.

### Choose a pricing model and payment milestones

| Model | Best when | Who absorbs surprises |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed price | Scope is clear and samples are seen | You |
| Time and materials | Scope is unclear or exploratory | The client |
| Monthly retainer | Maintenance and small changes after support | Shared, within a monthly cap of hours |

A sensible default for a first client is a fixed price paid in milestones, for example 40% on signing, 40% on acceptance and 20% at the end of support. The advance shows commitment and pays for your early days, paying on acceptance protects the client, and the last part keeps you responsive during support. Say whether prices include GST.

Put monthly running costs in the scope too (messaging provider, AI usage, hosting), paid by the client directly where possible.

## A worked example

Ritika's discovery at the Indore distributor, owned by Sanjay Jain, finds about 150 orders a day from 300 retailers, over 26 working days a month. They arrive on WhatsApp as typed text, voice notes, photos of handwritten lists in Hindi and English, and now and then a PDF forwarded from another app. Two staff type every order into billing software that imports CSV files. Her two-week baseline: about 3 minutes per order, and 2% of orders shipped with a wrong quantity.

### The scope she sends

**WhatsApp order entry, version 1, 2 December 2026**

- **Process:** starts when an order message reaches the business WhatsApp number; ends when the day's confirmed orders are in a CSV file the billing software imports.
- **In:** text and photo orders to the business number; AI extraction of retailer, products and quantities, matched to the product list; a review screen where staff confirm or correct every order; a daily CSV export.
- **Out:** voice notes (phase 2), payments and payment reminders, stock checks, automatic replies to retailers, and any change to the billing software.
- **Exceptions:** unknown products or retailers, unreadable photos and forwarded PDFs go to the review queue with the original message.
- **Access (provided by the client):** a WhatsApp Business Platform account through a provider, with Meta business verification started by 4 December and ready by 14 December; product and retailer lists with codes, by 4 December; one CSV file the billing software has accepted.
- **Data:** retailer names, phone numbers and orders pass through the provider, the automation server (hosted in India) and the AI provider's API. Raw messages and photos are deleted after 90 days. Only the two order staff and the owner can log in. Processor terms with both providers are confirmed before go-live.
- **Risks:** verification is delayed (owner: client; build on a test number meanwhile). Photos are unreadable (owner: Ritika; route them to review and report the rate weekly). Staff stop checking (owner: Sanjay Jain; every order needs a confirm click, plus a weekly spot check).
- **Assumptions:** the client keeps the product list current; the CSV format does not change; volumes stay under 250 orders a day.
- **Success:** entry time from about 3 minutes to about 1 minute per order; wrong quantities no higher than today's 2%, aiming for 1%.
- **Acceptance:** 200 in-scope text and photo orders from October 2026, chosen by the client. The client's accountant prepares the answer key from the billed invoices by 7 December. A line is correct when the product code and quantity both match the invoice. Pass: at least 90% of lines correct before staff review, and every exception in the review queue. Sign-off: Sanjay Jain, in writing, within five working days of the test.
- **Support:** 30 days of fixes after go-live. New formats, fields or systems, and changes on the client's side, are change requests at ₹7,500 a day.
- **Price and timeline:** ₹90,000 fixed, excluding GST, paid ₹36,000 on signing, ₹36,000 on acceptance and ₹18,000 at the end of support. Running costs of about ₹9,000 a month are paid by the client. Acceptance test in the week of 28 December; go-live on 4 January 2027 if WhatsApp access is ready by 14 December, otherwise later by the length of the delay.

### How she built the price

The day rate and running costs are illustrations, not market rates.

| Task | Days |
| --- | --- |
| Discovery, samples and this scope | 1 |
| WhatsApp intake and message store | 1.5 |
| AI extraction and product matching | 3 |
| Staff review screen | 2 |
| Daily CSV export in the billing format | 1 |
| Acceptance test and fixes | 1.5 |
| Handover and staff training | 0.5 |
| Contingency, about 15%, for photos and Hindi product names | 1.5 |
| **Total** | **12 days × ₹7,500 = ₹90,000** |

| Running cost | Working | Per month |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AI usage | 3,900 orders × an assumed ₹0.50 average (photos cost more than text) | ₹1,950 |
| Messaging provider | Assumed plan price | ₹4,000 |
| Hosting and database | Assumed | ₹1,500 |
| Meta message charges | The system only receives messages, and providers report that Meta does not charge for incoming messages | ₹0 |
| GST at 18% | On ₹7,450 | ₹1,341 |
| **Total** | | **₹8,791, about ₹9,000** |

For a real AI figure, run the 40 samples through your chosen model, multiply the logged token counts by the provider's current price, and note the date.

### Two weeks in

The owner asks for automatic payment reminders to retailers. The scope lists them as out, so Ritika puts them on the phase 2 list with a quote, and explains why they are not small. A reminder to a retailer who has not messaged in the last 24 hours must use a Meta-approved template, and each delivered one is charged. For example, 300 retailers × 4 reminders is 1,200 utility messages a month; at about ₹0.115 each, the figure third-party reports give for India (checked November 2026; confirm on Meta's rate card), that is about ₹138 before GST. The money is small; the work is not: template approval, retailers' consent, and the risk of reminding a shop that has paid. The conversation takes two minutes.

### Three weeks after go-live

Two requests arrive. The CSV drops lines when an order has more than 20 items: the system breaks the scope, so it is a free fix. The upgraded billing software expects new columns: a client-side change, so a change request for half a day.

## Common mistakes

- **Scope without samples,** or with samples picked to look good.
- **Silence on what is out.** Unwritten exclusions become free work.
- **Access as an afterthought.** Meta verification alone can outlast parts of the build.
- **Acceptance with no answer key or named signer.** The test becomes an opinion.
- **Measuring lines and promising orders.** At 90% of lines correct, many orders still need fixing.
- **No data section.** The first privacy question halts go-live.
- **No line between a fix and a change.** Every request arrives as "it is broken".
- **Hiding running costs,** or writing a scope the owner cannot read.

## Practice

Read Atlassian's guide to project scope and GOV.UK's guide to user stories, linked below.

1. **Samples.** For an idea from your process map, collect 20 to 40 real or realistic inputs and count them, with a decision per type.
2. **Scope.** Write all eleven parts in one or two pages of plain words, and compare with Ritika's.
3. **Acceptance.** Write the five-part test and ask someone else how they would run it. If they have a question, it is not finished.
4. **Price.** Build an effort table with contingency and a running-cost table, dating every price.

**You are done when you can** hand your scope to the business owner and have them agree, in writing, what is in, what is out, how success is measured and what it will cost.
