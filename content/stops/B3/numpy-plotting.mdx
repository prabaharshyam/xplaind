## What it is

**NumPy** is Python's library for fast numerical arrays. Pandas, scikit-learn and PyTorch all build on its ideas. **Matplotlib** is the standard plotting library; others (Seaborn, Plotly) build on similar concepts.

The core skills:

- create arrays and understand their **shape**;
- do maths on whole arrays at once (**vectorisation**) instead of Python loops;
- combine arrays of different shapes with **broadcasting**;
- select data with indexing and boolean masks;
- plot distributions, relationships and trends.

## How it works

**Arrays and shapes.** `np.array([[1, 2], [3, 4]])` has shape `(2, 2)`. A dataset of 10,000 customers with 12 features has shape `(10000, 12)`. Most bugs are shape mismatches; print `.shape` often.

**Vectorisation.** `prices * 1.18` adds 18% GST to every element at once, far faster than a loop. Functions such as `np.mean`, `np.std`, `np.percentile` work on whole arrays and along an axis (`axis=0` for columns).

**Broadcasting.** NumPy stretches smaller arrays to match bigger ones when shapes are compatible. Subtracting the column means from a `(10000, 12)` array with a `(12,)` array centres every column in one line.

**Boolean masks.** `incomes[incomes > 50000]` selects matching values; combine conditions with `&` and `|`.

**Random numbers.** `np.random.default_rng(42)` gives a reproducible generator for sampling and splitting data.

**Plots that matter in ML.**

- **Histogram**: the distribution of one feature; reveals skew, outliers and impossible values.
- **Scatter plot**: the relationship between two features, or a feature and the target.
- **Line chart**: values over time, such as daily orders or training loss by epoch.
- **Box plot**: comparing distributions across groups, such as delivery time by city.

Always label axes and units, and use a log scale for very skewed data.

## A worked example

A Bengaluru quick-commerce company wants to predict delivery time. You load 50,000 deliveries into arrays: distance (km), items, hour of day and actual minutes.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

print(X.shape, y.shape)            # (50000, 3) (50000,)
print(np.percentile(y, [50, 90, 99]))  # median 14, p90 24, p99 61 minutes

plt.hist(y, bins=60)
plt.xlabel("Delivery time (minutes)"); plt.ylabel("Orders")
plt.show()

plt.scatter(X[:, 0], y, s=2, alpha=0.3)
plt.xlabel("Distance (km)"); plt.ylabel("Minutes")
plt.show()
```

The histogram shows a long tail and a spike at exactly 0 minutes: 412 deliveries recorded as zero, a logging bug. The scatter plot shows that beyond 6 km, times jump: those orders came from a different dark store with fewer riders. Both findings change the model: drop or fix the zero rows, and add a store feature. No metric would have shown them as clearly as two plots.

## Common mistakes

- **Python loops over arrays.** Vectorise; it is shorter and much faster.
- **Ignoring shapes.** Print them and check broadcasting behaves as you expect.
- **Modelling before plotting.** Plots reveal bugs, outliers and leakage early.
- **Unlabelled charts.** Axes and units make plots readable to others.
- **Linear scales on skewed data.** Use log scales where values span orders of magnitude.
