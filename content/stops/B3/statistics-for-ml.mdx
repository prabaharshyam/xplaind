## What it is

Machine learning is learning from data, and data is noisy, skewed and incomplete. **Statistics** is the toolkit for learning from data under uncertainty without fooling yourself. For ML engineering, five areas come up daily:

| Area | Question it answers | Typical mistake it prevents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Describing data** | What is typical, and how spread out? | Reporting a misleading average |
| **Sampling** | Does our data represent the people we care about? | A model that fails in tier-3 towns |
| **Relationships** | Do these move together, and does one cause the other? | Claiming a feature causes an outcome |
| **Uncertainty** | How sure are we about this number? | Celebrating a 1-point gain that is noise |
| **Experiments** | Did our change really help? | Crediting a festival sale to a new model |

```text
  Order values (skewed: most small, a few huge)

  count
   |  ####
   |  ########
   |  ##########
   |  ######
   |  ###
   |  #                                         #
   +----------------------------------------------- value
      ^ median ₹780                    ^ mean ₹1,240 pulled right by a few big orders
```

This lesson follows the data team of an e-commerce company in Mumbai as a claim that a new recommendation model "raised order value by ₹70" turns out, under proper statistics, to be a much smaller, real gain. By the end you will be able to design a fair A/B test and explain why small test sets mislead.

## How it works

### Averages and spread

The **mean** adds everything up and divides. It is pulled by extreme values. The **median** is the middle value when sorted. It is not.

Indian income, order values and insurance claims are usually **skewed**: most values are small and a few are huge. So the median is often more typical. For spread:

| Data shape | Average to use | Spread to use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Roughly symmetric, bell-shaped | Mean | Standard deviation |
| Skewed, long tail | Median | Percentiles, such as the 90th and 99th |

Always plot a histogram before you summarise.

### Distributions

Many quantities are roughly **normal** (bell-shaped), such as measurement errors. Many business quantities are skewed or have long tails. Counts of rare events follow other shapes. The shape decides which summaries and models make sense.

### Sampling

A model trained on metro customers may fail in tier-3 towns. Ask: **who is missing from this data?** Was it collected in a way that favours some cases, such as only customers who complained, or only loans that were approved (so you never see how rejected applicants would have repaid)? This **selection bias** quietly breaks models after launch.

### Correlation and causation

Two things moving together does not mean one causes the other. Ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer: heat drives both. A model can use correlations to **predict**, but to claim a **cause**, for example that a discount increased retention, you need an experiment or careful causal methods.

### Confidence intervals

A measured accuracy of 87% on 200 test examples could easily be 82% or 92% on another sample of 200. A **confidence interval** expresses this range.

```text
  Accuracy on 200 examples, with 95% intervals:
  Model A  87%   |-------[====*====]-------|   about 82% to 92%
  Model B  88%    |-------[====*====]-------|  about 83% to 93%
                  heavily overlapping: no clear winner
```

Small test sets give wide intervals. Do not celebrate a 1-point gain on 200 examples. One practical way to compute an interval is **bootstrapping**: resample the test set many times with replacement, recompute the metric each time, and look at the middle 95% of results.

### A/B tests

To know whether a new model helps, **randomly** split users: half see the old version, half the new. Compare a metric you chose in advance.

| Rule | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Decide the metric and sample size in advance | Otherwise you pick whichever number looks good |
| Randomise who sees which version | So the groups differ only by the change |
| Run for full weeks | Weekday and weekend behaviour differ |
| Do not stop early because it looks good | Early results swing; stopping on a high inflates the effect |
| Watch guardrail metrics | A gain in order value should not come with more returns or complaints |

## A worked example

A Mumbai e-commerce company's data team reports that a new recommendation model raised average order value from ₹1,240 to ₹1,310: a ₹70 gain. Leadership is ready to celebrate. You check it.

**Averages.** Order values are skewed. A few large electronics orders moved the mean. The median rose only from ₹780 to ₹790.

**Sampling.** The comparison was last month, with the old model, against this month, with the new one. This month included a festival sale. The two months are not comparable.

**A proper test.** The team runs a two-week A/B test: 200,000 users per group, randomly assigned, with median order value and conversion rate chosen as metrics in advance, and returns as a guardrail.

| Measure | Old model | New model | Difference (95% interval) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Median order value | ₹781 | ₹799 | +₹18 (₹6 to ₹30) |
| Conversion rate | 3.42% | 3.43% | No clear change |
| Return rate (guardrail) | 6.1% | 6.0% | No clear change |

The model does help, but by about a quarter of what the first report claimed. Leadership decides the gain still justifies rolling it out, now with honest numbers, and the team adds "A/B test before claiming impact" to its launch checklist.

## Common mistakes

- **Reporting the mean of skewed data.** Look at the median and a histogram.
- **Comparing different time periods.** Seasons and festivals change everything; randomise instead.
- **Ignoring who is missing from the data.** Selection bias breaks models later.
- **Treating correlation as causation.** Prediction is not proof of cause.
- **Small test sets and tiny gains.** Check the confidence interval before claiming improvement.
- **Peeking and stopping A/B tests early.** Decide the duration in advance and stick to it.

Watch StatQuest's videos on histograms, mean versus median, confidence intervals and A/B testing, linked below, and the data module of Google's Machine Learning Crash Course.

1. **Mean versus median.** Load any public dataset with a money column. Plot a histogram, and compare the mean and the median.
2. **Bootstrap.** Compute a 95% confidence interval for a model's accuracy on 200 test examples by resampling the test set 1,000 times.
3. **Design a test.** Design an A/B test for a change you care about: the metric, guardrails, a sample size estimate and the duration.

**You are done when you can** explain why a 1-point accuracy gain on 200 examples is not convincing, and design a fair A/B test.
