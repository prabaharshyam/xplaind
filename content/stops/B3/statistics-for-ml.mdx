## What it is

Machine learning is learning from data, and data is noisy, skewed and incomplete. **Statistics** is the toolkit for learning from data under uncertainty without fooling yourself. For ML engineering, five areas come up every week:

| Area | Question it answers | Typical mistake it prevents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Describing data** | What is typical, and how spread out? | Reporting a misleading average |
| **Sampling** | Does our data represent the people we care about? | A model that fails in tier-3 towns |
| **Relationships** | Do these move together, and does one cause the other? | Claiming a feature causes an outcome |
| **Uncertainty** | How sure are we about this number? | Celebrating a 1-point gain that is noise |
| **Experiments** | Did our change really help? | Crediting a festival sale to a new model |

```text
  Order values (skewed: most small, a few huge)

  count
   |  ####
   |  ########
   |  ##########
   |  ######
   |  ###
   |  #                                         #
   +----------------------------------------------- value
      ^ median ₹780                    ^ mean ₹1,240 pulled right by a few big orders
```

This lesson follows the data team of an e-commerce company in Mumbai. A report claims a new recommendation model "raised average order value by ₹70". Under proper statistics, the real gain turns out to be about ₹1.55 per user: smaller, but real. By the end you will be able to compare two models fairly, size and run an A/B test, and explain why small test sets mislead.

## How it works

### Averages and spread

The **mean** adds everything up and divides by the count. It is pulled by extreme values. The **median** is the middle value when sorted. It is not.

The **standard deviation** (SD) measures spread: roughly, how far a typical value sits from the mean. For bell-shaped data, about 68% of values fall within one SD of the mean and about 95% within two.

That rule breaks on **skewed** data, where most values are small and a few are huge. Indian incomes, order values, insurance claims and app latencies are all skewed. There, "mean plus two SDs" misjudges the tail, and percentiles describe it honestly. The 90th percentile is the value that 90% of cases fall below.

| Data shape | Average to use | Spread to use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Roughly symmetric, bell-shaped | Mean | Standard deviation |
| Skewed, long tail | Median | Percentiles, such as the 90th and 99th |

Always plot a histogram before you summarise.

### Distributions you will meet

A **probability distribution** describes how likely each value is. Knowing the shape tells you which summaries, tests and models make sense. Four shapes cover most ML work:

| Distribution | What it counts | Where you meet it in ML |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Binomial** | Successes in n yes-or-no trials | Correct answers out of n test questions; buyers out of n visitors |
| **Poisson** | Events per fixed window, when events are independent | Orders per minute, support tickets per hour; alerting on unusual spikes |
| **Normal** | Values that are the sum or average of many small effects | Averages over large samples, which is why intervals for accuracy and means work |
| **Long-tailed** (such as log-normal) | Money, time and size | Order values, latency, session length: use medians and percentiles, and often take the log before modelling |

**Base rates.** When the thing you look for is rare, even a good classifier makes mostly false alarms. For example, suppose 0.5% of 1 lakh UPI transactions are fraud (500 cases). A model catches 90% of fraud and wrongly flags 5% of genuine payments. It flags 450 frauds and 4,975 genuine payments, so only 8% of its alerts are fraud. Always ask for the base rate before you judge precision.

### Sampling and selection bias

A model is only as good as the sample it learned from. Ask: **who is missing from this data?** Data collected in a way that favours some cases creates **selection bias**. Examples: only customers who complained, or only loans that were approved, so you never see how rejected applicants would have repaid.

Selection bias quietly breaks models after launch. Catch it before launch by comparing segment shares in your training data with recent live traffic. Use city tier, device, language and new versus returning users. For example, if 72% of training rows come from metros but only 48% of last month's users do, the model has seen too few small-town users. Collect more, reweight, or at least report accuracy per segment.

### Correlation, causation and Simpson's paradox

Two things moving together does not mean one causes the other. Ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer: heat drives both. A model can use correlations to **predict**. To claim a **cause**, for example that a discount increased retention, you need a randomised experiment or careful causal methods.

Mixing groups can even reverse a conclusion. This is **Simpson's paradox**. Suppose a support bot is rolled out mostly to Hindi users, whose questions are harder:

| Queries | Old bot resolved | New bot resolved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| English | 720 of 900 (80%) | 255 of 300 (85%) |
| Hindi | 50 of 100 (50%) | 385 of 700 (55%) |
| **All** | **770 of 1,000 (77%)** | **640 of 1,000 (64%)** |

The new bot is better in each language but looks worse overall, because it got more of the hard queries. Compare like with like, by segment, or randomise who gets which version.

### Standard error and confidence intervals

The SD describes individual values. The **standard error** (SE) describes how much an estimate, such as a mean or an accuracy, would wobble from sample to sample. It shrinks with the square root of the sample size: four times the data halves the SE. For an accuracy p measured on n examples, SE is the square root of p × (1 − p) ÷ n.

A **95% confidence interval** is roughly the estimate plus or minus 2 SE. It gives a range of plausible values for the true number, built by a method that captures the true value 95% of the time. With 200 test examples, the true accuracy behind a measured 87% is plausibly anywhere from about 82% to 92%.

| Test examples | 95% interval around a measured 87% |
| --- | --- |
| 200 | about ± 4.7 points |
| 1,000 | about ± 2.1 points |
| 5,000 | about ± 0.9 points |

A quick way to get an interval for any metric, including F1 or a median, is **bootstrapping**. Resample your test set many times with replacement, recompute the metric each time, and take the middle 95% of the results.

### Comparing two models on the same test set

Model A scores 87% and model B scores 88% on the same 200 questions. A common habit is to check whether the two intervals overlap. That rule is misleading: two 95% intervals can overlap even when the difference is real. It also ignores that both models answered the *same* questions, so questions that both get right or both get wrong cancel out, and only the disagreements matter.

Instead, measure the **difference directly, paired by question**:

```text
  Same 200 questions (illustrative):
  A right, B wrong:   7      B right, A wrong:   9      net: B +2 questions
  Paired bootstrap of B minus A:  +1 point, 95% interval -3 to +5 points
                                  includes 0: no clear winner
```

```python
import numpy as np

# 1 = answered correctly, 0 = wrong; the same 200 questions in the same order
a = np.array([1]*167 + [1]*7 + [0]*9 + [0]*17)   # model A: 174 right, 87%
b = np.array([1]*167 + [0]*7 + [1]*9 + [0]*17)   # model B: 176 right, 88%

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
idx = rng.integers(0, len(a), size=(10_000, len(a)))   # resample questions
diffs = b[idx].mean(axis=1) - a[idx].mean(axis=1)      # B minus A on the same draw
low, high = np.percentile(diffs, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"B minus A: {b.mean() - a.mean():+.1%}, 95% interval {low:+.1%} to {high:+.1%}")
# B minus A: +1.0%, 95% interval -3.0% to +5.0%
```

The classic alternative is **McNemar's test**, which looks only at the questions where the models disagree. Here the exact test gives p = 0.80. A useful gotcha: the exact McNemar test cannot reach p below 0.05 unless one model wins at least 6 more disagreements than it loses (6 to 0 gives p = 0.03), however many questions you have.

### p-values and significance in plain words

A **p-value** answers one question: if the change truly did nothing, how often would random noise alone produce a difference at least this large? A p-value of 0.03 means about 3% of the time. By convention, a result with p below 0.05 is called **statistically significant**.

A p-value is *not* the chance that the change works, and a small p-value does not mean a big effect. With a large enough sample, even a gain too small to be worth shipping becomes significant. Always report the size of the effect with its confidence interval, not just the p-value.

### A/B tests

To know whether a change helps, **randomly** split users: some see the old version (control), some the new (treatment). Run both during the same days and compare a metric you chose in advance.

Choose the primary metric with care. **Average order value** counts only users who bought. If the new model changes *who* buys, the buyers in the two groups are different people, and the comparison is biased. **Revenue per user** divides all revenue by all users in the group, buyers or not, so it stays fair. Make it the primary metric, and keep order value and conversion as secondary metrics.

| Rule | Why |
| --- | --- |
| One primary metric, sample size and duration fixed in advance | When the change does nothing, checking 20 independent metrics gives a 64% chance that at least one shows a "significant" difference by luck |
| Randomise who sees which version | So the groups differ only by the change |
| Check the sample ratio | A planned 50/50 split of 10 lakh users that comes out 5,03,000 to 4,97,000 signals a bug in assignment or logging; do not trust the result |
| Run for full weeks | Weekday and weekend behaviour differ |
| Do not stop early because it looks good | In a simulation we ran of a change that did nothing, checking daily for 14 days and stopping at the first p below 0.05 declared a "winner" 22% of the time, not 5% |
| Watch for novelty effects | Users click a new design because it is new; check whether the gain fades in week two |
| Watch guardrail metrics | A gain in revenue should not come with more returns or complaints |

### How many users?

Before the test, decide the sample size from four inputs:

1. **Baseline**: the current value, for example a 3.4% conversion rate.
2. **Minimum detectable effect (MDE)**: the smallest gain worth finding, for example a 10% relative lift, from 3.4% to 3.74%.
3. **Significance level**: usually 5%, the false-alarm rate you accept when the change does nothing.
4. **Power**: usually 80%, the chance of detecting a real effect of the MDE's size.

With these inputs you need about **46,700 users per group**. Halve the MDE to a 5% lift and you need about 1.83 lakh per group: halving the effect roughly quadruples the users. In Python, `statsmodels.stats.power.NormalIndPower().solve_power` does this calculation, with the effect size from `statsmodels.stats.proportion.proportion_effectsize(0.0374, 0.034)`.

Revenue per user needs more users than conversion does, about twice as many in the example below, because a few huge orders make it noisy. Two standard fixes help. Cap each order, or each buyer's revenue, at a high percentile of buyers' values, such as the 99th, before averaging. Do not use a percentile over all users: most spend nothing, so the 99th percentile of all users is far too low. Or use a variance-reduction method such as CUPED, which adjusts for each user's spending before the test.

### Before you claim a win

```flow
title: Is the gain real?
Claimed gain -> Both versions in the same period?
Both versions in the same period? -[no]-> Run a randomised A/B test
Both versions in the same period? -[yes]-> Split came out as planned?
Run a randomised A/B test -> Split came out as planned?
Split came out as planned? -[no]-> Fix assignment and rerun
Split came out as planned? -[yes]-> Interval of the difference excludes zero?
Interval of the difference excludes zero? -[no]-> No clear winner | more data or a bigger change
Interval of the difference excludes zero? -[yes]-> Guardrails healthy?
Guardrails healthy? -[no]-> Do not ship
Guardrails healthy? -[yes]-> Ship and report the interval
```

## A worked example

A Mumbai e-commerce company's data team reports that a new recommendation model raised average order value from ₹1,240 to ₹1,310: a ₹70 gain. Leadership is ready to celebrate. You check it. The test numbers below are illustrative, generated from one simulated test, but they behave the way real tests do.

**Step 1: averages.** Order values are skewed. A few large electronics orders moved the mean. The median order rose only from ₹780 to ₹790.

**Step 2: timing.** The comparison was last month, with the old model, against this month, with the new one. This month included a festival sale. The two months are not comparable, so the ₹70 says nothing about the model.

**Step 3: design a proper test.** Before starting, the team fixes:

- **Primary metric:** revenue per user, with each user's revenue capped at the 99th percentile of order values (about ₹7,300).
- **Secondary metrics:** conversion, mean and median order value. **Guardrail:** return rate.
- **Size:** detecting a 5% lift in capped revenue per user needs about 3.9 lakh users per group. Two full weeks of traffic gives about 5 lakh per group, so the test runs for exactly two weeks.

**Step 4: check the split.** The groups have 5,00,212 and 4,99,788 users. A chi-square test on these counts gives p = 0.67, so the split is consistent with a fair 50/50.

**Step 5: read the results.**

| Measure | Old model | New model | Difference (95% interval) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue per user, capped (primary) | ₹40.61 | ₹42.16 | +₹1.55 (+₹0.29 to +₹2.81) |
| Conversion rate | 3.38% | 3.45% | +0.07 points (about 0 to +0.14) |
| Mean order value | ₹1,237 | ₹1,258 | +₹21 (−₹12 to +₹54) |
| Median order value | ₹781 | ₹796 | +₹15 (−₹6 to +₹34) |
| Return rate per buyer (guardrail) | 6.28% | 6.15% | −0.13 points (−0.64 to +0.38) |

**Step 6: decide.** The primary metric's interval excludes zero, so the model really helps: about +3.8% revenue per user. The gain comes from slightly more buying and slightly bigger orders, neither clear on its own. Returns show no harm. The ₹70 in the first report came from the festival month and a few big orders. Notice that mean order value alone would have looked inconclusive.

Per 10 lakh users, the gain is worth about ₹15.5 lakh in capped revenue, with a plausible range of ₹2.9 lakh to ₹28 lakh. Leadership rolls the model out with these honest numbers. The team adds "A/B test before claiming impact" to its launch checklist.

## Common mistakes

- **Reporting the mean of skewed data.** Look at the median, percentiles and a histogram.
- **Ignoring the base rate.** A 95%-accurate fraud model can still be wrong on most of its alerts.
- **Comparing different time periods.** Seasons and festivals change everything; randomise instead.
- **Ignoring who is missing from the data.** Compare training segments with live traffic before launch.
- **Treating correlation as causation.** Prediction is not proof of cause, and pooled numbers can reverse (Simpson's paradox).
- **Judging two models by overlapping intervals.** Use a paired bootstrap or McNemar's test on the same test set.
- **Using a buyers-only metric as the primary metric.** Use revenue per user, not order value.
- **Peeking, stopping early, or checking many metrics.** Fix one primary metric, the sample size and the duration in advance.
- **Skipping the sample ratio check.** An uneven split means a broken test, not a result.
