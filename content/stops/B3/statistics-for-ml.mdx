## What it is

Statistics is the toolkit for learning from data under uncertainty. For ML engineering, five areas matter most:

- **Describing data**: averages, spread and shape.
- **Sampling**: whether your data represents the population you care about.
- **Relationships**: correlation and why it is not causation.
- **Uncertainty**: how sure you are about a number, with confidence intervals.
- **Experiments**: A/B tests to measure whether a change really helps.

## How it works

**Averages and spread.** The mean is pulled by extreme values; the median is not. Indian income, order values and claim amounts are usually skewed, so the median is often more typical. Spread: the standard deviation for roughly symmetric data, percentiles (such as the 90th) for skewed data. Always plot a histogram before summarising.

**Distributions.** Many quantities are roughly normal (bell-shaped), but many in business are skewed or have long tails. Counts of rare events often follow other distributions. The shape decides which summaries and models make sense.

**Sampling.** A model trained on metro customers may fail in tier-3 towns. Ask: who is missing from this data? Was it collected in a way that favours some cases (only customers who complained, only approved loans)? Selection bias quietly breaks models.

**Correlation and causation.** Two things moving together does not mean one causes the other. Ice-cream sales and drowning both rise in summer. A model can use correlations to predict, but you need experiments or careful methods to claim causes, for example that a discount increased retention.

**Confidence intervals.** A measured accuracy of 87% on 200 test examples could easily be 82% or 92% on another sample. Confidence intervals express this range. Small test sets give wide intervals; do not celebrate a 1-point gain on 200 examples.

**A/B tests.** To know whether a new model helps, randomly split users: half see the old, half the new. Compare a pre-chosen metric. Decide the sample size in advance, run for full weeks (to cover weekly patterns), and do not stop early because the result looks good. Watch guardrail metrics too.

## A worked example

A Mumbai e-commerce company's data team reports that a new recommendation model raised average order value from ₹1,240 to ₹1,310.

Checking it:

- **Averages**: order values are skewed; a few large electronics orders moved the mean. The median rose only from ₹780 to ₹790.
- **Sampling**: the comparison was last month (old model) against this month (new model), which included a festival sale. Not a fair comparison.
- **Proper test**: a two-week A/B test with 200,000 users per group, randomised, with the median order value and conversion rate as metrics. Result: median order value up ₹18 (a 95% confidence interval of ₹6 to ₹30), conversion unchanged.

The model does help, but by much less than the first report claimed. Leadership decides the gain still justifies rollout, now with honest numbers.

## Common mistakes

- **Reporting the mean of skewed data.** Look at the median and a histogram.
- **Comparing different time periods.** Seasons and festivals change everything; randomise.
- **Ignoring who is missing from the data.** Selection bias breaks models later.
- **Treating correlation as causation.**
- **Small test sets and tiny gains.** Check the confidence interval before claiming improvement.

## Practise

Watch StatQuest videos on histograms, mean versus median, confidence intervals and A/B testing, and the data module of Google's ML Crash Course.

1. Load any public dataset with a money column. Plot a histogram, and compare mean and median.
2. Compute a 95% confidence interval for a model's accuracy on 200 test examples by bootstrapping (resampling the test set many times).
3. Design an A/B test for a change you care about: metric, guardrails, sample size estimate and duration.

**You are done when you can** explain why a 1-point accuracy gain on 200 examples is not convincing, and design a fair A/B test.
