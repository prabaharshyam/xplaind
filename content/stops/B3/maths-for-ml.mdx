## What it is

People often put off machine learning because of the maths. You do not need to be a mathematician. You need **intuition** for three areas, and the ability to follow a formula when you meet one:

| Area | Core idea | Where it shows up in ML |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Linear algebra** | Data as **vectors** (lists of numbers) and **matrices** (tables of numbers), and operations on them | A customer is a vector of features; a dataset is a matrix; a model's weights are matrices |
| **Calculus** | How a quantity changes when you nudge its inputs | Models learn by following the **gradient**, the direction in which the error falls fastest |
| **Probability** | Describing uncertainty | Models output probabilities; data has distributions; evaluation asks how likely results are to hold |

```text
     Linear algebra             Calculus                  Probability
   +----------------+     +------------------+      +------------------+
   | data, weights  |     | how error changes|      | how sure are we? |
   | as vectors and | --> | when weights move| -->  | outputs, base    |
   | matrices       |     | (gradients)      |      | rates, Bayes     |
   +----------------+     +------------------+      +------------------+
      what a model          how it learns             how to read and
      computes                                        trust its output
```

This lesson follows a fintech in Hyderabad as it trains a simple loan-default model and learns, the hard way, why scale and base rates matter. By the end you will explain gradient descent with a sketch and say why rare events produce mostly false alarms.

## How it works

### Vectors and similarity

A **vector** is a list of numbers describing one thing. Two vectors pointing in a similar direction describe similar things. The **dot product** multiplies matching entries and adds them up:

```text
  a = (3, 1, 2)      b = (2, 0, 4)
  a . b = 3*2 + 1*0 + 2*4 = 6 + 0 + 8 = 14
```

A large dot product means a similar direction. **Cosine similarity** divides by the vectors' lengths, so only direction counts, not size. This is how recommendation systems and embedding search measure "similar".

### Matrices as transformations

Multiplying a vector by a matrix transforms it: rotating, stretching or mixing features. A layer of a neural network is a matrix multiplication followed by a simple non-linear function.

**Shapes must match.** A matrix with 100 rows and 20 columns, times a vector of 20 weights, gives 100 predictions:

```text
   data X            weights w        predictions
  (100 x 20)    x     (20 x 1)    =    (100 x 1)
       ^^                ^^
       these must be equal
```

Most matrix bugs in code are shape mismatches. Print the shapes.

### Derivatives and gradients

A **derivative** says how much the output changes when one input changes slightly. With many inputs, the **gradient** collects all those derivatives into a vector that points uphill.

**Gradient descent** trains a model by taking small steps downhill on the **loss**, the measure of error, again and again until it stops improving:

```text
   loss
    |  *
    |    *                        too-large steps bounce around:
    |      *                          *       *
    |        *  *                    *   *   *   *
    |            * * * * --           (never settles)
    +--------------------------> steps
          small, steady steps
```

The **learning rate** is the step size. Too large and training jumps around or explodes; too small and it crawls. Watch the loss curve and adjust.

### The chain rule and backpropagation

Neural networks stack many functions. The **chain rule** computes how the final error depends on early weights, through all the layers in between. **Backpropagation** is the chain rule applied efficiently, which is what makes training large networks possible.

### Probability

- A **probability distribution** says how likely each value is.
- **Expected value** is the long-run average.
- **Conditional probability**, such as "probability of default given income below ₹25,000", is the heart of classification.
- **Bayes' rule** updates beliefs with evidence, and explains why rare events produce many false alarms even with accurate tests.

### Scaling

If one feature ranges up to 400 and another stays below 1, gradient descent zigzags and trains slowly or unstably. **Standardising** features, so each has a similar range, helps many models train faster and better.

| Feature | Raw range | After standardising |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EMI as a share of income | 0.05 to 0.9 | about -2 to +2 |
| Missed payments last year | 0 to 12 | about -2 to +2 |
| Months at current job | 1 to 400 | about -2 to +2 |

## A worked example

A fintech in Hyderabad predicts monthly EMI default risk with a simple model:

```text
score = w1 x (EMI / income) + w2 x (missed payments last year)
      + w3 x (months at current job) + b
```

**Vectors and weights.** Each applicant is a vector of three features. The weights w1, w2, w3 and b form the model.

**Training.** It starts with random weights, predicts for 50,000 past loans, measures the loss (how far predictions are from what actually happened), computes the gradient (how the loss changes with each weight), and nudges each weight downhill. After a few hundred steps, the loss stops falling.

**The first problem: scale.** At first training was unstable: the loss jumped around. "Months at job" ranged up to 400, while the EMI ratio was below 1, so one weight needed tiny steps and another large ones. Standardising all three features fixed it.

**The second lesson: base rates.** Suppose 2% of applicants default, the model catches 90% of defaulters, and it wrongly flags 10% of good applicants. Out of 1,000 applicants:

| | Will default | Will not default | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Count | 20 | 980 | 1,000 |
| Flagged by the model | 18 (90% of 20) | 98 (10% of 980) | 116 |
| Not flagged | 2 | 882 | 884 |

Of the 116 flagged applicants, only 18, about one in six, will actually default. Most flags are false alarms, simply because good applicants are so much more common. That is why the bank uses the score to **prioritise review**, not to reject automatically.

## Common mistakes

- **Trying to master all the maths first.** Learn intuition, then deepen as you need it.
- **Ignoring shapes.** Most matrix bugs are shape mismatches; print the shapes.
- **Guessing the learning rate.** Watch the loss curve and adjust.
- **Unscaled features.** Standardise for gradient-based models.
- **Ignoring base rates.** Rare events plus imperfect models mean many false alarms.
- **Reading a probability as a certainty.** A 0.8 score means likely, not definite.
