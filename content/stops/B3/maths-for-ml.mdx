## What it is

Three areas of maths carry most of machine learning:

- **Linear algebra**: data as **vectors** (lists of numbers) and **matrices** (tables of numbers), and operations on them. A customer is a vector of features; a dataset is a matrix; a model's weights are matrices.
- **Calculus**: how a quantity changes when you nudge its inputs. Models learn by following the **gradient**, the direction in which the error falls fastest.
- **Probability**: describing uncertainty. Models output probabilities, data has distributions, and evaluation is about how likely results are to hold.

Aim for intuition and the ability to follow a formula, not proofs.

## How it works

**Vectors and similarity.** Two vectors pointing in a similar direction are similar. The dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them: a large value means similar direction. This is how recommendation and embedding search measure similarity.

**Matrices as transformations.** Multiplying a vector by a matrix transforms it: rotating, stretching, mixing features. A layer of a neural network is a matrix multiplication followed by a simple non-linear function. Shapes must match: a (100 × 20) data matrix times a (20 × 1) weight vector gives 100 predictions.

**Derivatives and gradients.** A derivative says how much the output changes when one input changes slightly. With many inputs, the gradient collects all these derivatives into a vector pointing uphill. **Gradient descent** takes small steps downhill on the loss (the error), repeatedly, until it stops improving. The **learning rate** is the step size: too large and training jumps around or explodes; too small and it crawls.

**The chain rule.** When functions are stacked, as in neural networks, the chain rule computes how the final error depends on early weights. Backpropagation is the chain rule applied efficiently.

**Probability.** A probability distribution says how likely each value is. Expected value is the long-run average. Conditional probability ("probability of default given income below ₹25,000") is the heart of classification. Bayes' rule updates beliefs with evidence and explains why rare events produce many false alarms even with accurate tests.

**Scaling.** Features on very different scales (income in lakhs, age in years) make gradient descent zigzag. Standardising features to similar scales helps many models train faster and better.

## A worked example

A Hyderabad fintech predicts monthly EMI default risk with a simple model:

score = w1 × (EMI ÷ income) + w2 × (missed payments last year) + w3 × (months at current job) + b

Each applicant is a vector of three features; the weights w1, w2, w3 and b form the model. Training starts with random weights, computes predictions for 50,000 past loans, measures the loss (how far predictions are from what actually happened), computes the gradient (how the loss changes with each weight) and nudges each weight downhill. After a few hundred steps the loss stops falling.

When training was unstable at first, the cause was scale: "months at job" ranged up to 400 while the EMI ratio was below 1. Standardising features fixed it.

Bayes in practice: if 2% of applicants default and the model catches 90% of defaulters with a 10% false-alarm rate, then of every 1,000 applicants, about 18 defaulters are flagged and about 98 good applicants are flagged wrongly. Most flagged applicants are not defaulters, which is why the bank uses the score to prioritise review, not to reject automatically.

## Common mistakes

- **Trying to master all the maths first.** Learn intuition, then deepen as needed.
- **Ignoring shapes.** Most matrix bugs are shape mismatches; print shapes.
- **Learning rate guesses.** Watch the loss curve; adjust.
- **Unscaled features.** Standardise for gradient-based models.
- **Ignoring base rates.** Rare events plus imperfect models mean many false alarms.
