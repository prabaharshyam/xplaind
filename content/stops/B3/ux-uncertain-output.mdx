## What it is

Traditional software is predictable: the same input gives the same output, and when it is wrong, it is a bug you can fix. AI output varies, and it is sometimes wrong by nature, even when nothing is broken. **UX for uncertain output** is designing for that reality.

The goal is **calibrated trust**: users trust the AI as much as it deserves, case by case.

```flow
dir: LR
Under-trust | users ignore a useful tool after one mistake: wasted value -> Calibrated trust | rely when likely right, check when unsure, recover fast
Calibrated trust -> Over-trust | accept every answer and pass mistakes on: real harm
```

Over-trust has a name: **automation bias**, the habit of accepting a machine's output without checking, which gets stronger when people are busy or the machine usually looks right. The lesson on designing human in the loop covers it in depth. Here, the point is simple: a screen that looks certain trains people to stop checking, and a screen that shows its evidence makes checking quick.

Two research-based guides shape this field. Microsoft's Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction group 18 guidelines by when they apply: **initially**, **during interaction**, **when the system is wrong**, and **over time**. Google's People + AI Guidebook covers similar ground, with a chapter on explainability and trust. This lesson turns them into decisions you can make on a screen, and adds the part designers often skip: where the "this might be wrong" signal actually comes from.

You will follow Divya, a product manager at an insurance company in Chennai, as she redesigns an AI coverage assistant after agents passed wrong answers to customers.

## How it works

### The patterns, by when they apply

The table follows Microsoft's four phases, so you can use it to audit any AI feature from first use to month six.

| Phase | Pattern | What it looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initially | **Set expectations** | "Answers questions about your policy wordings. Does not give legal advice." |
| Initially | **Show it is AI** | A clear label where users decide whether to rely on the answer |
| During interaction | **Show checked sources** | Numbered citations; tapping one shows the exact passage, matching sentence highlighted |
| During interaction | **Signal uncertainty** | "Check this amount" on the specific field, in words and an icon |
| During interaction | **Show progress** | Streaming text, or "checking 3 documents..." |
| When wrong | **Make correction easy** | Editable drafts, one-tap "Wrong answer" with a reason, undo |
| When wrong | **Fail gracefully** | "I could not find a clear answer. Try the underwriting helpdesk." |
| When wrong | **Keep people in control** | Preview before sending; confirm before money, messages or deletions |
| Over time | **Learn and tell** | Feedback reviewed weekly; "What's new" when coverage or behaviour changes |

The first two rows are cheap and teams still skip them. The "during interaction" rows only work if the system can tell when it is unsure, which is the next section.

### Where the uncertainty signal comes from

A designer can draw a yellow "please check" badge in a minute. An engineer then has to decide when to show it. If nobody decides, the badge either appears on everything (and users learn to ignore it) or on nothing. These are the signals teams actually use:

| Signal | How it works | Catches | Misses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Retrieval score | The best-matching passage scores below a threshold you tune on labelled questions | Questions the documents do not cover | Passages that look similar but are wrong, for example another plan's clause |
| Citation support check | Does the cited passage contain the answer's amount, date or yes or no? Exact match for numbers; a second checking step for wording | Invented numbers, citations that do not back the claim | Claims that are paraphrased too loosely to match |
| Rule and schema checks | The amount is in an allowed range, the plan matches the customer's plan, required fields are present | Impossible or mismatched values | Plausible but wrong values |
| Agreement across runs | Ask the same question two or three times, or ask two models; flag when the key fields disagree | Unstable, guessy answers | Answers that are wrong the same way every time |
| Self-reported confidence | Ask the model "how sure are you?" | Little on its own | Usually overconfident |

The last row is the trap. A 2024 study by Xiong and colleagues found that language models asked to state their confidence were highly overconfident, with values mostly between 80% and 100%. Methods that compare several answers did better. Treat a model's own confidence as weak evidence, never as the only signal.

Combine signals into a small set of display modes, decided in code, not left to the model:

```flow
title: From signals to what the user sees
Question -> Retrieve passages
Retrieve passages -[top score below threshold]-> No answer | say so and offer the helpdesk
Retrieve passages -[good match]-> Draft answer
Draft answer -[amount not in cited clause]-> Answer with flag | Check this amount
Draft answer -[all checks pass]-> Answer with sources
```

A minimal version of that decision for amounts looks like this. The threshold of 0.6 is an illustration; you set yours by scoring a few hundred labelled questions and choosing where weak matches start.

```python
import re

def numbers(text):
    return set(re.findall(r"\d[\d,]*", text))

def display_mode(answer, cited_passage, top_score, threshold=0.6):
    if top_score < threshold:
        return "no_answer"          # say so and offer the helpdesk
    if not numbers(answer) <= numbers(cited_passage):
        return "check_amount"       # an amount is not in the cited clause
    return "answer"                 # show with sources

clause = "Room rent is covered up to Rs 5,000 per day."
print(display_mode("Room rent is covered up to Rs 5,000 per day.", clause, 0.82))  # answer
print(display_mode("Room rent is covered up to Rs 7,500 per day.", clause, 0.82))  # check_amount
print(display_mode("Dental implants are covered.", clause, 0.41))                  # no_answer
```

Real systems also normalise "5000", "5,000" and "five thousand", but the shape stays the same: cheap checks, decided before the screen is drawn.

### Check citations before you show them

Citations are the strongest pattern here, and they can backfire. In a 2025 study by Ding and colleagues, people trusted chatbot answers more when citations were present, even when the citations were chosen at random. Trust only fell when people actually checked them. So a citation that does not support the answer is worse than none: it makes a wrong answer look verified.

Three rules follow:

- **Verify before showing.** Run the citation support check. If the cited passage does not contain the key claim, drop the citation and flag the claim.
- **Link to the passage, not the document.** "Policy wording, page 14" makes checking slow. Open the clause with the matching sentence highlighted.
- **One good citation beats five.** Ding and colleagues found one citation raised trust about as much as five. Extra links add clutter without adding checking.

### Say it in words, not percentages or colour alone

Precise numbers like "93.7% confident" sound scientific and invite misplaced trust, and the number behind them is rarely well calibrated. Users also cannot act on a percentage: is 81% good enough to tell a customer? Better options:

- put the flag on the specific field: "Check this amount", not a vague banner on the whole answer;
- use plain words tied to an action: "I am not sure about this limit. Confirm in clause 4.2";
- change behaviour when signals are weak: do not answer, and point to a person.

Do not rely on colour alone. Colour-blind users may not see a red or yellow border, and WCAG 2.1, the accessibility standard, says colour must not be the only way information is shown. Pair the colour with an icon and words, and make sure a screen reader announces "needs checking". In a voice product, say it: "I am not certain about this amount."

### Match friction to stakes

Every check you add costs a few seconds. Put a confirm step on everything and people click through it without reading, which is automation bias again. Put friction where mistakes cost the most:

| Stakes | Example | Design |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low | Summary of a call for the agent's own notes | AI label and easy edit; no forced checks |
| Medium | A coverage answer an agent repeats to a customer | Checked source on every answer, highlighted amounts and exclusions, "check before advising" |
| High | A claim rejection, a payment, a legal or medical statement | A confirm step that shows the key fields, human approval, or no AI answer at all |

A useful decision rule: ask "if this answer is wrong and nobody notices, who pays and how much?" The bigger the cost, the higher the row.

### Patterns in Indian products

Many Indian users meet AI in WhatsApp chats, voice assistants and regional-language apps, where screens are small and attention is short. The patterns still apply, in compact form. A WhatsApp bot can end an answer with "Source: Policy section 4.2. Reply WRONG if this does not look right." A voice assistant can say "I am not certain. Shall I connect you to an agent?"

Language raises a harder problem. The answer may be in Tamil or Hindi, but the policy wording is often English only, so a Tamil-speaking user cannot check the citation. Show both: the original English clause with the matching sentence highlighted, and a translated excerpt labelled "Machine translation. The English text is the official wording." Keep amounts in digits in both, and run the support check on the English original, because a translation can quietly change a number or a "not".

### Tell people it is AI: the rules

Labelling is becoming a legal expectation, not just good design. The EU AI Act's Article 50 requires AI systems that talk with people to be designed so people know they are dealing with AI, unless it is obvious, from 2 August 2026. That matters to Indian companies serving EU users. In India, the Reserve Bank's FREE-AI committee report of August 2025 recommends that financial firms make customers aware when they are dealing with AI. It is a committee report, so check RBI's current directions before treating it as a binding rule.

### Design for the first mistake

Users decide early whether to trust a feature. If the first mistake comes from a tool that looked certain, trust collapses and many never return. If the tool showed its limits and sources from the start, the first mistake costs much less: the user caught it, and the product helped them do so.

### Test with planted errors

Usability tests usually check whether users can complete a task. For AI features, also plant answers that are **deliberately wrong**, and watch whether users notice and recover. Count in one unit across all participants, so versions can be compared.

```text
  Test plan for an AI answer screen
  Per participant: 8 questions, 6 correct AI answers, 2 planted errors
  4 participants:  8 planted errors in total
  Measure:         planted errors caught (out of 8), seconds to catch,
                   did they know what to do next?
```

With four people, a test like this finds big design problems. It does not prove a small improvement, so confirm with production numbers.

### Measure trust after launch

Calibration drifts. People who checked carefully in week one may rubber-stamp by week six. Track three numbers, split by stakes:

| Metric | Worrying pattern |
| --- | --- |
| Citation open rate on high-stakes answers | Near zero while the accept rate stays very high: people stopped checking |
| Edit or override rate on flagged fields | Flagged fields accepted unchanged, in seconds: the flags are being ignored |
| Wrong-answer reports per 100 answers | Falling while customer complaints about AI answers rise: people stopped reporting, errors did not stop |

None of these is accuracy. Pair them with a weekly expert audit of a random sample of answers, so you know the real error rate the trust should match.

## A worked example

An insurance company in Chennai adds an AI assistant to its agent app, to answer policy coverage questions while agents talk to customers. The numbers below are illustrative, from a small usability test of the kind described above, not measured production results.

**Version one** shows a confident paragraph answer, with no sources, no limits and no way to report problems. Four agents each answered 8 role-play questions, 2 of them with planted wrong answers. Agents rarely checked: **0 of 8** planted errors were caught, and all 8 were passed to the "customer".

**Version two**, redesigned by Divya, the product manager:

| Change | What the agent now sees | Signal behind it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expectations | Header: "AI answers from policy wordings. Check before advising customers." | None needed |
| Checked sources | One citation per claim; tapping opens the clause, matching sentence highlighted | Citation support check |
| Uncertainty | "Check this amount" with an icon on amounts and exclusions not found in the clause | Number match against the cited clause |
| Graceful failure | "I could not find a clear answer" and a button for the underwriting helpdesk | Retrieval score below the tuned threshold |
| Plan match | Answers only from the wording of the customer's own plan variant | Rule check on plan code |
| Correction | A "Wrong answer" button with three reasons, reviewed weekly | User feedback |

Four different agents took the same test. They caught **7 of 8** planted errors, and their satisfaction rose from 3.1 to 4.2 out of 5. Agents trusted version two **more**, not less, because they could see why each answer was given and fix it when it was not.

The one missed error is the useful part. The answer said a procedure was covered and cited a real clause that did say so, but from the premium variant of the plan, not the customer's basic variant. The citation check passed because the text matched. Divya added the plan-match rule above, which is why it is in the table. The lesson: a citation check proves the passage says it, not that the passage applies.

A test with 4 agents and 8 planted errors shows the direction, not the size, of the improvement. Before calling it a win, Divya's team agreed what production must show over the first two months: citation open rate on claim-amount answers staying well above zero, flagged amounts edited at least sometimes, and complaints traced to AI answers not rising while wrong-answer reports fall.

## Common mistakes

- **Hiding that it is AI.** Users calibrate trust on what they know, and in some markets labelling is now a legal duty.
- **Uncertainty badges with no signal behind them.** If nobody decided when the flag shows, it shows always or never.
- **Trusting the model's own confidence.** Self-reported confidence is usually high, right or wrong; use computed checks.
- **Unchecked citations.** A citation that does not support the answer makes a wrong answer look verified.
- **Fake precision.** "93.7% confident" invites misplaced trust and gives users nothing to act on.
- **Colour-only cues.** A red border alone fails colour-blind users and screen readers; add an icon and words.
- **Same friction everywhere.** Confirm steps on low-stakes outputs teach people to click through the high-stakes ones.
- **Hard-to-correct outputs.** Final, uneditable outputs push users to accept mistakes.
- **Testing only success cases.** Plant wrong answers and count how many users catch.
- **Blank failures.** "Something went wrong" with no next step leaves users stuck.

## Practice

Read Microsoft's Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction, linked below, and the PAIR chapter on explainability and trust.

1. **Audit.** Pick an AI feature you use. For each of the four phases (initially, during interaction, when wrong, over time), note one thing it does well and one place it fails.
2. **Find the signal.** For one "please check" moment in your redesign, write down which signal from the table triggers it, and what it would miss.
3. **Sketch.** On paper, sketch a redesigned screen with expectations, a checked source, a field-level flag that does not rely on colour alone, and one-tap correction. Mark its stakes row and say why the friction fits.
4. **Test.** Show your sketch to two people with one correct and one planted wrong answer. Record how many planted errors they catch and how long it takes.

**You are done when you can** show a design where a user notices and recovers from a planted wrong answer within seconds, and name the computed signal that made the screen flag it.
