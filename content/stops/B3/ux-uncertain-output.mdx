## What it is

Traditional software is predictable: the same input gives the same output. AI output varies and is sometimes wrong. **UX for uncertain output** designs for that reality. The goal is **calibrated trust**: users rely on the AI when it is likely right, check it when it might be wrong, and can recover easily when it is wrong.

Research-based guidelines (such as Microsoft's Human-AI Interaction guidelines and Google's People + AI Guidebook) agree on a core set of patterns.

## How it works

**Set expectations up front.** Say clearly what the feature can and cannot do: "Answers questions about your policy documents. Does not give legal advice." Show it as AI where users would want to know.

**Show your sources.** Link answers to the documents or data they came from, with the quoted passage. Users can check, and wrong answers become obvious.

**Signal confidence carefully.** Where the system has a reliable signal, show it ("Low confidence: please check the amount"). Highlight uncertain fields instead of showing a misleading percentage.

**Make correction easy.** Editable drafts instead of final outputs; one-click "this is wrong" with a reason; easy undo. Corrections should improve the system over time.

**Keep people in control of consequences.** Preview before sending, confirm before acting, especially for money, messages and deletions.

**Fail gracefully.** When the AI cannot help, say so, and offer the next step (a person, a form, a search). Never leave a blank or a confident guess.

**Design for the first mistake.** Users decide early whether to trust a feature. Showing limits, sources and easy correction from the start means the first mistake costs less trust.

**Speed and streaming.** Show progress for slow answers (streaming text, "checking 3 documents..."). Waiting with no feedback feels broken.

**Test with real users and real errors.** Usability tests should include cases where the AI is wrong, to see whether users notice and recover.

## A worked example

A Chennai insurance company adds an AI assistant to its agent app to answer policy coverage questions.

Version one shows a confident paragraph answer. In testing, agents rarely check it, and two of eight test answers that were deliberately wrong were passed to customers.

Version two, redesigned with the PM, Divya:

- A header line: "AI answers from policy wordings. Check before advising customers."
- Every answer has numbered citations; tapping one shows the exact clause.
- When retrieval finds weak matches, the answer starts with "I could not find a clear answer" and suggests the underwriting helpdesk.
- Amounts and exclusions are highlighted for agents to confirm.
- A "Wrong answer" button with three reasons sends cases to the product team weekly.

In the next test, agents catch seven of eight wrong answers, and satisfaction scores rise because agents feel in control. Usage grows rather than falls after the first mistakes.

## Common mistakes

- **Hiding that it is AI.** Users calibrate trust on what they know.
- **Confident answers without sources.** Users cannot check and over-trust.
- **Fake precision.** "93.7% confident" invites misplaced trust; use plain signals.
- **Hard-to-correct outputs.** Final outputs push users to accept mistakes.
- **Testing only success cases.** Test what users do when the AI is wrong.

## Practise

Read Microsoft's Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction and the PAIR chapter on explainability and trust.

1. Pick an AI feature you use and check it against five of the guidelines. Note where it fails.
2. Sketch (on paper is fine) a redesigned screen with expectations, sources, uncertainty signals and easy correction.
3. Test your sketch with two people using one correct and one wrong AI answer, and note whether they spot the wrong one.

**You are done when you can** show a design where a user notices and recovers from a wrong AI answer within seconds.
