## What it is

Traditional software is predictable: the same input gives the same output, and when it is wrong, it is a bug. AI output varies, and it is sometimes wrong by nature. **UX for uncertain output** is designing for that reality.

The goal is **calibrated trust**:

```text
   Under-trust                 Calibrated trust                 Over-trust
 +---------------+         +-----------------------+        +----------------+
 | users ignore  |         | rely when likely      |        | accept every   |
 | a useful tool |         | right, check when     |        | answer, pass   |
 | after one     |         | unsure, recover fast  |        | mistakes to    |
 | mistake       |         | when wrong            |        | customers      |
 +---------------+         +-----------------------+        +----------------+
      wasted value                 the goal                     real harm
```

Research-based guidelines, such as Microsoft's Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction and Google's People + AI Guidebook, agree on a core set of patterns. This lesson turns them into decisions you can make on a screen.

You will follow Divya, a product manager at an insurance company in Chennai, as she redesigns an AI coverage assistant after agents passed wrong answers to customers. By the end you will design a screen where users notice and recover from a wrong AI answer within seconds.

## How it works

### The patterns

| Pattern | What it looks like | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Set expectations** | "Answers questions about your policy documents. Does not give legal advice." | Users know what to rely on it for |
| **Show it is AI** | A clear label where users would want to know | Users adjust how carefully they check |
| **Show sources** | Numbered citations; tapping one shows the exact passage | Users can check, and wrong answers stand out |
| **Signal uncertainty** | "Low confidence: please check the amount"; highlighted fields | Attention goes where it is needed |
| **Make correction easy** | Editable drafts, one-click "this is wrong" with a reason, undo | Mistakes are cheap to fix, and feedback improves the system |
| **Keep people in control** | Preview before sending; confirm before money, messages, deletions | Consequences need a human decision |
| **Fail gracefully** | "I could not find a clear answer. Try the underwriting helpdesk." | No blank screens and no confident guesses |
| **Show progress** | Streaming text; "checking 3 documents..." | Waiting without feedback feels broken |

### Signal confidence carefully

Precise numbers like "93.7% confident" sound scientific and invite misplaced trust, especially when the number is not well calibrated. Better options:

- highlight the specific fields the system is unsure about;
- use plain words: "I'm not sure about this amount";
- change behaviour, for example refusing to answer and pointing to a person, when retrieval is weak.

### Patterns in Indian products

Many Indian users meet AI in WhatsApp chats, voice assistants and regional-language apps, where screens are small and attention is short. The patterns still apply, in compact form. A WhatsApp bot can end an answer with "Source: Policy section 4.2. Reply WRONG if this does not look right." A voice assistant can say "I'm not certain. Shall I connect you to an agent?" A regional-language app can show the source passage in the same language as the answer. Small screens make sources and easy correction more important, not less, because users cannot see much context at once.

### Design for the first mistake

Users decide early whether to trust a feature. If the first mistake comes from a tool that looked certain, trust collapses. If the tool showed its limits and sources from the start, the first mistake costs much less: the user caught it, and the product helped them do so.

### Test with real errors

Usability tests usually check whether users can complete a task. For AI features, also include cases where the AI is **deliberately wrong**, and watch whether users notice and recover. That is the real test of the design.

```text
  Test plan for an AI answer screen
  8 questions:  6 with correct AI answers, 2 with planted errors
  Measure:      did the user spot the error?  how long did it take?
                did they know what to do next?
```

## A worked example

An insurance company in Chennai adds an AI assistant to its agent app, to answer policy coverage questions while agents talk to customers.

**Version one** shows a confident paragraph answer, with no sources, no limits and no way to report problems. In a usability test with eight questions, two answers were deliberately wrong. Agents rarely checked, and **both** wrong answers were passed to customers in the role-play.

**Version two**, redesigned by Divya, the product manager:

| Change | What the agent now sees |
| --- | --- |
| Expectations | A header line: "AI answers from policy wordings. Check before advising customers." |
| Sources | Every answer has numbered citations; tapping one shows the exact clause |
| Graceful failure | When retrieval finds only weak matches, the answer begins "I could not find a clear answer" and suggests the underwriting helpdesk |
| Uncertainty | Amounts and exclusions are highlighted for the agent to confirm |
| Correction | A "Wrong answer" button with three reasons, reviewed by the product team weekly |

**The next test:**

| Measure | Version one | Version two |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Planted wrong answers caught by agents | 0 of 2 per session on average | 7 of 8 across sessions |
| Agents' satisfaction score | 3.1 of 5 | 4.2 of 5 |
| Usage after the first real mistake | Dropped | Grew |

Agents trusted version two **more**, not less, because they felt in control and could see why an answer was given. After the first real mistakes in production, usage kept growing instead of falling.

## Common mistakes

- **Hiding that it is AI.** Users calibrate trust on what they know.
- **Confident answers without sources.** Users cannot check, so they over-trust.
- **Fake precision.** "93.7% confident" invites misplaced trust; use plain signals.
- **Hard-to-correct outputs.** Final, uneditable outputs push users to accept mistakes.
- **Testing only success cases.** Test what users do when the AI is wrong.
- **Blank failures.** "Something went wrong" with no next step leaves users stuck.

## Practise

Read Microsoft's Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction, linked below, and the PAIR chapter on explainability and trust.

1. **Audit.** Pick an AI feature you use and check it against five of the guidelines. Note where it fails.
2. **Sketch.** On paper, sketch a redesigned screen with expectations, sources, uncertainty signals and easy correction.
3. **Test.** Show your sketch to two people with one correct and one wrong AI answer, and note whether they spot the wrong one.

**You are done when you can** show a design where a user notices and recovers from a wrong AI answer within seconds.
