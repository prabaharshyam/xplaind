## What it is

An **AI policy** sets the rules for how an organisation uses and builds AI. The lessons on following a company AI policy and using AI safely cover the employee's side: sort your data, use the licensed tool, check the output. This lesson is the other side of the desk. You decide which tools are approved, what data each may see, who signs off on risky uses, and what happens when something goes wrong.

An employee needs to understand a rule. An author needs every rule to pass three tests:

1. **Can a new employee apply it to a real task in a minute?** "Use AI responsibly" fails. "Client code goes only into the IDE assistant, and not for clients on the opt-out list" passes.
2. **Can you defend it to an outsider?** A client's security reviewer, an auditor or, after an incident, a regulator.
3. **Does it leave a legal route?** If the only safe answer is "do not", people find an unsafe one.

A good first policy is **two to four pages**, written for employees rather than lawyers, with a one-page summary. It covers everyone who **uses** AI tools, and the teams who **build** AI features. Here is a template to copy; the rest of the lesson explains each row.

| Section | What you decide | A sensible default | Owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scope and principles | Who it covers; 3 to 5 commitments | All staff and contractors; people stay accountable | Leadership signs |
| Approved tools | Which tools, with which settings | Only tools that passed the checklist, listed by name | IT security |
| Data by tool | Which data class each tool may see | A one-page matrix, plus a client opt-out list | Security and legal |
| Uses | Encouraged, restricted, prohibited | Each rule has a reason and an approved alternative | Policy owner |
| Exceptions | How to ask, how fast you answer | A form, answered in 3 working days, logged | Policy owner |
| Building AI | What must exist before launch | Risk assessment, evaluation, oversight, monitoring | AI review group |
| Disclosure | When to tell people AI was involved | Chatbots say they are AI; realistic AI media is labelled | Product and legal |
| Incidents | What to report, to whom, how fast | To security within 1 hour, no blame for honest reports | Security |
| Training and review | What everyone learns; when the policy changes | 30 minutes with real tasks; review every 6 months | Policy owner |

You will follow Neha, a delivery head in Pune, as a client's security review forces her company's first AI policy. By the end you can draft a policy, test it on real tasks and show whether people follow it.

## How it works

### Step 1: Find out what is already happening

Write nothing until you know what people already use. Unapproved use is called **shadow AI**, and you cannot write a sensible rule about a tool you do not know exists. Four sources, from cheapest to richest:

- **Licence consoles.** If the company already pays for an assistant, its admin page shows how many seats are active.
- **Network and proxy logs.** IT can count visits to known AI websites. You learn which tools, not what was pasted.
- **Expense claims and card statements.** Personal AI subscriptions claimed back are a strong sign of real need.
- **A short anonymous survey.** Which tools, for which tasks, with what kind of data, and what would you need from an approved tool?

Promise in writing that nothing found in discovery is punished, or people hide exactly the use you most need to see.

The result is an **AI inventory**: every AI tool and feature in use, with its owner, the data it sees, the vendor, a risk level and its approval status. Keep it current. It is the first thing a client's security reviewer asks for.

### Step 2: Decide who decides

Name a person for each decision:

| Decision | Who decides | Target turnaround |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Approve a new tool | IT security, with legal for the contract | 2 to 3 weeks |
| Approve a restricted use, such as a client-facing bot | The policy owner, or a small AI review group for high risk | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Grant a one-off exception | The policy owner | 3 working days |
| Decide whether an incident must be reported outside | Security and legal together | Hours, not days |
| Change the policy | The policy owner, signed by leadership | Every 6 months, or after a trigger |

Write these times into the policy. A request that vanishes for a month teaches people to stop asking.

### Step 3: Approve tools with a checklist

"Configured not to train on company data" is one line of a review, not the whole review. Before you approve a tool, get written answers to these:

1. **Training.** Is your data excluded from training by contract, not just by a setting a user can change?
2. **Retention.** How long are prompts, files and outputs kept, and can an admin delete them?
3. **Location.** Where is data stored and processed? Some client contracts require it to stay in India or in named regions.
4. **Access.** Who at the vendor can read your prompts, and which other companies (sub-processors) handle your data?
5. **Admin control.** Single sign-on, removal of leavers, audit logs you can export, and switches to turn off risky features.
6. **Contract.** Confidentiality, a data processing agreement, and how fast the vendor must tell you about a breach.
7. **Hidden data paths.** Web search, plugins, connectors to email or drives, memory and agent modes each send data somewhere new. Approve only what you need.

Record the answers in the inventory and re-review yearly or when the vendor changes its terms.

Then write the **data-by-tool matrix**, using the data classes from the earlier lessons:

| Tool | Public | Internal | Confidential (client) | Personal data |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Enterprise assistant | Yes | Yes | Client documents but not code; not for opt-out clients | No |
| Code assistant in the approved IDE | Yes | Yes | Client code; not for opt-out clients | No |
| AI features built into the HR system | Yes | Yes | No | HR data, for HR purposes only |
| Any other tool, including free ones | Yes | No | No | No |

The **client opt-out list** turns contracts into something an engineer can check: which clients forbid third-party AI processing, or allow it only in certain locations. Legal keeps it; delivery checks it at every project start.

### Step 4: Sort uses by risk and write rules people can apply

| Encouraged | Restricted (needs approval) | Prohibited |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drafting, summarising and research in approved tools | Customer-facing chatbots and AI features | Confidential or personal data in unapproved tools |
| Coding help, with review and a licence check on generated code | Decisions about people, such as hiring, appraisal or credit | Deepfakes of real people made without their consent |
| Translating internal documents | Agents that can act in company systems | Fully automated decisions with legal effects on people |
| Transcribing internal meetings in the approved tool, after telling everyone | Recording external calls, only with the other side's agreement | Secrets, passwords and API keys in any prompt |

Three newer rows need care in 2026:

- **Agents.** An agent that can send email, merge code or move money needs the least access that does the job, human approval before anything hard to undo, and a log of every action. Approving a chat tool does not approve its agent mode.
- **Meeting transcription.** A sensible default: tell everyone at the start, let anyone object, and treat the transcript like the meeting's content.
- **Ownership of output.** Generated code can resemble open-source code under a licence your client forbids. Run the licence scanning you use for any third-party code, and check client contracts on who owns AI-assisted work.

**Disclosure defaults:** chatbots say they are AI in their first message; realistic AI images, audio and video are labelled; AI-drafted client deliverables are labelled when the contract or client asks.

Write each rule in four parts: **rule, reason, approved alternative, example.** For instance: "Do not paste client code into any tool except the IDE assistant. Reason: many client contracts forbid it. Instead: describe the pattern with a made-up snippet. Example: ask how to paginate a REST API without pasting the client's code."

### Step 5: Build in the rules from outside

Your policy cannot be looser than the law or your contracts. This is a summary as of November 2026, not legal advice; have counsel confirm the current text.

| Source | What it requires | What your policy says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Client contracts | Often limit third-party processing, locations or AI use | The client opt-out list, checked at project start |
| CERT-In directions (April 2022) | Listed cyber incidents, including data breaches and data leaks, reported to CERT-In within 6 hours of noticing | Staff report to security within 1 hour; security and legal decide on CERT-In |
| DPDP Act and Rules (notified November 2025) | From May 2027 most business duties apply, including telling affected people about a personal data breach without delay and a detailed report to the Data Protection Board within 72 hours | Personal data only for the stated purpose; the incident route already reaches legal |
| IT Rules amendment (in force 20 February 2026) | Platforms must label AI-made audio, images and video that look real | If you run a platform, legal reviews; everyone else labels such media by default |
| EU AI Act | Applies to non-EU firms when their AI system is offered in the EU or its output is used there. Chatbot disclosure duties apply from 2 August 2026; a 2026 amendment moved duties for high-risk uses such as hiring and credit to 2 December 2027 | If you have EU users or clients, legal maps your uses to the Act |

The classic mistake is an internal deadline longer than an external one. "Report within 24 hours" makes a 6-hour duty impossible. The internal clock must be the shortest.

```flow
dir: LR
title: An AI incident, from noticing to closing
Someone notices a problem -> Report to security within 1 hour | no blame for honest reports
Report to security within 1 hour -> Security and legal triage
Security and legal triage -[listed cyber incident or personal data]-> Outside reports | CERT-In within 6 hours; DPDP and client notices as required
Security and legal triage -[internal only]-> Fix and record
Outside reports -> Fix and record
Fix and record -> Update the policy or the tool settings
```

For principles, use India's AI Governance Guidelines from MeitY (November 2025) and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. The guidelines create no new AI law; they rely on existing laws and sector regulators, so in banking, insurance or health your regulator's rules sit on top.

### Step 6: Set a launch gate for AI features you build

Before an AI feature launches, require a package scaled to the risk: a risk assessment, evaluation results, a human oversight plan, monitoring, and approval by a named group. An internal summariser needs a page. A customer-facing credit assistant needs evaluation for accuracy and fairness, a person who can overrule it, and a plan for when it is wrong.

### Step 7: Launch, measure, review

Launch the policy and the approved tools **on the same day**; a policy without tools reads as a ban. Then:

- **Train** in 30 minutes using real tasks from each team.
- **Measure** monthly: active users of approved tools, traffic to unapproved AI sites, exception requests, incident reports and a three-task quiz.
- **Read the signals.** A few early incident reports mean the route works; zero usually means hidden mistakes.
- **Enforce in proportion.** Coaching for honest mistakes; the HR process for hiding incidents or repeated deliberate breaches. Block unapproved sites only once the approved tool does the job.
- **Review** every six months, and after a new tool, a new law or a serious incident.

## A worked example

This is an illustrative case. The company and the numbers are made up to show the method.

A 300-person software services company in Pune has no AI policy. During a security review, a large client asks: "How is AI use governed at your company, and which tools have seen our code?" Nobody can answer the second half. Neha, the delivery head, gets six weeks to fix that with the CTO and legal counsel.

**Discovery (weeks 1 and 2).** An anonymous survey gets 210 replies; 150 people use AI at work. Proxy logs show four chatbot sites in regular use. Two teams admit pasting client code into personal accounts. Nobody is punished, and legal checks those clients' contracts for any duty to tell them.

**Tool checks (weeks 2 to 4).** The enterprise assistant passes the checklist. The code assistant's vendor keeps prompts for 30 days and processes them outside India. Two clients' contracts require India-only processing, so they go on the opt-out list and their teams use a local code-search tool. The assistant's email connector stays off until reviewed separately.

**One hard request.** A team wants an AI agent that merges its own pull requests. Neha classes it as restricted: the agent may open pull requests and run tests, but a human merges, and it gets no access to production.

**What they wrote** (three pages, signed by the CEO):

| Section | What they wrote |
| --- | --- |
| Approved tools | Enterprise assistant and IDE code assistant, settings listed; connectors off |
| Data by tool | The matrix, plus an opt-out list of 2 clients |
| Restricted | Client-facing AI features need a risk assessment and the client's written approval; agents need least access and human merges |
| Prohibited | Personal AI accounts for client work; secrets in prompts; client or personal data in unapproved tools |
| Incidents | Report to security within 1 hour; security and legal decide on CERT-In and client notices |
| Exceptions and review | A form answered in 3 working days; review every 6 months |

**Two months later:**

| Measure | Before | After | How it was measured |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly active users of approved tools | 40 | 170 | Licence admin console |
| Visits to unapproved chatbot sites | About 900 a week | About 150 a week | Proxy logs |
| Exception requests | None (no route) | 11, 9 approved | Request form |
| Incidents reported | 0 | 2 | Security log |
| Client security review | Open question | Passed, with the inventory attached | Client's report |

The two incidents were a test file with real customer names pasted into the assistant, and a prompt containing a client's API key. The key was rotated within the hour, and security and legal checked both against CERT-In and the client contract the same day. Both surfaced because reporting carried no blame.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing before discovering.** A policy that ignores the tools people already use is ignored back.
- **Copying another company's policy.** Its tools, clients and laws are not yours; take the structure, not the rules.
- **Approving a tool on one setting.** "Does not train on our data" says nothing about retention, location, vendor access or connectors.
- **An internal deadline longer than an external one.** A 24-hour internal rule breaks a 6-hour CERT-In duty.
- **Bans and rules with no route.** People keep using AI on personal accounts, out of sight. Every "do not" needs an approved alternative and an exception route answered in days.
- **Vague words.** "Appropriate", "responsibly" and "sensitive" without examples mean different things to every reader.
- **A document instead of a system.** No owner, a stale inventory and no review date mean the policy is wrong within a year.

## Practice

Read the NIST AI RMF's Govern function, linked below, and the OWASP checklist's section on AI asset inventory. PRS's summary covers the DPDP law as passed in 2023; the 2025 Rules came later. Skim the CERT-In directions and the EU AI Act summary's high-risk uses.

1. **Discover.** For an organisation you know, list five AI tools in use, the data each sees and its owner, and how you would confirm each.
2. **Draft.** Fill in the template from the top of this lesson in two to three pages, including a data-by-tool matrix and an incident route with a 1-hour internal deadline.
3. **Check one tool.** Answer the seven checklist questions for one real AI tool from its public terms, and note which answers you could not find.
4. **Test it.** Give the draft and three real tasks to two people from different teams. Fix every rule they misread.

**You are done when you can** hand your policy to a new employee and they correctly say which tool and data they may use for three tasks, and a client's security reviewer could find every tool that has seen their data.
