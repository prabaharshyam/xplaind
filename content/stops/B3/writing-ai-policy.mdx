## What it is

An **AI policy** sets the rules for how an organisation uses and builds AI. A good first policy is two to four pages, written for employees, and reviewed every six months. It usually has two parts:

- **Using AI tools** (everyone): which tools, which data, what review and disclosure.
- **Building AI features** (product and engineering teams): risk assessment, testing, oversight, monitoring and approval before launch.

## How it works

**Principles.** Three to five short commitments, for example: people stay accountable; protect personal and confidential data; be transparent with customers; test for fairness and quality before launch; report problems. India's AI Governance Guidelines and frameworks such as NIST's AI RMF are good sources for principles.

**Approved tools.** Name them and the version (for example, the company's enterprise licence). State that personal accounts are not to be used for work data.

**Data classes.** Public, internal, confidential, personal. For each, say which tools may process it. Personal data follows the DPDP Act: purpose limits, consent or other lawful grounds, and minimum necessary use.

**Use cases.** Encouraged (drafting, summarising, research, coding assistance with review), restricted (needs approval: customer-facing features, decisions about people such as hiring or credit, medical, legal or financial advice), and prohibited (for example, uploading customer data to unapproved tools, creating deepfakes of real people, fully automated decisions with legal effects on people without review).

**Review and accountability.** The person who uses AI output is responsible for it. Customer-facing and high-risk uses need a named owner and documented review.

**Disclosure.** When to tell customers or readers that AI was used, such as chatbots identifying themselves and AI-assisted reports being labelled where required.

**Building AI features.** A short risk assessment (what could go wrong, who could be harmed), evaluation results, a human oversight plan, monitoring and an incident plan, approved by a named group before launch. Scale the depth to the risk.

**Incidents.** How to report an AI mistake, data leak or harmful output, and to whom, with no blame for honest reports.

**Training and updates.** A short training for all staff; the policy owner reviews it regularly as tools and laws change.

**Write it to be used.** Plain language, examples, a one-page summary and a contact for questions. A policy that only says "do not" drives use underground.

## A worked example

A 300-person Pune software services company has no AI policy; staff use a mix of tools, some with client code. A client asks during a security review how AI is governed.

The PM-turned-delivery head, Neha, drafts a three-page policy with the CTO and legal counsel:

- Principles: accountability, client confidentiality first, transparency, quality and fairness, report problems.
- Approved tools: the company's enterprise AI assistant and the code assistant in the approved IDE, both configured not to train on company data.
- Client code and data: only in approved tools and only where the client contract permits; a list of clients who have opted out.
- Restricted: client-facing AI features need a risk assessment and client approval.
- Prohibited: personal AI accounts for client work; any customer personal data in unapproved tools.
- Incidents: report to the security team within 24 hours, no blame for honest mistakes.

A 30-minute training is given to all staff, and usage of approved tools triples within a month as people learn what is allowed. The client's security review passes.

## Common mistakes

- **Banning AI outright.** People use it anyway, unsafely.
- **Long legal documents nobody reads.** Write plainly with examples.
- **Ignoring client contracts.** Many restrict how client data may be processed.
- **No owner or review date.** Tools and laws change fast.
- **No incident route.** Mistakes then stay hidden.

## Practise

Skim the NIST AI RMF's govern function, the PRS summary of the DPDP Act and the EU AI Act summary's list of prohibited and high-risk uses.

1. Draft a two-page AI policy for an organisation you know, with every section above.
2. Write a one-page summary for employees with five dos and five don'ts.
3. Ask two people from different teams to read it and tell you one thing that is unclear; fix it.

**You are done when you can** hand your policy to a new employee and have them correctly say which tool and data they may use for three example tasks.
