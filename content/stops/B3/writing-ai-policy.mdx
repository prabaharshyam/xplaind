## What it is

An **AI policy** sets the rules for how an organisation uses and builds AI. Without one, people guess: some avoid AI completely and fall behind, others paste client data into free tools. A good policy makes the safe path the easy path.

A good first policy is **two to four pages**, written for employees rather than lawyers, and reviewed every six months. It usually has two parts:

| Part | Who it applies to | What it covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Using AI tools** | Everyone | Which tools, which data, what review and disclosure |
| **Building AI features** | Product and engineering teams | Risk assessment, testing, oversight, monitoring and approval before launch |

```text
                  An AI policy at a glance
 +---------------------------------------------------------+
 | Principles      3-5 short commitments                    |
 | Approved tools  named, with settings                     |
 | Data classes    public / internal / confidential /       |
 |                 personal -> which tools may see each     |
 | Use cases       encouraged / restricted / prohibited     |
 | Review          who is accountable, what gets reviewed   |
 | Disclosure      when to tell customers AI was used       |
 | Building AI     risk assessment -> evals -> approval     |
 | Incidents       how to report, no blame                  |
 | Training        30 minutes for everyone, regular updates |
 +---------------------------------------------------------+
```

This lesson follows Neha, delivery head at a software services company in Pune, as a client's security review forces the company to write its first AI policy. By the end you will draft a practical policy that a new employee can apply to real tasks.

## How it works

### Principles

Three to five short commitments that everything else follows from, for example:

1. People stay accountable for AI-assisted work.
2. Protect personal and confidential data, including clients'.
3. Be transparent with customers when AI is involved.
4. Test for quality and fairness before launch.
5. Report problems quickly, without blame.

India's AI Governance Guidelines and frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework are good sources for principles.

### Approved tools

Name the approved tools and how they are configured, for example "the company's enterprise AI assistant, configured not to train on company data". State plainly that personal accounts are not to be used for work data.

### Data classes

| Class | Examples | Which tools may process it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Public | Published material, public circulars | Any tool |
| Internal | Process notes, internal templates | Approved tools only |
| Confidential | Client code, contracts, unreleased results | Approved tools only, and only where client contracts allow |
| Personal | Customer or employee personal data | Only specifically approved systems; follows the DPDP Act |

Personal data follows the **DPDP Act**: use it only for the purposes people were told about, on a lawful ground such as consent, and no more than necessary.

### Use cases

| Encouraged | Restricted (needs approval) | Prohibited |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drafting, summarising, research | Customer-facing AI features | Customer data in unapproved tools |
| Coding assistance, with review | Decisions about people, such as hiring or credit | Deepfakes of real people |
| Translating internal documents | Medical, legal or financial advice | Fully automated decisions with legal effects on people, with no review |

For each prohibited use, point to the allowed alternative where there is one. A policy that only says "do not" drives use underground.

### Review and accountability

The person who uses AI output is responsible for it. Customer-facing and high-risk uses need a **named owner** and a documented review.

### Disclosure

Say when to tell customers or readers that AI was used: chatbots identify themselves as AI, and AI-assisted reports are labelled where clients or rules require it.

### Building AI features

Before launch, a short package scaled to the risk:

```text
  Idea --> risk assessment --> evaluation results --> oversight plan
       --> monitoring and incident plan --> approval by a named group --> launch
```

A low-risk internal summariser needs a page; a customer-facing credit assistant needs much more.

### Incidents

How to report an AI mistake, data leak or harmful output, to whom, and how fast, for example "to the security team within 24 hours", with **no blame for honest reports**. Hidden mistakes are the expensive ones.

### Training and updates

A short training for all staff, a named policy owner, and a review date. Tools and laws change fast; a policy that is two years old is probably wrong.

## A worked example

A 300-person software services company in Pune has no AI policy. Staff use a mix of tools, some with client code. During a security review, a large client asks: "How is AI use governed at your company?" Nobody has a good answer.

Neha, the delivery head, drafts a three-page policy with the CTO and legal counsel in two weeks:

| Section | What they wrote |
| --- | --- |
| Principles | Accountability, client confidentiality first, transparency, quality and fairness, report problems |
| Approved tools | The enterprise AI assistant and the code assistant in the approved IDE, both configured not to train on company data |
| Client code and data | Only in approved tools, and only where the client contract permits; a list of clients who have opted out |
| Restricted | Client-facing AI features need a risk assessment and the client's approval |
| Prohibited | Personal AI accounts for client work; any customer personal data in unapproved tools |
| Incidents | Report to the security team within 24 hours; no blame for honest mistakes |
| Training | 30 minutes for all staff; policy owner reviews every six months |

**What happened next:**

| Measure | Before the policy | One month after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Use of approved AI tools | Low; many on personal accounts | About three times higher |
| Client security review | Open question | Passed |
| Incidents reported | None reported, several suspected | Two reported and fixed quickly |

Usage of approved tools tripled because people finally knew what was allowed. The two reported incidents, a test file with real names and a prompt with a client's API key, were caught within a day, because reporting carried no blame.

## Common mistakes

- **Banning AI outright.** People use it anyway, unsafely and out of sight.
- **Long legal documents nobody reads.** Write plainly, with examples and a one-page summary.
- **Ignoring client contracts.** Many restrict how client data may be processed.
- **No owner or review date.** Tools and laws change fast.
- **No incident route.** Mistakes then stay hidden.
- **Only "don'ts".** Always point to the approved way of doing the task.

## Practise

Skim the NIST AI RMF's govern function, linked below, PRS's summary of the DPDP Act, and the EU AI Act summary's list of prohibited and high-risk uses.

1. **Draft.** Write a two-page AI policy for an organisation you know, with every section above.
2. **Summarise.** Write a one-page summary for employees with five dos and five don'ts.
3. **Test it.** Ask two people from different teams to read it and tell you one thing that is unclear; fix it.

**You are done when you can** hand your policy to a new employee and have them correctly say which tool and data they may use for three example tasks.
