## What it is

An **LLM judge** is a language model asked to grade another model's output: "Is this answer faithful to the passages? Answer yes or no with a reason", or "Rate this reply from 1 to 5 against this rubric". Judges make it possible to evaluate open-ended outputs (summaries, explanations, replies) at scale, where code checks cannot.

But a judge is itself a model, with errors and biases. It must be checked before its scores are trusted.

## How it works

**Write a narrow, specific rubric.** Judges do better on one clear question at a time ("Does the answer contain any claim not supported by the passages?") than on vague scales ("How good is this answer, 1 to 10?"). Prefer yes or no, or a small labelled scale, and ask for a short reason first.

**Give the judge what it needs.** The question, the output, and the reference: retrieved passages for faithfulness, the expected answer for correctness, the policy for compliance.

**Check agreement with people.** Have one or two people grade 30 to 100 outputs with the same rubric. Compare with the judge. If agreement is low, read the disagreements and fix the rubric or examples. Recheck after changing the judge's prompt or model.

**Known biases.**

- *Position bias*: when comparing two answers, judges often favour the first (or second) shown. Swap positions and average.
- *Length bias*: longer, more detailed answers tend to get higher scores even when not better.
- *Self-preference*: models may rate outputs from their own family higher.
- *Leniency*: judges are often too generous; calibrate with clearly bad examples.

**Use code when code can decide.** Exact values, JSON validity, required fields, word limits, presence of a citation, banned phrases: these are better checked by code, which is free, fast and always consistent.

**Cost.** Judges add model calls per case. Use smaller models where agreement stays high, and run judges offline or on samples.

## A worked example

A Chennai legal-tech startup summarises court judgments. They want to measure whether summaries contain unsupported claims.

- Judge prompt: "Here is a judgment excerpt and a summary. List any statement in the summary not supported by the excerpt. Then answer SUPPORTED or UNSUPPORTED."
- Two lawyers grade 80 summaries. The judge agrees with them on 74 (92.5%).
- In a separate comparison task (which of two summaries is better), the judge picked whichever was shown first 63% of the time. They switch to showing both orders and only counting consistent verdicts.
- They also find the judge rated longer summaries higher. They add a code check for a 150-word limit and a rubric line saying length is not a quality.

The faithfulness judge now runs on every model or prompt change, and lawyers spot-check 20 cases a month.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting a judge before checking agreement with people.**
- **Vague 1-to-10 scales.**
- **Ignoring position and length bias.**
- **Using a judge for things code can check.**
- **Never rechecking after changing the judge.**
