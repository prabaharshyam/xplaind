## What it is

Three numbers describe how big a language model is and how much went into it.

1. **Parameters**: the numbers inside the model that training adjusts. "Llama 3 8B" has about 8 billion. More parameters give a model more capacity to learn, and need more memory and compute to run.
2. **Training tokens**: how much text it learned from. A token is a piece of a word. Meta's models went from 1 to 1.4 trillion tokens (Llama 1) to 2 trillion (Llama 2) to over 15 trillion (Llama 3).
3. **Training compute**: how many arithmetic operations training took, counted in FLOPs (floating-point operations). A common estimate is **6 × parameters × training tokens**.

A **scaling law** is a rule, found by experiment, for how a model's loss (its error at predicting the next token) falls as these numbers grow. The striking finding is that the fall is smooth and predictable across many sizes. So a lab can train a series of small models, fit a curve, and forecast how a much larger one will do before spending the money.

Since 2024 a third axis has joined model size and training data: **compute at answer time**. A reasoning model spends extra tokens thinking before it answers, and that can beat simply making the model bigger on some problems.

```flow
title: What decides how good a model is
Training budget -> Model size | parameters
Training budget -> Training data | tokens, quality, variety
Model size -> Quality
Training data -> Quality
Thinking at answer time -> Quality
Model size -> Running cost | memory, speed per token
Quality -> Pick the smallest setup that meets your bar
Running cost -> Pick the smallest setup that meets your bar
```

## How it works

### Size and memory

To run a model, its weights must fit in GPU memory: parameters times bytes per parameter. At 16-bit precision (2 bytes), a 7B model needs about 14 GB and a 70B model about 140 GB. That is before the KV cache (the stored attention state for each conversation, which grows with context length and with the number of users) and other overhead. Quantisation to 8 or 4 bits shrinks the weights, as the GPU lesson explains.

### Dense and sparse models

In a **dense** model every parameter works on every token. A **mixture-of-experts** (MoE) model splits some layers into many "experts", and a small router picks a few for each token. Mixtral 8x7B has 46.7 billion parameters in total but uses 12.9 billion per token. Qwen3-30B-A3B has about 30.5 billion in total and 3.3 billion active.

The rule to remember: **compute and speed follow the active parameters; memory follows the total.** Qwen3-30B-A3B does about as much arithmetic per token as a 3B dense model, but at 16-bit it still needs about 61 GB just to hold its weights.

### Counting compute

Why 6 × parameters × tokens? For each token, each parameter takes part in one multiply and one add in the forward pass: about 2 operations. The backward pass, which works out how to change each weight, costs about twice the forward pass: 4 more. So training costs about 6 operations per parameter per token, and generating one token at answer time costs about 2 per parameter.

The rule has limits. It ignores the attention calculation, which grows with context length and matters for long-context training. For an MoE model, use the **active** parameters. And it gives the arithmetic only: real runs lose time to communication between GPUs, restarts and failed experiments.

### Kaplan (2020): bigger models first

Jared Kaplan and colleagues at OpenAI showed in 2020 that loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters, data and compute, across several orders of magnitude. Their fit also said how to spend a growing budget: the best model size grew roughly as compute to the power 0.73. In other words, ten times more compute meant a model about five times bigger and only about twice as much data. This advice shaped the next two years: GPT-3 (175 billion parameters) and DeepMind's Gopher (280 billion) were both trained on about 300 billion tokens.

### Chinchilla (2022): grow size and data together

Jordan Hoffmann and colleagues at DeepMind trained over 400 models, from about 70 million to over 16 billion parameters, on 5 to 500 billion tokens. They asked: for a fixed compute budget, which mix of size and data gives the lowest loss? Their answer: **grow both equally**. Double the parameters, double the tokens. Their results work out to a rule of thumb of about **20 training tokens per parameter**.

To test it, they trained Chinchilla: 70 billion parameters on 1.4 trillion tokens, using the same compute as Gopher. Chinchilla beat Gopher, GPT-3, Jurassic-1 (178 billion) and Megatron-Turing NLG (530 billion) on a wide range of tasks. It scored 67.5% on the MMLU knowledge benchmark, more than 7 points above Gopher. The big models of 2020 and 2021 had been undertrained: GPT-3 saw under 2 tokens per parameter.

**Why did Kaplan and Chinchilla disagree?** It is a classic interview question. Hoffmann's team suggested that Kaplan's runs used a learning-rate schedule not matched to each run's length. Later work tested that idea. Porian and colleagues (2024) found the schedule was not the cause. They traced the gap to three choices in Kaplan's setup: not counting the last layer's compute, a fixed warm-up period too long for the small models, and optimisation settings not tuned for each size. Pearce and Song (2024) blamed the non-embedding count, which matters most at the small sizes Kaplan studied. The lesson for practice: how you count and tune can change what a scaling law tells you.

One more gotcha. In 2024, Besiroglu and colleagues at Epoch AI tried to replicate one of Chinchilla's three fitting methods. They found the published formula was poorly fitted and its constants were rounded in a way that biased its predictions. Their refit agreed with the paper's other two methods. The 20-tokens rule survived; the exact constants in the formula should not be trusted.

### Beyond Chinchilla: paying for use, not just training

Chinchilla minimises the cost of **training**. But a popular model is **used** millions of times, and every reply costs about 2 operations per parameter per token. A smaller model is cheaper for every user, forever.

Sardana, Portes, Doubov and Frankle (ICML 2024) added that running cost to the Chinchilla analysis. They found that a lab expecting large demand, around a billion requests, should train a smaller model for longer than Chinchilla suggests, up to thousands of tokens per parameter. In their experiments, models kept improving with more data at ratios up to 10,000 tokens per parameter. Llama 3 8B, trained on over 15 trillion tokens (about 1,900 per parameter), fits this pattern.

### Running out of data

Training sets have grown faster than the supply of good public text, and many languages have very little. **Repeating data** helps up to a point. Muennighoff and colleagues (NeurIPS 2023) ran about 400 training runs, with models of up to 9 billion parameters. They found that up to about **4 passes (epochs)** over the same data was almost as good as fresh data. Beyond that the value of each repeat fell quickly, and with enough repeats extra compute added almost nothing.

For Indian languages the shortage is sharp. In Common Crawl's November 2026 crawl (CC-MAIN-2026-47), Kannada was the main language of about 0.015% of pages, against 42.7% for English. That is pages, not tokens, but it gives the scale: for every Kannada page there are about 2,800 English ones.

### Diminishing returns

A power law means each step costs more than the last. For example, suppose the part of the loss that scale can remove falls as compute to the power −0.05 (an illustrative exponent in the range early studies reported). Then 10 times more compute removes only about 11% of it, and 100 times more removes about 21%. The fitted laws also include a floor: some error stays however big you go. On tasks the same pattern shows up: moving from 90% to 95% accuracy can cost far more than moving from 60% to 90%.

### A third axis: compute at answer time

In 2024 OpenAI reported that its o1 model got steadily better with more reinforcement-learning compute during training and with more time spent thinking at answer time. Snell, Lee, Xu and Kumar (ICLR 2025) studied this on maths problems. Choosing the answer-time strategy by question difficulty was over 4 times more efficient than simply sampling many answers and keeping the best. In a comparison at equal total compute, a small model with extra thinking beat a model about 14 times larger, but only on problems the small model could already sometimes solve.

The decision rule: extra thinking helps when the model nearly knows how to solve the problem. It cannot supply knowledge the model never learned; for that you need a bigger or better-trained model. The reasoning-models lesson covers this in depth.

### A quick calculator

```python
A100_BF16 = 312e12   # NVIDIA A100 peak at 16-bit (BF16), dense, operations per second
H100_BF16 = 989e12   # NVIDIA H100 SXM peak at 16-bit (BF16), dense

def weights_gb(params_billion, bytes_per_param=2):
    return params_billion * bytes_per_param          # 1B params x 2 bytes = 2 GB

def training_flops(params, tokens):
    return 6 * params * tokens                       # for MoE, pass ACTIVE params

def gpu_hours(flops, gpu_peak=A100_BF16, utilisation=0.4):
    return flops / (gpu_peak * utilisation) / 3600   # utilisation is an assumption

print(weights_gb(8))                                 # 16: an 8B model at 16-bit
print(weights_gb(30.5), weights_gb(30.5, 0.5))       # 61.0 15.25: MoE memory uses TOTAL params
print(round(gpu_hours(training_flops(1e9, 20e9))))   # 267 A100-hours: 1B model, Chinchilla point
print(round(gpu_hours(training_flops(8e9, 15e12), H100_BF16)))  # 505561 H100-hours: Llama 3 8B
```

The 40% utilisation is an assumption, not a measured figure. Check the last line against reality: Meta's model card reports about **1.3 million** H100-hours for Llama 3 8B, about 2.6 times the estimate. So treat 6 × parameters × tokens as a lower bound that is right to within a few times, not as a quote.

## A worked example

A startup in Bengaluru wants a model that writes well in Kannada. A founder proposes pre-training a 1-billion-parameter model from scratch. They have about 3 billion tokens of good Kannada text, counted with a tokenizer built for Kannada.

They do the arithmetic:

1. **Data needed.** Chinchilla suggests about 20 billion tokens for 1B parameters, nearly seven times what they have.
2. **Compute.** 6 × 1 billion × 20 billion = 1.2 × 10²⁰ operations. On the calculator, that is about 270 A100-hours at an assumed 40% utilisation, or a day and a half on eight GPUs. Even at three times the estimate, it is affordable.
3. **Repeats.** Up to four passes over their text is nearly as good as fresh data, which gives about 12 billion useful tokens. Reaching 20 billion means about seven passes, where the returns are already falling. And a 1B model starts with no general knowledge at all.
4. **The tokenizer.** The tokenizer splits text into tokens, and English-centred tokenizers break Kannada into many small pieces. We measured this on the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, using the whole declaration in English and Kannada:

| Tokenizer | English tokens | Kannada tokens | Kannada vs English |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| cl100k_base (GPT-4; Llama 3's gave the same Kannada count) | 1,975 | 18,638 | about 9.4 times |
| o200k_base (GPT-4o) | 1,977 | 4,306 | about 2.2 times |

This is one short legal text, so treat the ratios as rough. Still, the gap is large. With a Llama 3-style tokenizer, every Kannada reply needs about four times as many tokens as with o200k_base. That means about four times the generation time and cost per reply, and a context window that holds a quarter as much Kannada. Continued training on their text also costs more, because the same text becomes more tokens. Sarvam AI reports that its Sarvam-1 tokenizer needs 1.4 to 2.1 tokens per word across its supported languages, Kannada among them, against 4 to 8 for existing multilingual models (the company's own figures).

They compare three plans on 200 real Kannada writing tasks:

| Plan | Quality (out of 10, native reviewers, illustrative) | Cost to build | Cost to run |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1B model pre-trained from scratch | 4.1 | Data work plus compute | Very low |
| 8B open model, used as is | 6.2 | None | Low, more if its tokenizer handles Kannada badly |
| 8B open model, continued training on their Kannada text | 7.6 | Moderate: about 100 H100-hours per pass of 3 billion tokens by the formula, more if its tokenizer splits Kannada finely | Same as above |

The scores are for illustration, not measured results. Compute was never the bottleneck; data, the head start of an existing model and tokenizer efficiency were. They choose continued training, starting from the candidate whose tokenizer needs the fewest tokens on a sample of their own text.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing parameter counts across generations.** Newer training often beats raw size. Instruction-tuned Llama 3 8B outscored Llama 2 70B chat on MMLU, though the base 8B was just below the base 70B. Compare on your own task.
- **Mixing up total and active parameters in MoE models.** Memory follows the total; speed, cost and the 6 × parameters × tokens rule follow the active count.
- **Forgetting the KV cache and overhead** when sizing GPUs from the weights alone.
- **Treating 6 × parameters × tokens as a quote.** It is a lower bound; Llama 3 8B used about 2.6 times the estimate at 40% utilisation.
- **Treating Chinchilla as the goal for a product model.** It minimises training cost; models used by many people should train much longer.
- **Trusting fitted constants.** The ratios hold up better than the exact formula. Re-fit on your own runs before forecasting.
- **Counting tokens without naming the tokenizer.** "3 billion tokens" of Kannada can mean very different amounts of text.
- **Ignoring data quality and repeats.** Better text beats more text, and repeating data past about four passes gives little.

## When bigger is not better

- **Narrow tasks** such as classification or extraction often reach the bar with small models.
- **High volume** makes running cost dominate: one crore replies a month on a 70B model costs roughly nine times as much arithmetic as on an 8B one.
- **Latency budgets** favour smaller models, which write each token faster.
- **On-device or private deployments** may only fit small models.
- **Reasoning-heavy questions** the model nearly solves may gain more from thinking time than from size.

## From demo to production

- **Start from the smallest model that might work**, and move up only when your test set says so.
- **Size hardware from total parameters, precision and the KV cache**, with headroom.
- **Measure token counts on your own text** in every language you serve, before you compare prices or context windows.
- **Revisit model choices regularly.** Newer small models keep closing the gap.
- **Plan data before compute** for any training project, and measure how much useful, non-repeated data you really have.
