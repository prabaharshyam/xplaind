## What it is

Four regimes come up most often in Indian AI deployments:

- **India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act)**, with its Rules notified on 14 November 2025 and obligations phased in over eighteen months. It governs digital personal data of people in India.
- **The EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)**, which applies when you handle personal data of people in the EU, for example an Indian company serving European customers.
- **SOC 2**, not a law but an independent audit report on a service provider's security controls. Enterprise buyers, especially in the US, often require it of vendors.
- **HIPAA**, the US health privacy law, which reaches Indian firms that process US patients' health information for US healthcare clients.

AI-specific frameworks, such as the EU AI Act, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC 42001, add duties about risk, transparency and oversight; the governance lessons on other paths cover them.

This lesson is a working engineer's map, not legal advice. Always involve the client's legal and privacy team.

## How it works

**DPDP Act essentials.** The organisation that decides why and how data is processed is the **data fiduciary**; vendors processing on its behalf are **data processors**. Processing needs a lawful ground, mainly consent that is free, specific, informed and unambiguous, given after a clear notice, and as easy to withdraw as to give; the Act also lists certain legitimate uses. Data may be used only for the stated purpose and should be erased when no longer needed. People have rights to access, correct and erase their data and to a grievance process. Fiduciaries must take reasonable security safeguards and report personal data breaches to the Data Protection Board and the affected people. Children's data needs verifiable parental consent and may not be used for tracking or targeted advertising. Large or sensitive fiduciaries can be classed as significant data fiduciaries, with extra duties such as audits and impact assessments. Penalties run up to ₹250 crore for failing to protect data.

**GDPR essentials.** Similar ideas with more detail: six lawful bases, purpose limitation, data minimisation, rights including access and erasure, data protection impact assessments for high-risk processing, rules for transfers outside the EU, and fines up to 4% of global turnover.

**SOC 2.** An auditor tests the provider's controls against criteria for security and, optionally, availability, confidentiality, processing integrity and privacy. A Type I report checks the design at a point in time; a Type II report checks that controls worked over a period, typically several months. Clients ask whether you, and every vendor in your stack such as the model provider, have one.

**HIPAA.** Protects health information held by covered entities (providers, insurers) and their business associates. A vendor must sign a business associate agreement before touching such data, and use only the minimum necessary.

**What it means for design.**

- Map every flow of personal data: source, purpose, where stored, which vendors see it, how long it stays.
- Minimise: send models only the fields the task needs; mask the rest.
- Check model vendors' terms: is data used for training, stored, and where?
- Retention and deletion: set periods per store, and make erasure requests reach the index, logs and backups.
- Access control, audit logs and breach response plans.

## A worked example

A Pune health-tech company builds an AI scribe that drafts clinical notes from doctor-patient conversations, for clinics in India and one US hospital network.

The privacy review produces a data flow map: audio from the clinic, transcript, draft note, the doctor's edits, the final note in the clinic's records, logs.

For India (DPDP): the clinic is the data fiduciary and the company a data processor under a contract. Patients get a notice and give consent at registration, with a simple way to withdraw. Audio is deleted 7 days after the note is signed. Erasure requests delete transcripts and drafts.

For the US hospital (HIPAA): a business associate agreement is signed, US patient data stays in a US cloud region, and the model provider is used only under its own HIPAA-eligible terms.

For buyers generally: the company starts a SOC 2 Type II audit, and keeps the model provider's SOC 2 report in its vendor file. Logs mask names and identifiers. The design review took three weeks, and the deal closed because every question had a documented answer.

## Common mistakes

- **"We don't store data" without checking.** Logs, traces, caches, backups and vendors all store data.
- **Ignoring vendor terms.** The model provider's retention and training terms are part of your compliance.
- **Collecting everything in case it is useful later.** Purpose limitation and minimisation say no.
- **No erasure path through derived data.** Deleting the source document but not its chunks, embeddings and logs is not erasure.
- **Engineers giving legal opinions.** Describe the system precisely; let lawyers interpret the law.

## Practise

Read PRS's summary of the DPDP Bill, GDPR.eu's overview and the SOC 2 section of the AICPA page.

1. Draw a data flow map for one AI system you have built: every place personal data goes, why, for how long and which vendor sees it.
2. For each flow, write the DPDP question a privacy officer would ask (purpose, consent, retention, security, erasure) and your answer.
3. Read one model provider's data usage and retention terms, and summarise them in five lines for a client.

**You are done when you can** walk a privacy officer through your data flow map and show how an erasure request removes a person from every store.
