## What it is

Choosing a model means picking which language model, from which provider and in which size, powers each part of your application. There are hundreds of options: hosted models behind an API, and open-weight models you run yourself. New ones arrive every month, each with a press release about record scores.

The scores come from **benchmarks**: fixed public test sets that measure one skill, such as school maths, general knowledge or writing code. A **leaderboard** ranks models by combining scores from a chosen set of benchmarks, or by people voting between two anonymous answers.

Benchmarks are useful, but they answer a different question from yours. They ask "which model is good at these tests?" You need to know "which model is good enough at my task, at a price and speed I can afford, under my rules?" A reliable way to choose has four steps.

```flow
title: Choosing a model in four steps
All candidate models -> Filter on hard requirements | licence, data location, languages, price
Filter on hard requirements -> Shortlist with public benchmarks | 3 to 5 models
Shortlist with public benchmarks -> Test on your own data | your inputs, your rubric
Test on your own data -> Meets quality, cost and speed?
Meets quality, cost and speed? -[no]-> Change the design | smaller task, better prompt, retrieval
Change the design -> Test on your own data
Meets quality, cost and speed? -[yes]-> Launch and monitor | re-check when models change
```

## How it works

**Step 1: filter on hard requirements.** Some things you cannot fix with prompting. Write them down first, and remove every model that fails one.

- **Licence and terms.** Can you use it commercially? Can you keep its outputs? Some open-weight licences have limits for large companies.
- **Data location and privacy.** Can it run in an Indian cloud region or on your own servers? Under India's data protection law, and for banks under RBI rules, this can rule out many options.
- **Languages.** Does it handle the languages your users write in, including mixed Hinglish?
- **Context length, speed and price.** Can it read your longest document? Does it reply fast enough? Does the cost per request fit your budget?

**Step 2: shortlist with public benchmarks.** Use benchmarks and leaderboards to pick 3 to 5 candidates worth testing, not to choose the winner. Read them with these limits in mind:

- **Leaderboards choose their tests.** A leaderboard covers a handful of benchmarks out of thousands, and the choice decides who wins. Most are weighted toward English and general knowledge.
- **Small details change scores.** Hugging Face once found that the same model got very different scores on the same benchmark depending on how the prompt was written and scored. Compare scores only when they were measured the same way.
- **Saturation.** When top models all score near 100%, a benchmark no longer separates them. Leaderboards replace saturated benchmarks every year or two.
- **Contamination.** Benchmark questions are public, so they leak into web text and then into training data. A model that has seen the questions scores well from memory. Labs check for this in two common ways: looking for long word sequences shared between the test and the training data (n-gram overlap), and checking whether the model finds test questions suspiciously easy to predict (low perplexity). Treat scores on old, popular benchmarks with care.
- **Vote-based rankings** reflect what many people prefer in short chats, often fluent, confident and nicely formatted answers. That may not match your task.

**Step 3: test on your own data.** This is the step that decides. Build a test set of 100 to 300 real inputs, with anonymised personal data, covering each language and each kind of request. Write a scoring rubric before you look at any outputs. Run every shortlisted model with the same prompt, and record quality, cost and speed together. The lessons on test sets and LLM judges cover how to score.

A small harness keeps the comparison fair:

```python
import json, time

def run(model_name, call_model, cases, score):
    """call_model(prompt) returns (text, cost_in_rupees). score(case, text) returns 0 to 1."""
    total, cost, slow = 0.0, 0.0, []
    for case in cases:
        start = time.time()
        text, rupees = call_model(case["input"])
        slow.append(time.time() - start)
        total += score(case, text)
        cost += rupees
    slow.sort()
    return {
        "model": model_name,
        "quality": round(total / len(cases), 3),
        "cost_per_1000": round(cost / len(cases) * 1000, 1),
        "p95_seconds": round(slow[int(len(slow) * 0.95) - 1], 2),
    }

cases = [json.loads(line) for line in open("helpdesk_test.jsonl")]
```

**Step 4: launch and monitor.** Sample real conversations every week and score them. Re-run your test set when the provider updates the model, when prices change, or when a cheaper model appears. Your test set is what makes switching safe.

**Build or buy.** Hosted APIs are quick to start, scale for you and are often the strongest models. Open-weight models give you control over data and cost at high volume, but you run the servers. Many teams start with an API, then move the high-volume, simple steps to a smaller open model once their test set proves it is good enough.

## A worked example

A Chennai lending company needs a helpdesk assistant for loan questions in Tamil and English. Data must stay in India, and the budget is about ₹0.37 per conversation.

They start with eight candidates. The filter removes three: one cannot run in an Indian region, one licence forbids their use, and one is far over budget. Public leaderboards help them drop one more model that is weak at following instructions. Four remain.

They build a test set of 240 real questions (120 Tamil, 120 English), with names and loan numbers removed, and a rubric: correct facts from the policy, clear next step, polite tone, right language.

| Model | Leaderboard rank | Own test: Tamil | Own test: English | Cost per conversation | p95 reply time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A, large hosted | 1 | 81% | 93% | ₹0.62 | 3.1 s |
| B, medium hosted | 6 | 84% | 90% | ₹0.21 | 1.4 s |
| C, open-weight, self-hosted | 11 | 74% | 88% | ₹0.09 | 1.9 s |
| D, small hosted | 15 | 62% | 85% | ₹0.05 | 0.8 s |

The numbers are for illustration, to show how to compare. The leaderboard leader, A, is best in English but over budget, and slightly worse in Tamil than B. B meets the bar in both languages at a third of A's price, so they choose B. They keep C as a fallback that could be fine-tuned on Tamil replies later, and they re-run the 240 questions every time a new model is announced.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing by headline score.** A leaderboard win means good at those tests, not good at your task.
- **Testing in English only.** Quality often drops in Indian languages and in mixed-language text. Test in every language your users write in.
- **Comparing scores measured differently.** Different prompts and scoring rules can move scores a lot.
- **Ignoring cost and speed until launch.** A model that is 2 points better but 3 times the price may be the wrong choice.
- **Never re-checking.** Prices fall and models improve. A choice from six months ago may now be overpriced.
- **Trusting old benchmarks.** Popular, older test sets are likely in training data, so high scores may be memory.

## When benchmarks do help

- **Building a shortlist fast.** They rule out models that are clearly weak at a skill you need, such as coding or long documents.
- **Tracking the field.** A fresh, hard benchmark shows how quickly models are improving.
- **Checking a claim.** If a vendor says their model is best at maths, a public benchmark gives a first check before you spend time testing.
