## What it is

An **incident** is any event that harms users or the business and needs an urgent response. AI systems add new kinds to the usual outages:

- **Availability**: provider outage, rate limits, a broken pipeline.
- **Cost**: runaway agents or traffic spikes burning budget.
- **Quality**: answers suddenly wrong after a model, prompt or document change.
- **Safety and security**: harmful content, prompt injection, data leakage, unauthorised actions.
- **Fairness and compliance**: a pattern of unfair outputs, or personal data used improperly.

**Incident response** is the planned way to handle them: detect, assess, contain, fix, communicate, and learn.

## How it works

**Detect.** Alerts on errors, latency, cost, guardrail triggers, refusal rates, negative feedback and online eval scores. User and staff reports through a clear channel. Many AI incidents first appear as a few complaints; take them seriously.

**Assess severity.** Define levels in advance. For example: severity 1 for data leakage, harmful output to customers or unauthorised actions; severity 2 for widespread wrong answers or a full outage; severity 3 for degraded quality in one area. Severity sets who is woken and how fast.

**Roles.** An incident commander who coordinates and decides, someone hands-on fixing, someone communicating with stakeholders. One person can hold several roles in a small team, but the commander role must be clear.

**Contain first.** Stop the harm before finding the cause: flip a kill switch for a tool or feature, roll back to the previous release, disable a document from the index, route to humans. Containment options should exist and be tested before you need them.

**Investigate with records.** Traces, audit logs and release identifiers show what happened. Preserve them.

**Communicate.** Clients and users need timely, honest updates. Regulated clients may have notification duties, such as personal data breach reporting under the DPDP Act; involve their privacy and legal teams at once.

**Learn.** A blameless post-incident review: timeline, impact, root causes (usually several), what went well, what to change, with owners and dates. Add the triggering cases to the eval suite and to monitoring.

## A worked example

Saturday, 11:40 am. A Chennai e-commerce company's support assistant begins telling customers that orders above ₹5,000 qualify for free returns within 60 days. The policy says 15 days.

11:55: negative feedback on returns questions triples; an alert fires. The on-call engineer becomes incident commander and declares severity 2.

12:05: traces show answers citing a "Diwali returns campaign" page published that morning by marketing, which mentioned 60 days for one product category only. Containment: the page is removed from the index and the assistant's returns answers are switched to a fixed, approved policy message.

12:20: customer support leads are told; they prepare to honour the 60-day promise for affected customers. Audit logs identify 312 conversations; the company decides to honour the statement for those orders.

Monday's blameless review finds three causes: no review of marketing pages before indexing, no effective-date and scope metadata on campaign pages, and no golden-set questions on returns. Actions: marketing pages go into a separate index with a review step, scope metadata added, 20 returns questions added to the golden set, and an alert on sudden changes in policy-related answers.

## Common mistakes

- **Investigating before containing.** Stop the harm first.
- **Untested kill switches and rollbacks.** Practise them.
- **No severity levels.** Every incident becomes either a panic or ignored.
- **Silence towards the client.** Bad news travels anyway; tell them early and honestly.
- **Blame-focused reviews.** People hide mistakes, and the system does not improve.

## Practise

Read the Google SRE chapter on managing incidents and its workbook chapter on postmortem culture. Browse incidents in the AI Incident Database.

1. Define severity levels and roles for an AI system you have built, and list the containment actions available for each type of incident.
2. Run a tabletop exercise: "the assistant is quoting a wrong price", and walk through detection, containment, communication and investigation.
3. Write a blameless review of that exercise with at least three actions.

**You are done when you can** show your containment options working (a rollback and a kill switch) and a review template your team would actually use.
