## What it is

An **incident** is any event that harms users or the business and needs an urgent response. AI systems add new kinds to the usual outages:

- **Availability**: provider outage, rate limits, a broken pipeline.
- **Cost**: runaway agents or traffic spikes burning budget.
- **Quality**: answers suddenly wrong after a model, prompt or document change.
- **Safety and security**: harmful content, prompt injection, data leakage, unauthorised actions.
- **Fairness and compliance**: a pattern of unfair outputs, or personal data used improperly.

**Incident response** is the planned way to handle them: detect, assess, contain, fix, communicate, and learn.

```flow
dir: LR
Detect | alerts, complaints -> Assess | severity 1, 2 or 3; roles named
Assess -> Contain | kill switch, rollback, remove, document, humans take over
Contain -> Investigate | traces, logs, release ids
Investigate -> Communicate | client, users, regulators as required
Communicate -> Learn | blameless review, actions with owners, new eval cases
Learn -[monitoring improved]-> Detect
```

This lesson follows a Saturday incident at an e-commerce company in Chennai, where the support assistant promised the wrong returns window.

## How it works

**Detect.** Alerts on errors, latency, cost, guardrail triggers, refusal rates, negative feedback and online eval scores. User and staff reports through a clear channel. Many AI incidents first appear as a few complaints; take them seriously.

**Assess severity.** Define levels in advance. For example: severity 1 for data leakage, harmful output to customers or unauthorised actions; severity 2 for widespread wrong answers or a full outage; severity 3 for degraded quality in one area. Severity sets who is woken and how fast.

**Roles.** An incident commander who coordinates and decides, someone hands-on fixing, someone communicating with stakeholders. One person can hold several roles in a small team, but the commander role must be clear.

**Contain first.** Stop the harm before finding the cause: flip a kill switch for a tool or feature, roll back to the previous release, disable a document from the index, route to humans. Containment options should exist and be tested before you need them.

**Investigate with records.** Traces, audit logs and release identifiers show what happened. Preserve them.

**Communicate.** Clients and users need timely, honest updates. Regulated clients may have notification duties, such as personal data breach reporting under the DPDP Act; involve their privacy and legal teams at once.

**Learn.** A blameless post-incident review: timeline, impact, root causes (usually several), what went well, what to change, with owners and dates. Add the triggering cases to the eval suite and to monitoring.

**Severity levels and responses:**

| Level | Examples | Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Data leakage, harmful output to customers, unauthorised actions | Page on-call and leads at once; contain within minutes |
| 2 | Widespread wrong answers, full outage | On-call within 15 minutes; client informed within hours |
| 3 | Degraded quality in one area | Next working day; tracked to closure |

**A runbook page per incident type.** For each kind of incident, write one page: how it is detected, the first three containment actions with links to the switches, who to inform, and where the relevant traces and logs are. Keep it short enough to read under pressure. During a tabletop exercise, follow the page exactly and note every place it was unclear or out of date, then fix it the same week.

**Writing to the client during an incident.** Send short, factual updates at agreed intervals: what is happening, what users may have seen, what has been done, and when the next update will come. Do not guess at causes in early updates. Saying "we have stopped the wrong answers and are confirming how many customers were affected" builds more trust than a confident explanation that turns out wrong.

**Detect drift from normal, not just hard limits.** Many AI incidents are a rate quietly changing. Compare the last hour with the same hour over recent days:

```python
from statistics import mean, pstdev

def anomalous(current, history, min_sigma=3.0, min_abs=0.02):
    """current: rate this hour; history: same-hour rates from the last 14 days."""
    mu, sd = mean(history), pstdev(history) or 0.005
    return (current - mu) / sd > min_sigma and current - mu > min_abs

# e.g. refusal rate, guardrail triggers, thumbs-down rate, judge failure rate
if anomalous(0.11, [0.03, 0.04, 0.03, 0.05, 0.04, 0.03, 0.04]):
    page_on_call("Refusal rate 11% vs about 4% normal for this hour")
```

**Contain a bad document in one step.** The fastest fix for "the assistant is quoting a wrong price" is often removing one source:

```python
def quarantine_document(doc_id, index, cache, audit, reason, who):
    index.set_flag(doc_id, quarantined=True)        # every search filters quarantined docs
    purged = cache.delete_where(source_doc=doc_id)  # cached answers built from it
    audit.write(actor=who, action="quarantine", target=doc_id, detail={"reason": reason, "cache_purged": purged})
```

Make it an admin button, test it before launch, and include it in the runbook.

**Measure the impact from traces.** Customers and regulators ask "how many people saw it?". If traces record retrieved document ids, the answer is one query:

```sql
SELECT count(DISTINCT user_id) AS users, count(*) AS answers,
       min(created_at) AS first_seen, max(created_at) AS last_seen
FROM traces
WHERE 'pricing-2026-11' = ANY(retrieved_doc_ids)
  AND created_at BETWEEN '2026-11-06 09:00+05:30' AND '2026-11-07 14:30+05:30';
```

Without trace data this becomes days of guesswork, which is a good argument for logging retrieved ids from day one.

**Reporting duties in India.** A personal data breach can trigger notice duties to the Data Protection Board and the affected people under the DPDP Act and its Rules. Separately, CERT-In's 2022 directions require many kinds of cyber security incidents to be reported within 6 hours. Agree in advance who decides whether an incident is reportable (usually the client's legal or privacy lead) and keep their contact in the runbook.

**Practise.** Run a tabletop exercise every quarter with a different scenario: a leaked document, a jailbreak screenshot on social media, a provider outage, a model update that breaks formatting. Time detection and containment, and fix whatever slowed you down.

## A worked example

Saturday, 11:40 am. A Chennai e-commerce company's support assistant begins telling customers that orders above ₹5,000 qualify for free returns within 60 days. The policy says 15 days.

11:55: negative feedback on returns questions triples; an alert fires. The on-call engineer becomes incident commander and declares severity 2.

12:05: traces show answers citing a "Diwali returns campaign" page published that morning by marketing, which mentioned 60 days for one product category only. Containment: the page is removed from the index and the assistant's returns answers are switched to a fixed, approved policy message.

12:20: customer support leads are told; they prepare to honour the 60-day promise for affected customers. Audit logs identify 312 conversations; the company decides to honour the statement for those orders.

Monday's blameless review finds three causes: no review of marketing pages before indexing, no effective-date and scope metadata on campaign pages, and no golden-set questions on returns. Actions: marketing pages go into a separate index with a review step, scope metadata added, 20 returns questions added to the golden set, and an alert on sudden changes in policy-related answers.

| Time | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 11:40 | Assistant starts promising 60-day returns |
| 11:55 | Feedback alert; severity 2 declared |
| 12:05 | Campaign page removed from the index; fixed policy message switched on |
| 12:20 | Support leads told; 312 conversations identified and honoured |
| Monday | Blameless review: three causes, four actions with owners |

## Common mistakes

- **Investigating before containing.** Stop the harm first.
- **Untested kill switches and rollbacks.** Practice them.
- **No severity levels.** Every incident becomes either a panic or ignored.
- **Silence towards the client.** Bad news travels anyway; tell them early and honestly.
- **Blame-focused reviews.** People hide mistakes, and the system does not improve.
- **No retrieved ids in traces**, so impact cannot be measured.
- **Containment tools that exist only on paper.** Test every switch and quarantine before launch.
- **Waiting for a root cause before telling the client.** Send facts as you have them.

## When a lighter process is enough

- **Internal prototypes** with a handful of users: a named owner, a kill switch and a chat channel may do.
- **Read-only tools on public data**, where the worst case is a wrong answer people can check.

Full severity levels, on-call rotas, runbooks and drills are needed once customers, personal data, money or regulated decisions are involved.

## From demo to production

- **Alert on rate changes** for refusals, guardrail triggers, feedback and judge failures, not only errors.
- **Keep containment one click away**: kill switches, rollback and document quarantine, all tested.
- **Log retrieved ids, versions and users** so impact can be measured fast.
- **Name the reporting decision-maker** for DPDP and CERT-In duties in the runbook.
- **Drill quarterly** and fix the slowest step each time.
