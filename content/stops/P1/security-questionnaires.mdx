## What it is

A **security questionnaire**, also called a vendor risk assessment, is how an enterprise checks a supplier before giving it access to data or systems. It may be the client's own spreadsheet, a standard set such as the Cloud Security Alliance's **CAIQ** (Consensus Assessments Initiative Questionnaire), its newer **AI-CAIQ** for AI systems or the **SIG** from Shared Assessments, or a vendor-risk portal where you type answers and upload evidence.

The questions cover the same ground almost every time: policies and reports, access control, encryption, penetration tests, logging and incident response, backups, and data location, retention and sub-processors. Now there is also an AI section: training on client data, model providers, prompt injection, human oversight and change testing.

### Reports and standards a reviewer will name

- **SOC 2** is an independent auditor's report on your controls. **Type I** says they were designed properly at one point in time; **Type II** says they also worked over a review period of several months, so reviewers prefer it. If that period ended long ago, expect a request for a "bridge letter" covering the time since. SOC 2 is an attestation report, not a certificate: write "we have a SOC 2 Type II report", not "we are SOC 2 certified".
- **ISO/IEC 27001** certifies an information security management system; **ISO/IEC 42001** certifies an AI management system, meaning how a company governs the AI it builds or uses.
- The **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** is a voluntary US framework. Nobody is certified against it; you can show how your process maps to it.

## How it works

### Build an answer library

Most questions repeat across clients. Keep a reviewed library of approved answers, each with an **owner** (usually the person who runs that control), a **last-reviewed date** and **evidence links** (policies, the SOC 2 report, a penetration test summary, architecture diagrams, test results). Answer each new questionnaire from the library, then add the new answers back. When the architecture changes, for example a new model provider or tracing tool, fix every affected answer the same week. An answer about last year's system is a wrong answer.

To cut repeat work further, fill in a standard questionnaire such as the CAIQ once and offer it before the client sends its own. And run a **trust centre**: a public security summary plus a gated area where approved clients download the SOC 2 report and other evidence under a non-disclosure agreement.

### Write answers that survive one follow-up

A bare "Yes" invites follow-ups, and an overclaim can become a contract breach. A good answer says what, how and where. When a control is only partly in place, say "Partially": what exists, what is missing and the date for the rest. Reviewers trust "not yet, and here is the plan" far more than a vendor who claims everything.

| Question | Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Encryption at rest? | "Yes" | "AES-256 with keys in Azure Key Vault, Central India region; keys rotate yearly" |
| Is data used for training? | "No" | "Provider X's enterprise terms exclude training on our API data; it keeps requests 30 days for abuse checks; clause attached" |
| Prompt injection? | "We use industry-standard AI security" | "We reduce the risk: least-privilege tools, approval for outbound actions, injection test set run in CI; latest results attached" |
| Pen testing? | "Yes" | "Annual external test of the web app; AI endpoints are in the test booked for 15 January 2027" |

The provider, retention period and dates here are examples; write yours from your own contracts.

### The AI section

Expect these questions in almost every AI questionnaire, and keep the answers current:

- **Providers and terms:** which providers and regions, and what the contract says about training and how long requests are kept. Record the date you last read each provider's terms, because terms change.
- **Data sent:** which fields reach the model, and whether personal data is masked first.
- **Retention everywhere:** prompts live with the provider, in your tracing tool, logs and backups, not just the app. Answer for all of them.
- **Prompt injection and actions:** which tools the model can call, with what permissions, and which actions need a person's approval.
- **Guardrails, audit logs and kill switches,** with test results.
- **Change control:** how a new model or prompt is tested before release.

### Sub-processors and data flows

List every third party that touches client data: cloud, model provider, vector database, tracing tool, email service, and any AI tool you use to draft answers. Give each its purpose and data location. Contracts often let the client object to a new one, so adding one means telling the client first. A one-page **data flow map** (where data enters, every store and service it passes through, the region of each, who can see it) answers many questions on its own.

### Indian regulatory questions

An Indian bank's own rules shape much of its questionnaire. The bank carries most of the legal duties and passes them to you through the contract.

| Rule | Who it binds | What the bank will usually ask you | Evidence to prepare |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RBI (Commercial Banks – Managing Risks in Outsourcing) Directions, 2025, issued 28 November 2025, with a separate chapter on IT outsourcing | The bank, which must put terms in its contract with you | Will you accept audits by the bank and inspection by RBI? Who are your subcontractors? How do you exit and return data? | Agreed audit and inspection clauses, subcontractor list, continuity and exit plan |
| RBI's cybersecurity and technology risk directions for commercial banks, issued 31 July 2026 | The bank, which must report cyber incidents to RBI's DAKSH platform within 6 hours of detection | How fast will you tell us about an incident? Can we see your penetration test report and the fixes? | Incident notice clause in hours, latest test summary, fix tracker |
| CERT-In directions, 28 April 2022, under section 70B(6) of the IT Act | You directly, and the bank: it covers service providers, data centres, body corporates and more | Do you report listed incidents to CERT-In within 6 hours of noticing them, keep ICT logs for a rolling 180 days within Indian jurisdiction, and sync clocks to NIC or NPL time servers? Who is your point of contact? | Incident runbook with the CERT-In step, log retention settings, time server settings, named contact |
| Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, and DPDP Rules, 2025 | The bank as Data Fiduciary; you as its Data Processor, engaged only under a valid contract | What personal data do you process, and why? What security safeguards do you use? How fast will you tell us about a personal data breach? Will you erase data on our instruction? | Data processing agreement, data inventory, safeguard list, erasure procedure |
| RBI circular on Storage of Payment System Data, 6 April 2018 | Payment system providers, and the vendors they engage | Does your system receive payment data? Where is it stored? | Data flow map showing payment data stays in India, or never reaches you |
| RBI's FREE-AI committee report, 13 August 2025 | Nobody yet: it is a committee report with 7 principles ("sutras") and 26 recommendations, not a binding direction | Can you support our AI inventory and AI policy? Can a decision be explained and traced? Where is a person in the loop? | Model card, eval results, audit log schema, approval steps |

Five gotchas experienced reviewers know:

- **No RBI rule says all bank data must stay in India.** The clear legal rule covers payment system data. The DPDP Act lets the government restrict transfers to countries it names, rather than listing allowed ones. Most banks still require Indian hosting by policy, so expect it, but never write "RBI forbids any data abroad".
- **Use the Act's own words.** The DPDP Act speaks of the **Data Fiduciary** (the bank), the **Data Processor** (you), the **Data Principal** (the customer) and a **personal data breach**. "Sub-processor" and "controller" come from GDPR-style contracts that many questionnaires copy. Answer the question as asked, but state legal positions in the Act's terms.
- **Know the dates.** The DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025; most duties, including security safeguards and breach intimation, apply from May 2027. Contracts signed now run past that date, so banks ask now.
- **The clocks belong to different people.** The bank's 6-hour RBI report runs from when the bank detects the incident, so your late notice makes the bank miss its deadline. Agree only to a notice window your on-call team can meet at 2 a.m. on a Sunday. Under the DPDP Act the Data Fiduciary tells the Data Protection Board and affected people; your job is to tell the bank fast.
- **Versions differ.** Small finance banks, NBFCs and other regulated entities have their own versions, and older questionnaires may cite the 2023 Master Direction on outsourcing of IT services. Answer the substance, cite the version the bank confirms, and let your legal adviser approve any compliance claim. Banks often want penetration tests by a CERT-In-empanelled auditor, so ask before booking.

### Evidence, drafting and follow-up calls

Share sensitive evidence, such as the full SOC 2 report, only under a non-disclosure agreement, through a link that expires. Link to living documents rather than pasting text that goes stale. Keep the CI results for injection and permission tests exportable as a short PDF: AI evidence is what reviewers have seen least.

A model can draft answers from your library and design notes. Three rules. It uses only the library and attached documents, citing the library ids it used. A named person approves every answer, so no invented control goes out. And the client's questionnaire is confidential: draft only with an approved provider and region already on your sub-processor list. Pasting a bank's questionnaire into a personal chatbot account is itself a data-handling failure.

On follow-up calls, bring the engineer who knows the system and the data flow map, answer only what you know, and send anything you had to check the same day.

### The answer library as data

Store answers in a file the team reviews like code, and script the bookkeeping:

```yaml
# security-answers.yaml
- id: enc-at-rest
  question: Is customer data encrypted at rest?
  answer: >
    Yes. Postgres and object storage use AES-256 with keys in Azure Key Vault (Central India), rotated yearly.
  owner: platform-lead
  evidence: [docs/architecture.pdf#storage, key-vault-policy.pdf]
  reviewed: 2026-09-14
  tags: [encryption, storage]
- id: ai-training
  question: Is our data used to train AI models?
  answer: >
    No. Our model provider's enterprise terms exclude training on API data; it keeps requests for 30 days
    for abuse checks (terms version and clause attached).
  owner: cto
  evidence: [provider-terms-2026-07.pdf]
  reviewed: 2026-07-30
  tags: [ai, vendor, training]
- id: prompt-retention
  question: Do you retain prompts or model outputs?
  answer: >
    Our app deletes them at the end of each session. Our tracing tool keeps them for 14 days.
  owner: ml-lead
  evidence: []
  reviewed: 2026-03-02
  tags: [ai, retention, logs]
```

```python
import yaml
from datetime import date, timedelta

def load(path="security-answers.yaml"):
    with open(path) as f:
        return yaml.safe_load(f)

def stale(answers, today=None, max_age_days=180):
    today = today or date.today()
    cutoff = today - timedelta(days=max_age_days)
    return [(a["id"], a["owner"]) for a in answers
            if a["reviewed"] < cutoff or not a.get("evidence")]

def suggest(question, answers, top=3):
    words = set(question.lower().split())
    def overlap(a):
        text = a["question"] + " " + " ".join(a["tags"])
        return len(words & set(text.lower().split()))
    return [a["id"] for a in sorted(answers, key=overlap, reverse=True)[:top]]

answers = load()
print(stale(answers, today=date(2026, 11, 28)))
print(suggest("Are prompts retained in logs?", answers, top=1))
```

Run on 28 November 2026, `stale` prints `[('prompt-retention', 'ml-lead')]`: that answer is over 180 days old, has no evidence, and covers tracing settings that change often. `suggest` prints `['prompt-retention']`. Run `stale` monthly and send each owner their list. Use `suggest`, or embeddings for a large library, to find candidates, but a person checks every match: a close answer can still be wrong.

## A worked example

This example is illustrative: the counts and dates show the method, not a real deal.

After a successful pilot, a Bengaluru AI startup receives a 240-question spreadsheet from a large private bank, due in two weeks. The FDE, Meera, coordinates.

**Day 1: triage.** The library matches 170 questions. Of the other 70, 40 are AI-specific and 30 are bank-specific. Each group gets an owner and a due date.

**Days 2 to 6: the AI questions.** She answers from the real design documents (data flow map, gateway configuration, masking rules, guardrail tests, approval step, audit log schema), and each answer cites a document section.

Checking "Do you retain prompts?", she finds the tracing tool keeps full prompts outside India; the library answer was stale. The team moves tracing to an Indian region and masks account numbers first. Meera updates the answer, sub-processor list and data flow map the same day.

**Days 3 to 9: the bank-specific questions.** With the startup's legal adviser, she maps them to the rules in the table above:

- **Outsourcing:** the startup accepts audit and inspection clauses and lists its cloud, model provider and tracing tool as its subcontractors.
- **Incident notice:** the bank asks for notice within 2 hours. Meera confirms with the on-call lead, then adds a "tell the bank" step to the runbook beside the CERT-In step.
- **Logs:** retention is set to 180 days in the Indian region.
- **DPDP:** the answers describe the startup as the bank's Data Processor, and attach the draft data processing agreement and the erasure procedure.

**Days 10 to 12: gaps and review.** Two real gaps appear: no penetration test of the AI endpoints, and no incident plan for model misbehaviour. She answers "Partially" for both, attaching a test booked with a CERT-In-empanelled firm and a draft runbook. A named person reviews every answer.

**Outcome.** The bank accepts with two conditions: the test report within 60 days, and the final runbook before go-live. The 70 new answers go into the library with owners and dates, so the next bank starts with more matches.

## Common mistakes

- **Overclaiming.** A false "Yes" can become a legal problem. Say "Partially" and give the plan.
- **Vague AI answers.** "We use industry-standard AI security" means nothing. Name providers, regions, terms and controls.
- **Missing a sub-processor or a copy of the data,** such as a tracing tool added last month that keeps prompts, or the AI tool used to draft answers.
- **Answers with no owner or review date,** so nobody notices when one stops being true.
- **Sending AI-drafted answers unchecked.**
- **Saying "SOC 2 certified" or "NIST AI RMF certified".** SOC 2 is an audit report, and the NIST framework has no certification.
- **Stating Indian law loosely.** "We are RBI compliant" is too vague, and "RBI requires all data in India" is wrong. Say which rule, what you do and what evidence shows it.
- **Promising a notice window on-call cannot meet.**

## When a full library is overkill

- **Your first one or two questionnaires**: answer them carefully, then turn the answers into the library.
- **Small clients** asking a few questions by email: answer directly, just as precisely.
- **Internal projects** covered by the company's own security review process.

## From demo to production

- **Keep the library in version control** and flag stale answers monthly and on every architecture change.
- **Maintain the sub-processor list and data flow map** together, with purpose and location for each service.
- **Keep an Indian rules sheet** (rule, date, who it binds, your evidence) and have legal review it when regulators issue new directions.
- **Track each questionnaire**: date, answer version sent, follow-ups promised.
