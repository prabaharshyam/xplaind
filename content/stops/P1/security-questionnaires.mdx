## What it is

A **security questionnaire** (vendor risk assessment) is how an enterprise checks a supplier before giving it access to data or systems. It may be the client's own spreadsheet, a standard such as the Cloud Security Alliance's CAIQ or a SIG questionnaire, or a portal from a vendor-risk platform. Questions cover:

- company security programme, policies and certifications (SOC 2, ISO 27001);
- access control, authentication and staff vetting;
- encryption in transit and at rest, key management;
- network security, vulnerability management and penetration tests;
- logging, monitoring and incident response;
- business continuity, backups and disaster recovery;
- data handling: location, retention, deletion, sub-processors;
- and, increasingly, AI-specific questions: training on client data, model providers, prompt injection, output review, human oversight.

## How it works

**Build an answer library.** Most questions repeat across clients. Keep a reviewed library of approved answers, each with an owner, a last-checked date and links to evidence (policy documents, the SOC 2 report, a penetration test summary, architecture diagrams). Answer new questionnaires from it, and add new answers back after review.

**Answer precisely and truthfully.** "Yes" with no detail invites follow-ups; an overclaim can become a contract breach. Good answers say what, how and where: "All data is encrypted at rest with AES-256 using keys in Azure Key Vault in Central India; keys rotate yearly."

**Partial and planned.** "Partially" with an explanation and a date for the rest is acceptable; a false "Yes" is not.

**The AI section.** Prepare answers you will be asked again and again:

- Which model providers and regions are used, and under what terms (training on data, retention)?
- What data is sent to the model, and is personal data masked?
- How is prompt injection handled, and are outputs reviewed by people for risky actions?
- What guardrails, audit logs and kill switches exist?
- How are model or prompt changes tested before release?

**Sub-processors.** List every third party that touches client data (cloud, model provider, tracing tool, email service) with purpose and location. Clients often have the right to object to new ones.

**Evidence and NDAs.** Share sensitive evidence such as the full SOC 2 report under a non-disclosure agreement, through a secure link that expires.

**Weak and strong answers:**

| Question | Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Encryption at rest? | "Yes" | "AES-256 with keys in Azure Key Vault, Central India; rotated yearly" |
| Is data used for training? | "No" | "Provider X's contract excludes training on client data; retention 30 days; clause 7.2 attached" |
| Prompt injection? | "We use industry-standard AI security" | "Least-privilege tools, approval for outbound actions, injection test set run in CI; latest results attached" |
| Pen testing? | "Yes" | "Annual external test of the web app; AI endpoints added in the test booked for next month" |

**Keeping the library alive.** Give each answer an owner, usually the person who runs that control, and a review date. When the architecture changes, such as a new model provider or a new tracing tool, search the library for affected answers and update them the same week. After every questionnaire, add new questions and their reviewed answers. A library maintained this way turns a two-week scramble into a few days of focused work.

**Working with the client's reviewers.** Expect follow-up calls. Bring the engineer who knows the system, the data flow map and the evidence. Answer only what you know, write down anything you need to check, and send it the same day. Reviewers trust vendors who say "we do not do that yet, and here is the plan" far more than vendors who claim everything.

## A worked example

A Bengaluru AI startup, after a successful pilot with a large private bank, receives a 240-question spreadsheet with a two-week deadline. The FDE, Meera, coordinates.

She starts from the answer library, which covers 170 questions. Of the rest, 40 are AI-specific and 30 are bank-specific (for example, RBI outsourcing guidelines and data localisation).

For the AI questions, she uses the system's actual design documents: the data flow map, the gateway configuration (Indian region only, model provider contracted not to train on data), the masking rules, the guardrail list with test results, the human approval step for customer-facing messages, and the audit log schema. Each answer cites the relevant document section.

Two questions reveal real gaps: no formal yearly penetration test of the AI endpoints, and no documented incident plan specific to model misbehaviour. She answers "Partially" for both, with an external penetration test booked for next month and a draft incident runbook attached. The bank's security team accepts the plan with conditions, and the contract proceeds in four weeks instead of the usual three months.

## Common mistakes

- **Overclaiming.** A false "Yes" can become a legal problem later. Say "Partially" and explain.
- **Starting from scratch each time.** Maintain an answer library with owners and dates.
- **Vague AI answers.** "We use industry-standard AI security" means nothing. Name providers, regions, terms and controls.
- **Forgetting sub-processors.** A tracing or email tool that sees client data counts.
- **Letting the library go stale.** Answers about last year's architecture are wrong answers.

## Practise

Download the CSA CAIQ (free) and skim its control domains; reread the OWASP Top 10 for LLM applications.

1. Pick 20 CAIQ questions and answer them for a system you have built, with evidence links or "not yet" plus a plan.
2. Write ten AI-specific questions a bank might add, and answer them precisely.
3. List every sub-processor in your system with its purpose and data location.

**You are done when you can** hand a reviewer your answers and evidence and have every answer stand up to one follow-up question.
