## What it is

AI governance frameworks tell organisations how to identify, manage and account for AI risks. Four matter most for Indian teams:

- **The EU AI Act**: a law that classifies AI uses by risk. A few uses are banned (such as social scoring by authorities). **High-risk** uses, such as AI in hiring, credit scoring, education access, essential services and certain safety components, carry obligations: risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, transparency, human oversight, accuracy and robustness. Limited-risk uses (chatbots, generated content) carry transparency duties. Providers of general-purpose AI models have their own obligations. It applies to systems placed on the EU market or used in the EU, regardless of where the company is based, with obligations phased in over several years.
- **The NIST AI Risk Management Framework**: a voluntary US framework with four functions: **govern** (policies, roles, culture), **map** (context and risks), **measure** (testing and metrics) and **manage** (prioritise and act). It is widely used as a shared vocabulary.
- **ISO/IEC 42001**: an international standard for an AI management system. Organisations can be audited and certified against it, as with ISO 27001 for security.
- **India's AI Governance Guidelines** (MeitY, November 2025): principles for safe and trusted AI, such as people first, fairness, accountability and transparency, with recommendations for institutions and industry. They do not create a separate AI law; they rely on existing laws such as the IT Act and the DPDP Act, and on sector regulators. Regulators such as the RBI have issued their own guidance for their sectors.

## How it works

**Classify the use case.** What does the system decide or influence, for whom, and how severe is a mistake? A drafting assistant for internal emails is low risk; a model that ranks loan applicants or job candidates is high risk under the EU AI Act and sensitive everywhere.

**Build a risk register.** For each risk (wrong output, bias, privacy breach, security attack, over-reliance, misuse), record likelihood, impact, controls and owner. The frameworks overlap; one register can map to all of them.

**Map controls to requirements.** Typical controls and where they show up:

- documented purpose and limits (an AI system card);
- data governance: sources, quality checks, personal data handling;
- evaluation including fairness across groups and languages;
- human oversight with real authority to override;
- logging and audit trails;
- transparency to users ("you are talking to an AI assistant");
- incident response and post-market monitoring;
- change management for models and prompts.

**Evidence, not intentions.** Auditors and risk committees want artefacts: eval reports, test results by group, logs, approval records, training records. Produce them as part of normal engineering.

**Proportionality.** Low-risk systems need light documentation; high-risk ones need the full set. Over-governing low-risk tools wastes effort and discourages adoption.

## A worked example

A Bengaluru HR-tech company sells a resume-screening assistant to Indian employers and wants its first European client.

Classification: screening job candidates is a high-risk use under the EU AI Act, and sensitive in India too.

The team builds a register of 14 risks. Examples: unfair ranking by gender, college or region (controls: fairness evaluation across groups, removing names, photos and addresses before scoring, quarterly bias audits); over-reliance by recruiters (controls: the tool suggests a shortlist with reasons, recruiters must review every rejection, override rates are monitored); data protection (controls: DPDP and GDPR-compliant consent and retention, deletion after the hiring cycle).

They map each control to the EU AI Act's high-risk obligations and to NIST functions, and produce technical documentation, a system card and a logging design. A fairness evaluation shows a lower shortlist rate for candidates from tier-3 colleges with the same skills; the team removes college names from the scoring input and the gap narrows from 11 points to 2.

The European client's procurement team accepts the documentation pack. The same pack also shortens reviews with Indian enterprise clients.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating frameworks as paperwork after the build.** Controls such as fairness testing and oversight must shape the design.
- **One level of governance for everything.** Match effort to risk.
- **Ignoring the EU AI Act because you are in India.** It applies to systems used in the EU.
- **Fairness tested only on overall accuracy.** Measure by group, including language and region.
- **Oversight in name only.** A human who cannot or does not override is not oversight.

## Practise

Read the EU AI Act summary, the NIST AI RMF overview and browse a few cases in the AI Incident Database.

1. Classify three AI use cases you know (an internal chatbot, a loan pre-screening tool, an exam proctoring tool) by EU AI Act risk tier, with reasons.
2. Write a risk register of at least eight risks for one of them, with controls and owners.
3. Map each control to an EU AI Act obligation and a NIST AI RMF function.

**You are done when you can** explain to a risk committee which tier your system falls into, what that requires and what evidence you have.
