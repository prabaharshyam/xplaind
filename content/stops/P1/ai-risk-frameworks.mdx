## What it is

AI risk frameworks tell an organisation how to find, manage and prove control of AI risks. Some are law, some voluntary, one certifiable. Indian teams usually meet five.

| Framework | Kind | Current version (checked 28 November 2026) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EU AI Act | Law, with fines | Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since 27 July 2026 |
| NIST AI Risk Management Framework | Voluntary | AI RMF 1.0 (NIST AI 100-1, January 2023) plus the Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024). NIST says 1.0 is being revised; no new version is published yet |
| ISO/IEC 42001 | Certifiable standard | ISO/IEC 42001:2023, first edition. Europe adopted it unchanged in 2026 as EN ISO/IEC 42001:2026 |
| India's AI Governance Guidelines | Guidance | Released by MeitY on 5 November 2025 |
| RBI FREE-AI and model risk guidance | Guidance for regulated finance | FREE-AI committee report, 13 August 2025; draft model risk management guidance, 24 June 2026 (no final version found) |

**The EU AI Act** sorts AI by what it is used for, not by which model it uses.

- **Banned practices**, such as social scoring and manipulation that exploits vulnerable people. The Omnibus adds a ban on "nudifier" apps and AI-generated child sexual abuse material.
- **High-risk uses**, listed in Annex III: hiring and managing workers, credit scoring of individuals, access to education, essential services, certain biometrics and others, plus AI that is a safety part of regulated products such as medical devices (Annex I). Duties: risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, instructions for users, human oversight, and accuracy, robustness and security.
- **Transparency duties** (Article 50): a chatbot must tell people they are talking to an AI, and generated or manipulated content must be marked.
- **Minimal risk**: spam filters, drafting aids. Nothing extra beyond basic AI literacy for staff.

Makers of general-purpose models have separate duties. The Act reaches any company whose system is placed on the EU market or whose output is used in the EU, wherever it is based. Fines go up to 35 million euros or 7% of worldwide turnover for banned practices, and 15 million euros or 3% for most other breaches.

**Dates matter**, because the Act switches on in stages:

| Date | What starts to apply |
| --- | --- |
| 1 August 2024 | The Act enters into force |
| 2 February 2025 | Banned practices; AI literacy duty for staff |
| 2 August 2025 | Duties for general-purpose AI models; EU governance bodies |
| 2 August 2026 | Article 50 transparency duties, such as chatbot disclosure |
| 2 December 2026 | New nudifier ban; end of the marking grace period for generative systems already on the market before 2 August 2026 |
| 2 December 2027 | High-risk duties for Annex III uses, such as hiring and credit (moved from 2 August 2026) |
| 2 August 2028 | High-risk duties for AI inside regulated products (moved from 2 August 2027) |

The delay is not a holiday: buyers ask for evidence now, and a year of quarterly fairness reports cannot be produced the month before a deadline.

**The NIST AI RMF** is a vocabulary many buyers share. Its four functions are **govern** (policies, roles, culture), **map** (context and risks), **measure** (tests and metrics) and **manage** (prioritise and act). Teams building on large language models map to its **Generative AI Profile**, which lists 12 risks, such as confabulation (confident false output), data privacy, information security, harmful bias and human-AI configuration (people over-trusting the system).

**ISO/IEC 42001** describes an AI management system: policy, roles, risk and impact assessment, controls, internal audit and improvement. Accredited bodies certify against it (ISO/IEC 42006:2025 sets their rules), and ISO/IEC 42005:2025 guides impact assessment. A certificate shows your process works; it does not prove any single model is fair or safe.

**India** has no AI-specific law. MeitY's guidelines set seven principles ("sutras"), such as people first, fairness and equity, accountability and understandable by design, and recommend an AI Governance Group, testing by the AI Safety Institute and a national AI incident database. Binding rules come from existing law: the amended IT Rules (from 20 February 2026) require synthetic content to be labelled, and the DPDP Act covers personal data, with most duties under its Rules starting in May 2027. In finance, the RBI's FREE-AI report made 26 non-binding recommendations, such as a board-approved AI policy and an inventory of AI models. The June 2026 draft model risk guidance would require inventories, independent validation and human oversight from banks and NBFCs once final.

This lesson follows an HR-tech company in Bengaluru preparing its resume-screening assistant for its first European client.

## How it works

### 1. Classify the use, not the model

Ask what the system decides or influences, about whom, and how bad a mistake is. The same model is minimal risk drafting emails and high risk ranking job applicants.

```flow
title: First pass at EU AI Act classification
Describe the use -> Banned practice?
Banned practice? -[yes]-> Stop | redesign
Banned practice? -[no]-> Safety part of a regulated product?
Safety part of a regulated product? -[yes, needs third-party checks]-> High risk | full duties
Safety part of a regulated product? -[no]-> Annex III area?
Annex III area? -[yes]-> Profiles people?
Profiles people? -[yes]-> High risk
Profiles people? -[no]-> Narrow or preparatory task only?
Narrow or preparatory task only? -[no]-> High risk
Narrow or preparatory task only? -[yes]-> Not high risk | document and register
Annex III area? -[no]-> Talks to people or generates content?
Talks to people or generates content? -[yes]-> Transparency duties
Talks to people or generates content? -[no]-> Minimal risk
```

The gotcha is Article 6(3). An Annex III system escapes high risk only if it does a narrow procedural or preparatory task, improves finished human work or flags deviations from past decisions for human review, without materially influencing the decision. A system that profiles people, such as scoring candidates or borrowers, is always high risk, whoever makes the final call. Relying on the exception means documenting why and still registering in the EU database.

### 2. Decide whether you are the provider or the deployer

For high-risk systems this decides who does what.

| Role | Who | Main duties |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Provider | Builds it and markets it under its name | Design duties (risk management, data governance, logging, documentation), quality management system, conformity assessment, CE marking, EU database registration, post-market monitoring, incident reporting, an EU authorised representative if based outside the EU |
| Deployer | Uses it in its own work | Use as instructed, trained overseers with authority, logs kept at least six months, inform workers and affected people; a fundamental-rights impact assessment if it is a public body or public-service provider, or scores credit or prices life or health insurance |

For most Annex III uses, including hiring and credit, conformity assessment is internal: the provider checks and documents itself, with no notified body. A deployer that rebrands the system, changes its purpose or modifies it substantially becomes the provider.

### 3. Keep one risk register

For each risk (wrong output, bias, privacy breach, prompt injection, over-reliance, misuse), record likelihood, impact, controls, an owner and dated evidence. One register maps to every framework: an "unfair ranking" row is EU AI Act data governance and accuracy, NIST map and measure, and an ISO/IEC 42001 risk assessment. Keep it as data, and let a script fail the review when a serious risk lacks a control, an owner or fresh evidence:

```python
from datetime import date

LEVEL = {"low": 1, "medium": 2, "high": 3}
RISKS = [
    {"id": "R1", "risk": "Unfair ranking by college tier", "likelihood": "medium", "impact": "high",
     "controls": ["college names removed from input", "quarterly fairness eval"],
     "owner": "Head of data science", "evidence_date": date(2026, 9, 30)},
    {"id": "R2", "risk": "Candidate data sent to a vendor that trains on it", "likelihood": "low", "impact": "high",
     "controls": ["zero-retention contract"], "owner": "", "evidence_date": None},
    {"id": "R3", "risk": "Recruiters accept shortlists without reading them", "likelihood": "high", "impact": "high",
     "controls": [], "owner": "Head of product", "evidence_date": date(2026, 5, 2)},
]

def review(risks, today=None, max_age_days=120):
    today = today or date.today()          # a default of date.today() would freeze at import time
    findings = []
    for r in risks:
        score = LEVEL[r["likelihood"]] * LEVEL[r["impact"]]   # 1 to 9
        if score < 3:
            continue
        if not r["controls"]:
            findings.append(f"{r['id']}: score {score} with no controls")
        if not r["owner"]:
            findings.append(f"{r['id']}: no owner")
        if not r["evidence_date"] or (today - r["evidence_date"]).days > max_age_days:
            findings.append(f"{r['id']}: evidence missing or older than {max_age_days} days")
    return findings

for f in review(RISKS, today=date(2026, 11, 28)):
    print(f)
```

On 28 November 2026 it prints:

```text
R2: no owner
R2: evidence missing or older than 120 days
R3: score 9 with no controls
R3: evidence missing or older than 120 days
```

R1 passes: controls, an owner and evidence from 59 days ago. Each control should point at something that runs (an eval, a CI test, an alert, a scheduled review); one that points at nothing is an intention.

### 4. Test fairness by group, including the groups you did not plan for

Compare selection rates across groups on a labelled test set. A common flag is the **four-fifths rule**: a group selected at under 80% of the top group's rate deserves investigation. It comes from US employment guidance (the 1978 Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures), where it is a rule of thumb, and it is not a legal test under the EU AI Act or Indian law. Treat it as an alarm, not a verdict.

```python
from collections import defaultdict

def by_group(rows, key):
    """rows: dicts with candidate fields plus 'selected' and 'qualified' (bools, from the labelled set)."""
    g = defaultdict(lambda: {"n": 0, "sel": 0, "qual": 0, "qual_sel": 0})
    for r in rows:
        s = g[key(r)]
        s["n"] += 1; s["sel"] += r["selected"]; s["qual"] += r["qualified"]
        s["qual_sel"] += r["selected"] and r["qualified"]
    rates = {k: v["sel"] / v["n"] for k, v in g.items()}
    top = max(rates.values())
    for k, v in sorted(g.items()):
        ratio = rates[k] / top if top else 1.0
        tpr = v["qual_sel"] / v["qual"] if v["qual"] else float("nan")
        note = "  <- below 0.8, investigate" if ratio < 0.8 else ""
        note += "  (small group)" if v["n"] < 100 else ""
        print(f"{k:<22} n={v['n']:<4} selected {rates[k]:>4.0%}  qualified picked {tpr:>4.0%}  ratio {ratio:.2f}{note}")
```

"Qualified picked" is the share of qualified candidates the model shortlisted; equal selection rates can hide a model that misses more qualified people in one group. The `key` argument lets you test combinations, such as tier and gender. Three traps catch experienced teams:

- **Proxies.** Removing a column does not remove its signal. PIN code, city, school board, English fluency in free text or the gap years on a resume can stand in for region, caste, class or gender. Test outcomes by group even when the group is not an input.
- **Intersections.** A model can look fair for each attribute alone and unfair for a combination, such as women from tier-3 colleges.
- **Small groups.** A rate from 60 people moves several points by chance. Collect more cases before you conclude.

In India, check gender, state or region, input language, college tier and urban versus rural.

### 5. Match the effort to the tier

Record each system's tier and revisit it when the use changes, for example when an internal tool starts deciding things for customers.

| Tier | Artefacts to keep |
| --- | --- |
| Minimal | One-page system card, normal security review, staff AI training |
| Transparency | The card, plus the disclosure text and content marking, tested in the product |
| High risk | Full register, technical documentation, fairness evals by group on a schedule, oversight records and override rates, logs, incident drills, post-market monitoring plan |

Make evidence a side effect of engineering: save each eval report with its date and model version, keep logs queryable, record approvals, and write the system card while the design is fresh.

## A worked example

A Bengaluru HR-tech company sells a resume-screening assistant to Indian employers. In November 2026 a retailer in the Netherlands wants to use it to shortlist store staff. The numbers below are illustrative.

**Classify.** Screening applicants is an Annex III employment use, and scoring individuals is profiling, so it is high risk whoever makes the final call. The duties apply from 2 December 2027, but the client wants evidence before it signs.

**Split the roles.** The Bengaluru company is the provider; the retailer is the deployer.

| The Bengaluru company owes | The Dutch retailer owes |
| --- | --- |
| Technical documentation and a quality management system | Use as instructed, by trained recruiters |
| Internal conformity assessment, EU declaration of conformity, CE marking | Real human oversight, with authority to reject the tool's ranking |
| EU database registration and an EU authorised representative | Keep the logs it controls for at least six months |
| Logging built into the system; instructions for use | Inform its works council and the candidates that the tool is used |
| Post-market monitoring and serious incident reporting | Report serious incidents and risks to the provider |

**Build the register.** The team lists 14 risks, including:

- **Unfair ranking** by gender, college or region: names, photos and addresses removed before scoring; quarterly fairness evals by group.
- **Over-reliance by recruiters:** shortlists come with reasons, every rejection is reviewed, override rates are monitored.
- **Data protection:** GDPR notices and retention for EU candidates, deletion after the hiring cycle, and a plan to meet the DPDP Rules before May 2027.

**Measure.** On 400 labelled past applicants, tier-1 college candidates were shortlisted at 30% and tier-3 at 19%: an 11-point gap, ratio 0.63. With college names removed from the scoring input:

```text
tier-1                 n=200  selected  30%  qualified picked  55%  ratio 1.00
tier-3                 n=200  selected  28%  qualified picked  52%  ratio 0.93
```

The gap is now 2 points. Splitting by tier and gender:

```text
tier-1, men            n=120  selected  30%  qualified picked  55%  ratio 0.98
tier-1, women          n=80   selected  30%  qualified picked  55%  ratio 0.98  (small group)
tier-3, men            n=140  selected  31%  qualified picked  57%  ratio 1.00
tier-3, women          n=60   selected  22%  qualified picked  40%  ratio 0.71  <- below 0.8, investigate  (small group)
```

Women from tier-3 colleges fall below four-fifths, but the group has only 60 people. The team logs an open finding with an owner and a date, collects 300 more labelled cases, and checks proxies such as gap years and the city of the last job.

The retailer accepts the pack: system card, instructions for use, eval reports by group, logging design and the register. The same pack shortens risk reviews with Indian enterprise clients.

## Common mistakes

- **Governance written after the build.** Fairness tests, logging and oversight must shape the design.
- **Ignoring the EU AI Act because you are in India.** It applies when the system or its output is used in the EU.
- **Reading the delay as relief.** December 2027 is the legal date; buyers want evidence now.
- **Classifying by technology.** "It is just a chatbot" says nothing; what the output is used for decides the tier.
- **Trusting "a human decides".** Profiling keeps a system high risk, and a reviewer who never overrides is not oversight.
- **Assuming the client is the provider.** If you build it and sell it under your name, the provider duties are yours.
- **Fairness checked once, on overall numbers.** Measure by group and by combinations, on a schedule.
- **Treating four-fifths as the law, or a dropped column as a fix.** One is a US rule of thumb; the other leaves proxies in place.

## When a framework is overkill

- **Team productivity tools** on non-sensitive data: a short card and normal security are enough.
- **Prototypes** that never reach real users or real decisions.
- **Internal drafting tools** where a person always reviews and owns the output.

Keep the full set for systems that decide or strongly influence things about people: hiring, lending, insurance, health, education and public services.

## From demo to production

- **Run the register check in CI** before each release.
- **Schedule fairness evals** by group and combination, and store each report with its date and model version.
- **Name an owner** for every serious risk; review the register quarterly.
- **Record each system's tier and role**, and recheck them when the use changes.
- **Map controls once** to the EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001, and reuse the mapping for buyer questionnaires.
- **Recheck the dates each quarter.** The EU timeline, the RBI draft and the DPDP Rules are all still moving.
