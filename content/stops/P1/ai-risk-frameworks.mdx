## What it is

AI governance frameworks tell organisations how to identify, manage and account for AI risks. Four matter most for Indian teams:

- **The EU AI Act**: a law that classifies AI uses by risk. A few uses are banned (such as social scoring by authorities). **High-risk** uses, such as AI in hiring, credit scoring, education access, essential services and certain safety components, carry obligations: risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, transparency, human oversight, accuracy and robustness. Limited-risk uses (chatbots, generated content) carry transparency duties. Providers of general-purpose AI models have their own obligations. It applies to systems placed on the EU market or used in the EU, regardless of where the company is based, with obligations phased in over several years.
- **The NIST AI Risk Management Framework**: a voluntary US framework with four functions: **govern** (policies, roles, culture), **map** (context and risks), **measure** (testing and metrics) and **manage** (prioritise and act). It is widely used as a shared vocabulary.
- **ISO/IEC 42001**: an international standard for an AI management system. Organisations can be audited and certified against it, as with ISO 27001 for security.
- **India's AI Governance Guidelines** (MeitY, November 2025): principles for safe and trusted AI, such as people first, fairness, accountability and transparency, with recommendations for institutions and industry. They do not create a separate AI law; they rely on existing laws such as the IT Act and the DPDP Act, and on sector regulators. Regulators such as the RBI have issued their own guidance for their sectors.

| EU AI Act tier | Examples | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Banned | Social scoring by authorities | Not allowed |
| High risk | Hiring, credit, education access, essential services | Full obligations |
| Limited | Chatbots, generated content | Transparency duties |
| Minimal | Spam filters, drafting aids | Nothing extra |

## How it works

**Classify the use case.** What does the system decide or influence, for whom, and how severe is a mistake? A drafting assistant for internal emails is low risk; a model that ranks loan applicants or job candidates is high risk under the EU AI Act and sensitive everywhere.

**Build a risk register.** For each risk (wrong output, bias, privacy breach, security attack, over-reliance, misuse), record likelihood, impact, controls and owner. The frameworks overlap; one register can map to all of them.

**Map controls to requirements.** Typical controls and where they show up:

- documented purpose and limits (an AI system card);
- data governance: sources, quality checks, personal data handling;
- evaluation including fairness across groups and languages;
- human oversight with real authority to override;
- logging and audit trails;
- transparency to users ("you are talking to an AI assistant");
- incident response and post-market monitoring;
- change management for models and prompts.

**Evidence, not intentions.** Auditors and risk committees want artefacts: eval reports, test results by group, logs, approval records, training records. Produce them as part of normal engineering.

**Proportionality.** Low-risk systems need light documentation; high-risk ones need the full set. Over-governing low-risk tools wastes effort and discourages adoption.

**The frameworks compared:**

| Framework | Type | Where it bites | Main ask |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EU AI Act | Law | Systems used in the EU | Obligations by risk tier |
| NIST AI RMF | Voluntary framework | Widely used vocabulary, US buyers | Govern, map, measure, manage |
| ISO/IEC 42001 | Certifiable standard | Enterprise procurement | An audited AI management system |
| India's AI Governance Guidelines | Principles and recommendations | Indian deployments, alongside sector rules | Safe, trusted, accountable AI |

**A risk register row, in full:**

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Risk | Unfair ranking of candidates by college tier |
| Likelihood and impact | Medium likelihood, high impact |
| Controls | Remove college names from scoring input; fairness eval by group each quarter |
| Owner | Head of data science |
| Evidence | Quarterly fairness report; input schema showing removed fields |
| Mapped to | EU AI Act data governance and accuracy duties; NIST measure and manage |

**Building evidence as you go.** The cheapest compliance is a side effect of good engineering. Save every eval report with its date and version, keep logs queryable, record approvals, and write the system card when the design is fresh. Trying to rebuild this evidence months later for an auditor takes far longer and is less convincing.

**A register a script can check.** Keep risks as data, score them, and fail a review when a serious risk lacks controls, an owner or recent evidence:

```python
from datetime import date

LEVEL = {"low": 1, "medium": 2, "high": 3}
RISKS = [
    {"id": "R1", "risk": "Unfair ranking by college tier", "likelihood": "medium", "impact": "high",
     "controls": ["remove college names from input", "quarterly fairness eval"],
     "owner": "Head of data science", "evidence": "fairness-2026-Q3.pdf", "evidence_date": date(2026, 9, 30)},
    {"id": "R2", "risk": "Candidate data sent to a vendor that trains on it", "likelihood": "low", "impact": "high",
     "controls": ["zero-retention contract"], "owner": "", "evidence": None, "evidence_date": None},
]

def review(risks, today=date.today(), max_age_days=120):
    findings = []
    for r in risks:
        score = LEVEL[r["likelihood"]] * LEVEL[r["impact"]]
        if score >= 3:
            if not r["controls"]:
                findings.append(f"{r['id']}: score {score} with no controls")
            if not r["owner"]:
                findings.append(f"{r['id']}: no owner")
            if not r["evidence_date"] or (today - r["evidence_date"]).days > max_age_days:
                findings.append(f"{r['id']}: evidence missing or older than {max_age_days} days")
    return findings
```

**Fairness checks in code.** For a screening or ranking system, compare outcomes across groups on a labelled test set. Two simple numbers go a long way:

```python
from collections import defaultdict

def by_group(rows):
    """rows: dicts with 'group', 'selected' (bool) and 'correct' (bool)."""
    g = defaultdict(lambda: {"n": 0, "selected": 0, "correct": 0})
    for r in rows:
        s = g[r["group"]]
        s["n"] += 1; s["selected"] += r["selected"]; s["correct"] += r["correct"]
    rates = {k: v["selected"] / v["n"] for k, v in g.items()}
    top = max(rates.values())
    for k, v in g.items():
        ratio = rates[k] / top if top else 1
        flag = "  <- below four-fifths" if ratio < 0.8 else ""
        print(f"{k:<16} n={v['n']:<4} selected {rates[k]:.0%}  accuracy {v['correct'] / v['n']:.0%}  ratio {ratio:.2f}{flag}")
```

Groups worth checking in India often include gender, state or region, language of the input, college tier and urban versus rural. Small groups give noisy rates, so collect enough cases per group before drawing conclusions, and look at accuracy per group as well as selection rates.

**Link risks to tests.** Each control in the register should point to something that runs: an eval, a CI test, a dashboard alert or a scheduled review. If a control cannot be pointed at anything, it is an intention, not a control.

**Proportionality in practice.** Write down the tier for each system and what documentation that tier needs. A one-page card and a light register for low-risk tools; the full register, fairness evals, human oversight records and incident drills for high-risk ones. Revisit the tier when the use changes, such as an internal tool starting to decide things for customers.

## A worked example

A Bengaluru HR-tech company sells a resume-screening assistant to Indian employers and wants its first European client.

Classification: screening job candidates is a high-risk use under the EU AI Act, and sensitive in India too.

The team builds a register of 14 risks. Examples: unfair ranking by gender, college or region (controls: fairness evaluation across groups, removing names, photos and addresses before scoring, quarterly bias audits); over-reliance by recruiters (controls: the tool suggests a shortlist with reasons, recruiters must review every rejection, override rates are monitored); data protection (controls: DPDP and GDPR-compliant consent and retention, deletion after the hiring cycle).

They map each control to the EU AI Act's high-risk obligations and to NIST functions, and produce technical documentation, a system card and a logging design. A fairness evaluation shows a lower shortlist rate for candidates from tier-3 colleges with the same skills; the team removes college names from the scoring input and the gap narrows from 11 points to 2.

The European client's procurement team accepts the documentation pack. The same pack also shortens reviews with Indian enterprise clients.

| Finding | Change | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lower shortlist rate for tier-3 college candidates with equal skills | College names removed from scoring input | Gap narrowed from 11 points to 2 |
| Recruiters accepting shortlists without review | Every rejection must be reviewed; override rates monitored | Real oversight |
| Data kept indefinitely | Deletion after the hiring cycle | Meets DPDP and GDPR retention |

## Common mistakes

- **Treating frameworks as paperwork after the build.** Controls such as fairness testing and oversight must shape the design.
- **One level of governance for everything.** Match effort to risk.
- **Ignoring the EU AI Act because you are in India.** It applies to systems used in the EU.
- **Fairness tested only on overall accuracy.** Measure by group, including language and region.
- **Oversight in name only.** A human who cannot or does not override is not oversight.
- **Controls that point at nothing that runs.** Link each control to a test, eval or alert.
- **Fairness checked once at launch.** Data and users change; repeat it on a schedule.
- **Drawing conclusions from tiny groups.** Collect enough cases per group first.

## When a framework is overkill

- **Personal or team productivity tools** on non-sensitive data: a short card and normal security practice are enough.
- **Prototypes** that never reach real users.
- **Low-impact internal drafting tools**, where a person always reviews and owns the output.

Use the full register, mapping and evidence for systems that decide or strongly influence things about people: hiring, lending, insurance, health, education and public services.

## From demo to production

- **Keep the register in the repository** and run the review check before each release.
- **Schedule fairness evals** by group, with results stored as evidence.
- **Name an owner** for every serious risk, and review the register quarterly.
- **Record the tier** of each system and what it requires.
- **Map controls once** to the frameworks your buyers and regulators use, and reuse the mapping.
