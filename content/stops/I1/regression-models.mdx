## What it is

**Regression** predicts a number from features: a delivery time, a price, next week's demand. **Linear regression** assumes the prediction is a weighted sum of features plus a constant:

predicted delivery minutes = 6 + 2.1 × distance in km + 0.4 × items + 3 × (rain? 1 or 0)

Each weight is a **coefficient**. It says how much the prediction changes per unit of that feature, holding the others fixed. Here, one extra km adds 2.1 minutes and rain adds 3. Training finds the weights that bring predictions closest to the actual values in past data.

Other regression models (decision trees, gradient boosting, neural networks) can follow curves and interactions. Linear regression is still where you start: it is fast, it is easy to explain, and it is the baseline any complex model has to beat.

```text
  price
   |                                  *
   |                          *   *
   |                 *    * /        <- fitted line: price = a + b x feature
   |          *    *   /  *
   |     *      /  *
   |   *    /                  residual = actual - predicted
   |    /  *                   (vertical gap from point to line)
   +-------------------------------- car age, km, ...
```

The gap between a real point and the line is the **residual**. Almost everything in this lesson is about measuring residuals honestly and reading their patterns.

This lesson follows a used-car marketplace in Chennai building a suggested-price feature for sellers.

## How it works

```flow
dir: LR
title: The regression loop
Split by time -> Baseline
Baseline -> Linear model
Linear model -[read residuals]-> Fix features or target
Fix features or target -> Tree model
Tree model -[compare on latest months]-> Ship and monitor
```

### Fitting

The usual loss is **mean squared error** (MSE): the average of the squared gaps between predicted and actual values. Squaring makes a ₹2 lakh miss count four times as much as a ₹1 lakh miss, so the line bends towards large errors. Libraries such as scikit-learn fit this in one call.

### Splitting the data

Always evaluate on data the model did not train on. For anything that changes over time (prices, demand, delivery times), the test set should be the **latest** period: train on older months, test on the most recent ones. A random split mixes months, so the model has already seen the price level of the test months. Its score then looks better than it will be next month, when the market has moved. This is a form of data leakage.

### Measuring error

- **MAE** (mean absolute error): the average size of the errors, in the original units. "Off by ₹59,000 on average" is easy to explain to a business team.
- **RMSE** (root mean squared error): the square root of MSE, also in original units. It is always at least as large as MAE. A big gap between the two means a few predictions are badly wrong.
- **MAPE** (mean absolute percentage error): "off by 11% on average". Useful when values range from cheap to expensive, but it breaks when actual values are near zero.
- **R²**: the share of variation explained. It is at most 1. On test data it can be **negative**, which means the model is worse than predicting the average. It is useful for comparing models on the same data, but business users rarely find it intuitive.

| Measure | Reads as | Use when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MAE | "Off by ₹59,000 on average" | You want a number business users understand |
| RMSE | Like MAE, but big misses count more | Large errors are especially costly, such as refunds or buy-back guarantees |
| MAPE | "Off by 11% on average" | Values are never near zero and percentage error is what matters |
| R² | Share of variation explained | Comparing models on the same test data |

**A decision rule.** Pick the metric that matches the cost of a mistake. If every rupee of error costs the same, use MAE. If one ₹3 lakh miss hurts more than three ₹1 lakh misses, watch RMSE. Report the median baseline next to the model every time.

### Reading coefficients

Coefficients depend on units and on the other features in the model. When two features carry the same information (distance and travel time, or car age and kilometres), the model can trade weight between them. Then the coefficients swing from one sample to the next, even though the predictions stay similar. This is **multicollinearity**. To detect it, check correlations between features, or compute the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each one. A common rule of thumb treats VIF above 4 as worth a look and above 10 as serious. To fix it, drop or merge one of the overlapping features.

Coefficients describe association, not cause. If you want confidence intervals on them, the usual formulas assume the errors are independent of each other and have roughly the same spread everywhere. Repeat sales of the same car, or errors that grow with price, break those assumptions.

### Residual patterns and what to do

Plot the residuals against each feature and against the predicted value.

- **A random cloud:** the model has captured the pattern.
- **A curve:** a non-linear effect. Add a transformed feature (distance squared, age bands) or try a tree model.
- **A funnel** (errors grow as the price grows): errors are proportional. Model the log of the target.
- **A cluster of large errors:** a group the model treats badly, such as one brand, or a data problem.
- **A few extreme points:** squared error lets them pull the line. Investigate them first. Remove them only if they are data errors (a ₹50 listing, a car with 9,99,999 km); otherwise consider a robust loss.

### Log targets and the way back

Used-car prices are skewed: many cheap cars and a long tail of expensive ones. Training on log(price) makes the model fit percentage errors. It also turns "each year cuts the price by 11%" into a straight line, and it never predicts a negative price.

The gotcha is the way back. Taking exp of the predicted log price gives roughly the **median** price for those features, not the mean. To get the mean, multiply by the average of exp(residual) on the training data. This is called a smearing correction. The correction is small when log errors are small and grows when they are large.

### Regularisation and scaling

**Ridge** and **lasso** regression add a penalty on the size of the coefficients. This reduces overfitting when you have many features or features that overlap. Lasso can set some coefficients exactly to zero, which removes those features. The penalty strength (alpha) is chosen by cross-validation, and `RidgeCV` does this for you.

The penalty acts on coefficient size, and size depends on units. A coefficient per kilometre is tiny and a coefficient per year is large, so without scaling they are shrunk unevenly. Always put a `StandardScaler` before ridge or lasso in a pipeline. Tree models split on thresholds, so they do not need scaling.

### When to move beyond linear regression

Move to tree-based models such as gradient boosting when residual plots show curves or interactions (one effect depends on another) that you cannot fix with a few features, or when you have many mixed features. Keep the linear model in your report anyway. If the complex model wins only by a little, the simpler one may be the better product.

One limit of trees matters for prices: they cannot predict above the highest values they saw in training. A linear model can extrapolate a trend, for better or worse.

### The code: baseline, linear, log target and boosting

Synthetic used-car sales over 24 months. Prices fall with age, premium 2,000 cc cars lose value faster, and the whole market drifts up slowly. The test set is the latest 4 months.

```python
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.dummy import DummyRegressor
from sklearn.linear_model import RidgeCV
from sklearn.compose import TransformedTargetRegressor
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error, mean_squared_error, r2_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n = 8000
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "month": rng.integers(0, 24, n),      # sale month: 0 is the oldest
    "age": rng.uniform(0, 12, n),         # years
    "km": rng.uniform(5_000, 150_000, n),
    "engine_cc": rng.choice([1000, 1200, 1500, 2000], n),
    "metro": rng.integers(0, 2, n),       # 1 if sold in a metro city
})
rate = 0.10 + 0.06 * (df.engine_cc == 2000)   # premium cars lose value faster
price = (900_000 * np.exp(-rate * df.age) * (df.engine_cc / 1200)
         * (1 - df.km / 600_000) * (1 + 0.05 * df.metro) * (1 + 0.004 * df.month))
df["price"] = price * rng.lognormal(0, 0.12, n)

features = ["age", "km", "engine_cc", "metro"]
train, test = df[df.month < 20], df[df.month >= 20]   # older sales vs latest 4 months
X_tr, y_tr, X_te, y_te = train[features], train.price, test[features], test.price

alphas = np.logspace(-3, 3, 13)
models = {
    "median baseline": DummyRegressor(strategy="median"),
    "ridge": make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), RidgeCV(alphas=alphas)),
    "ridge, log target": TransformedTargetRegressor(
        make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), RidgeCV(alphas=alphas)), func=np.log, inverse_func=np.exp),
    "boosting": HistGradientBoostingRegressor(max_iter=300, random_state=5),
}
for name, m in models.items():
    pred = m.fit(X_tr, y_tr).predict(X_te)
    mae, rmse = mean_absolute_error(y_te, pred), mean_squared_error(y_te, pred) ** 0.5
    print(f"{name:<18} MAE ₹{mae:>9,.0f}  RMSE ₹{rmse:>9,.0f}  R² {r2_score(y_te, pred):6.2f}")
```

Output with scikit-learn 1.9.1:

```text
median baseline    MAE ₹  201,364  RMSE ₹  274,141  R²  -0.07
ridge              MAE ₹   97,976  RMSE ₹  129,071  R²   0.76
ridge, log target  MAE ₹   80,155  RMSE ₹  109,775  R²   0.83
boosting           MAE ₹   59,212  RMSE ₹   82,164  R²   0.90
```

Notice the baseline's R² is negative: one constant guess taken from older sales does worse than the test months' own average price.

**Errors by group.** An average can hide a segment the model gets wrong. Percentage error is fairer here, because expensive cars have larger rupee errors:

```python
lin, best = models["ridge, log target"], models["boosting"]
pct = pd.DataFrame({
    "ridge_log": np.abs(lin.predict(X_te) - y_te) / y_te,
    "boosting": np.abs(best.predict(X_te) - y_te) / y_te,
})
print((pct.groupby(X_te.engine_cc).mean() * 100).round(1))
```

```text
           ridge_log  boosting
engine_cc
1000            12.0      10.6
1200            11.9      10.7
1500            18.1      10.4
2000            17.0      11.0
```

**Reading the linear model.** Coefficients of the scaled pipeline are per standard deviation. Divide by the scaler's spread to get them back in real units:

```python
ridge = lin.regressor_
coef = ridge[-1].coef_ / ridge[0].scale_
print(f"per year: {np.expm1(coef[0]):.1%}   per 10,000 km: {np.expm1(coef[1] * 10_000):.1%}   "
      f"metro: {np.expm1(coef[3]):+.1%}   alpha chosen: {ridge[-1].alpha_:.2f}")
```

```text
per year: -10.8%   per 10,000 km: -1.8%   metro: +5.6%   alpha chosen: 0.32
```

**A price range instead of a point.** Sellers and buyers trust a band more than one number:

```python
q = dict(loss="quantile", max_iter=300, random_state=5)
lo = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(quantile=0.1, **q).fit(X_tr, y_tr).predict(X_te)
hi = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(quantile=0.9, **q).fit(X_tr, y_tr).predict(X_te)
print(f"inside the 10-90% band: {((y_te >= lo) & (y_te <= hi)).mean():.0%}   "
      f"above the top: {(y_te > hi).mean():.0%}")
```

```text
inside the 10-90% band: 66%   above the top: 25%
```

A well-calibrated 10 to 90% band holds about 80% of real prices, with about 10% above it. This one holds 66%, and a quarter of real prices sit above the top. Two things cause it. Even on a random split this band holds only about 70%, because quantile models are often too narrow. The time split makes it worse, because prices rose after the training months. Widen the band using a recent validation month, and retrain often, before showing ranges to users.

**Guard the edges.** Record the range of each feature in the training data. Flag requests outside it (a 25-year-old car, 4,00,000 km) instead of returning a confident price.

## A worked example

The Chennai marketplace team uses the code above as its first experiment. The data is synthetic, so the numbers are illustrations of the method, not results from a real market. Read the outputs in order.

1. **Baseline.** Predicting the median price every time gives MAE ₹2,01,364. Any useful model must beat this by a wide margin.
2. **Linear regression** (scaled, ridge) halves the error to ₹97,976. Cross-validation chose a small alpha, so the penalty barely changed anything here: with four features and thousands of rows, there is little to overfit.
3. **Log target.** Prices fall by a percentage each year, which is a curve in rupees but a straight line in log price. MAE drops to ₹80,155. The model also gives a readable story: about 10.8% less per year of age, 1.8% less per 10,000 km, and 5.6% more in metro cities.
4. **Error analysis.** The group table shows the linear model's percentage error jumps from about 12% to 17 to 18% for 1,500 cc and 2,000 cc cars. Two things cause this. Engine size does not have a straight-line effect on log price, and premium cars depreciate faster, which is an interaction a plain linear model cannot represent.
5. **Gradient boosting** learns both effects from the data. MAE falls to ₹59,212, about 11% error in every engine group, and R² rises to 0.90.
6. **Honesty checks.** On the training months, the boosted model's MAE is about ₹40,500, far below the ₹59,212 on the latest months. A random split instead of a time split gives about ₹55,000, which looks better than reality. The price band covers only 66% of real prices, not 80%.

| Model | Test MAE | Test RMSE | What it taught the team |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Median baseline | ₹2,01,364 | ₹2,74,141 | The bar to beat |
| Ridge, scaled | ₹97,976 | ₹1,29,071 | A line misses the curve in age |
| Ridge, log price | ₹80,155 | ₹1,09,775 | Percentage effects; readable coefficients |
| Gradient boosting | ₹59,212 | ₹82,164 | Captures the premium-car interaction |

**What ships.** The boosted model sets the suggested price, shown as a widened range. The log-linear coefficients feed the explanation shown to sellers ("each extra year usually lowers the price by about 11%"), clearly labelled as approximate. Using two models for two jobs is common: the most accurate one for the number, and a simpler one for an honest explanation. The team retrains monthly, because the drift showed that last quarter's prices go stale.

## Common mistakes

- **No baseline.** Without "always predict the median" next to your model, you cannot tell whether it adds value.
- **Evaluating on training data.** Errors look far smaller than reality, as the ₹40,500 against ₹59,212 gap shows.
- **A random split on time-ordered data.** Market prices drift. Train on earlier months and test on the latest ones.
- **Treating coefficients as causes.** They describe association under the model's assumptions, and they swing when features overlap.
- **Ridge or lasso without scaling.** The penalty then depends on units, not on how useful a feature is.
- **Ignoring skewed targets.** Predicting log price often works better, but remember that exp of the prediction is near the median, not the mean.
- **Trusting extrapolations.** Predictions outside the training range are guesses.
- **Reporting only R², or only overall MAE.** Business users understand "off by ₹59,000 on average", and a single average can hide a segment with large errors.
- **Point predictions for decisions that need a range**, such as price advice, or ranges whose coverage nobody checked.

## When a regression model is not needed

- **A simple formula already exists**, such as a tariff table or a published rate card.
- **Too little data** to learn from: a rule of thumb from domain experts may be more reliable.
- **The question is a category**, not a number, such as "will it sell this week": use classification.

## From demo to production

- **Keep the baseline in every report.**
- **Check errors by segment** and feature range on each retrain.
- **Flag out-of-range inputs** instead of predicting.
- **Offer ranges** where decisions depend on uncertainty, and check their coverage every month.
- **Monitor errors as real outcomes arrive**, and retrain when the market drifts.
