## What it is

**Regression** predicts a number from features. **Linear regression** assumes the prediction is a weighted sum of features plus a constant:

predicted delivery minutes = 6 + 2.1 × distance in km + 0.4 × items + 3 × (rain? 1 or 0)

The weights (coefficients) say how much the prediction changes per unit of each feature, holding the others fixed. Training finds the weights that make predictions closest to actual values on past data.

Other regression models (decision trees, gradient boosting, neural networks) can capture curves and interactions, but linear regression is the baseline to beat and the easiest to explain.

```text
  price
   |                                  *
   |                          *   *
   |                 *    * /        <- fitted line: price = a + b x feature
   |          *    *   /  *
   |     *      /  *
   |   *    /                  residual = actual - predicted
   |    /  *                   (vertical gap from point to line)
   +-------------------------------- car age, km, ...
```

This lesson follows a used-car marketplace in Chennai building a suggested-price feature for sellers.

## How it works

**Fitting.** The usual loss is mean squared error: the average of squared differences between predicted and actual values. Squaring punishes large errors heavily. Libraries such as scikit-learn fit this in one call.

**Evaluating.** Always on data the model did not train on.

- **MAE** (mean absolute error): average size of errors, in the original units ("off by 3.2 minutes on average"). Easy to explain.
- **RMSE** (root mean squared error): like MAE but penalises large errors more.
- **R²**: share of variation explained, from 0 (no better than predicting the average) to 1. Useful but not intuitive for business users.

Compare with a simple baseline, such as predicting the median every time. If your model barely beats it, it is not adding much.

**Reading coefficients.** Coefficients depend on units and on other features. Correlated features (distance and travel time) make coefficients unstable. Do not read them as causes.

**Non-linearity.** Real relationships curve: delivery time may rise slowly up to 5 km, then jump. Add transformed features (distance squared, buckets), or use tree-based models.

**Outliers.** Squared error lets a few extreme values pull the fit. Investigate them; cap or remove data errors; consider MAE-based or robust methods.

**Extrapolation.** Linear models will happily predict beyond the range they saw (deliveries of 40 km when training had at most 12). Those predictions are unreliable.

**Regularisation.** Ridge and lasso regression shrink coefficients to reduce overfitting when there are many features; lasso can set some to zero.

**Choosing an error measure:**

| Measure | Reads as | Use when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MAE | "Off by ₹29,000 on average" | You want a number business users understand |
| RMSE | Like MAE, but big misses count more | Large errors are especially costly |
| MAPE | "Off by 6% on average" | Values are never near zero |
| R² | Share of variation explained | Comparing models on the same data |

**Reading residuals.** Plot the errors against each feature. A random cloud means the model has captured the pattern. A curve, a funnel or a cluster means it has missed something: a non-linear effect, a group it treats badly, or a data problem.

**When to move beyond linear regression.** Linear regression is fast, explainable and a strong baseline. Move to tree-based models such as gradient boosting when residual plots show curves or interactions the line cannot capture, when you have many features of mixed types, or when the accuracy gain is worth the extra complexity. Keep the linear model running in your evaluation anyway: if a complex model only beats it by a little, the simpler one may be the better product choice.

## A worked example

A Chennai used-car marketplace wants to suggest listing prices.

Data: 18,000 past sales with car age, kilometres driven, brand, model, fuel type, city and number of owners.

1. Baseline: predict the median price for the model and year. MAE ₹68,000.
2. Linear regression on the features (with brand and model one-hot encoded, and log of price as the target because prices are skewed). MAE ₹41,000.
3. Error analysis: errors are largest for premium cars and cars over 12 years old. A plot of residuals against age shows a curve.
4. Gradient-boosted trees on the same features. MAE ₹29,000.

The team ships the boosted model for prices but keeps the linear model's coefficients for the explanation shown to sellers ("each extra year reduces price by about 9% for this model"), clearly labelled as approximate.

| Model | MAE | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Median price for the model and year | ₹68,000 | Baseline |
| Linear regression, log price | ₹41,000 | Explainable coefficients |
| Gradient-boosted trees | ₹29,000 | Captures curves for old and premium cars |

Shipping two models for two jobs is common: the most accurate one for the number, and a simpler one for an honest, approximate explanation.

## Common mistakes

- **No baseline.** You cannot judge a model without one.
- **Evaluating on training data.** Errors look far smaller than reality.
- **Treating coefficients as causes.** They describe association under the model's assumptions.
- **Ignoring skewed targets.** Predicting the log of prices often works better.
- **Trusting extrapolations.** Predictions outside the training range are guesses.

- **Reporting only R².** Business users understand "off by ₹29,000 on average" far better than "R² of 0.87". Report MAE in rupees or minutes alongside it.
