## What it is

An AI product manager interview checks whether you can decide what AI feature to build, prove it is good enough, and keep it safe and affordable once real users arrive. Most of it looks like a classic PM interview. The difference is that AI output is probabilistic: the same feature is right most of the time and confidently wrong some of the time. So interviewers listen for three habits that classic PM answers often skip:

1. **Design for wrong answers.** What does the user see when the model is wrong, and how do they get to a person?
2. **Measure quality before launch.** An eval on real cases and a launch bar agreed in advance, not "the demo looked good".
3. **Know the unit economics.** Every request costs money in tokens, and every wrong action can cost more.

The rounds vary by company, and their order is not fixed. A common set:

| Round | Typical question | What a good answer shows |
|---|---|---|
| Product sense | "Design an AI feature for a bus-ticket app." | A chosen user problem, a reason AI fits, and a plan for failure |
| Metrics and evals | "Your eval says 93%. Do you launch?" | A metric tied to the goal, a launch bar, error severity |
| Technical depth | "Retrieval or fine-tuning?" | Plain trade-offs: freshness, cost, latency, effort |
| Strategy | "Build on an API or train our own model?" | Cost per request, differentiation, vendor risk |
| Responsible AI | "Who could this hurt?" | Named harms, tests for them, where humans stay in control |
| Behavioural | "Tell me about a launch that went wrong." | A STAR story with real numbers and a lesson |

Some companies also send a take-home assignment, covered below. This lesson follows Kavya, who is preparing for AI PM interviews in Bengaluru, through a full design answer with numbers.

## How it works

### A structure for design questions

1. *Clarify*: the user, the business goal, and constraints such as languages, phones, connectivity and regulation. If the interviewer will not choose a goal, state an assumption and move on.
2. *User problems*: list three or four, pick one, and say why (how often it happens, how painful it is, what it is worth).
3. *Is AI the right tool?* Name the simpler option you considered, such as a form, rules or better search, and why it falls short.
4. *Experience*: step by step, including what happens when the AI is wrong: show sources, confirm before acting, let users correct it, hand over to a person.
5. *How it works*: data needed, retrieval or not, which model for which step, where a human reviews.
6. *Metrics*: one primary metric, supporting metrics, guardrails, and the launch bar.
7. *Risks*: quality, cost, privacy, fairness and misuse, each with a mitigation.
8. *Launch plan*: offline eval, shadow mode, a small pilot with a control group, and the result that would make you stop.

In a 25-minute design round, a balanced answer spends time like this:

| Step | Minutes | What the interviewer listens for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Clarify | 2 | Users, goal and constraints before ideas |
| 2. User problems | 3 | One problem chosen, with reasons |
| 3. Is AI the right tool? | 2 | A simpler option considered honestly |
| 4. Experience | 6 | A clear flow, including when the AI is wrong |
| 5. How it works | 3 | Data, retrieval, model choice, oversight |
| 6. Metrics | 4 | A metric that follows from the goal, and a launch bar |
| 7. Risks | 3 | Specific harms, with mitigations |
| 8. Launch plan | 2 | Pilot, control group, stop rules |

Many candidates spend fifteen minutes on the experience and never reach metrics or risks, which is where AI PM judgement shows most. Glance at the clock after step 4.

### Metrics: start from the goal

Pick metrics in three layers, in this order:

- **Primary (product outcome)**: the user result that proves the goal was met, for example repeat orders, completed applications or time saved.
- **Supporting (AI behaviour)**: deflection rate (share of cases closed without a person), escalation rate (share handed to a person), correct-resolution rate on a sampled set.
- **Guardrails**: things that must not get worse, such as complaints, wrong actions, cost per request and fairness across languages or regions.

The trap is promoting a supporting metric to primary. Deflection rate rises if the assistant simply makes it hard to reach a person. Ask yourself: "Could this number go up while users are worse off?" If yes, it is not your primary metric.

### Evals and the launch bar

An eval is a repeatable test: a fixed set of real cases, each with a correct outcome, scored the same way every time. In an interview, say four things:

1. **Where the cases come from.** Past real cases, sampled to include hard ones, every language you support and rare but serious types.
2. **Who labels them.** Domain experts, such as senior support agents, with two labellers per case and a lead resolving disagreements. Their agreement rate tells you how hard the task is even for people.
3. **How you score at scale.** An LLM-as-judge (a model that grades outputs against a rubric) can score thousands of outputs, but check a sample by hand and launch only if the judge agrees with humans.
4. **The bar itself, set before testing.** A target score, compared with today's baseline (often the human team), plus zero tolerance for named critical errors. An overall 93% means nothing if the remaining 7% includes the errors that hurt people.

The bar then gates each step of the launch:

```flow
dir: LR
title: From eval to rollout
Offline eval | labelled real cases -[meets bar]-> Shadow mode | AI suggests, humans decide
Offline eval -[misses bar]-> Fix and re-test
Shadow mode -[agrees with humans]-> Pilot | 5% of users, control group
Pilot -[metrics hold]-> Wider rollout
Pilot -[guardrail breached]-> Roll back
```

### Cost per request

You do not need exact prices in an interview, but you need the shape of the sum:

> cost per request = (input tokens x input price + output tokens x output price) / 1,000,000

Blend it across the models you route to (a small, cheap model for easy cases, a larger one for hard cases), multiply by daily volume, and compare with two things: the cost of the human baseline and the cost of the AI's mistakes. The second comparison is the one most candidates miss, and the worked example shows why it matters. If you quote a real price, say where it is from and the date you checked it; prices change often.

### Technical depth

You are not expected to code. You are expected to explain trade-offs in plain words. For example: "The scheme rules change every few weeks, so I would use retrieval over the current documents with citations. Fine-tuning would freeze old rules into the model and need retraining at every change." Other common topics: why models hallucinate, latency versus quality, what an agent is and why it needs limits, and what a context window allows.

### Responsible AI, with Indian rules in mind

Show you can name harm and still ship. Who could be hurt? How would you test for it? Where does a human decide? What would you refuse to build? Know the rules that touch common case studies:

- The **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023** covers digital personal data. Its Rules were notified in November 2025, and most duties, including notice and consent, were given an 18-month lead time, so consent and purpose limits belong in any design that uses customer data.
- **Investment advice** is regulated by SEBI: paid advice generally needs registration as an investment adviser, and the exemptions are narrow. The safe default for a broking app's assistant is to explain, not recommend.
- **Languages and devices** are a fairness issue: test quality separately for each language and for slow connections, not just on the overall average.

### Handling pushback

Follow-ups such as "Costs went up 20%" or "Users don't trust it" test how you think under pressure. A good shape: restate the problem, ask or assume what data you have, diagnose before acting (volume, mix or leakage?), make one specific change, and say what result would change your mind. Never abandon your design at the first push, and never defend it without data.

### Behavioural stories

Prepare five STAR stories (Situation, Task, Action, Result): a launch, a failure, a disagreement with engineering, a time you said no, and a decision with incomplete data. Here is the shape of a strong AI story from someone moving into PM. The numbers are illustrative; use your own real ones.

- *Situation*: claims staff at an insurer in Chennai spent four days sorting about 2,000 emails a week.
- *Task*: I owned a pilot to route emails with AI without misrouting urgent hospital cases.
- *Action*: two senior claims staff labelled 300 past emails. We set the bar first: 90% correct routing and zero misrouted urgent cases. We ran in shadow mode for two weeks.
- *Result*: sorting time fell to same day for most emails. Shadow mode showed mixed Tamil and English emails were routed correctly only 70% of the time, so we sent those to people until the model improved.

The caught failure is what makes the story believable.

### Take-home assignments

Some companies send a case to solve at home: a short PRD (product requirements document), a teardown of an existing product, or a strategy question with a deck, usually followed by a live presentation and questions. Ask the recruiter how much time they expect and stay close to it. A strong submission is short and decision-led: the user and problem, one recommendation with the options you rejected, metrics and a launch bar, risks, and a list of assumptions. Using AI tools to draft is common, but you must defend every line live.

## A worked example

Question: "Design an AI feature for a popular Indian food-delivery app."

Kavya's answer, abridged:

*Clarify*: "Users are in metros and smaller cities, use many languages and mostly phones. Is the goal order frequency or satisfaction?" The interviewer says "you choose". Kavya: "I will assume the goal is to keep customers who had a bad order, because they are the most likely to leave."

*Problem*: she lists choosing what to eat, dietary needs and post-order issues, and picks post-order issues (missing items, wrong items, delays): frequent, painful, and expensive for support.

*Why AI*: "Complaints arrive as free text and photos in several languages. A form forces users into fixed categories and still needs an agent to read the photo. AI can read both and resolve routine cases at once."

*Experience*: the assistant reads the complaint and photo, checks order data, and offers a refund up to a limit. It shows exactly what will happen before the user confirms. It works in Hindi, Tamil and English, and anything unusual goes to a person in one tap.

*Metrics*: this is where the clarifying assumption pays off.

- Primary: share of customers who reported an issue and ordered again within 14 days. This follows directly from her stated goal.
- Supporting: median time to resolution, deflection rate, escalation rate.
- Guardrails: wrong-refund rate (refunds an expert would not approve), wrong-denial rate, refund cost per order, satisfaction, and the same metrics split by language.

*Launch bar*: two senior support agents label 500 past complaints with the correct outcome, and the support lead settles disagreements. The bar, agreed before testing, is to match the human team's correct-resolution rate on the same set. Separately, there must be zero automatic denials on food-safety complaints such as a foreign object or an allergen; those always go to a person.

*Cost*: Kavya does a back-of-envelope sum and says clearly that every number is an assumption for illustration:

| Item | Assumption | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Volume | 50,000 complaints a day, 60% handled by AI | 30,000 AI conversations a day |
| Tokens | 6,000 input and 600 output per conversation | |
| Small model, 80% of cases | Suppose $0.25 and $1.50 per million input and output tokens | $0.0024 per conversation |
| Larger model, 20% of cases | Suppose $2 and $12 per million | $0.0192 per conversation |
| Blended model cost | About ₹90 to the dollar | About ₹0.52, call it ₹1 with retries and checks |
| Model cost per day | 30,000 x ₹1 | ₹30,000 |
| Human baseline | Suppose ₹35 per ticket | ₹10.5 lakh for the same 30,000 tickets |
| Wrong refunds | Suppose 3% of the 30,000 get a wrong refund, ₹150 average | ₹1.35 lakh a day |

Her conclusion: "Token cost is small. Each one-point rise in the wrong-refund rate costs about ₹45,000 a day, more than the whole model bill. So the launch decision depends on the wrong-refund rate, and that is the guardrail I would watch most closely." This single insight is what separates an AI PM answer from a generic one.

*Risks*: refund fraud (limits for new accounts, photo checks, account history), wrong denials (one-tap hand-over), quality gaps by language (separate eval slices), and privacy of photos and addresses (keep only what the complaint needs).

*Launch*: shadow mode first, then 5% of orders in two cities against a control group. Stop if the wrong-refund rate or food-safety handling breaks its bar.

The interviewer pushes back: "Refund costs rose 20% in the pilot. What now?" Kavya diagnoses before acting: "Is it more complaints, a different mix, or leakage? I would split refunds by account age and complaint type, and compare with the control group. If the rise is in new accounts, I would lower their auto-refund limit and require a photo. I would not cut automation if the 14-day repeat-order rate is up enough to pay for it. I would change my mind if the control group shows the same rise, because then the cause is not the AI."

## Common mistakes

- **Jumping to a solution.** Clarify, state an assumption about the goal, and pick a problem first.
- **AI for its own sake.** Say what simpler option you considered and why it falls short.
- **No plan for wrong answers.** Every design needs confirmation before action, and an easy route to a person.
- **A gameable primary metric.** Chats, sessions and deflection can rise while users suffer. The primary metric must follow from the goal.
- **"95% accurate" with no context.** Say on which cases, compared with what baseline, and which errors are never acceptable.
- **A launch bar set after the results.** Agree the bar with domain experts before testing, or it means nothing.
- **Token price without the cost of errors.** Model cost is often the smallest number in the sum.
- **"We will use the latest model."** A bigger model is not a quality plan; an eval is.
- **Averages that hide groups.** Check quality for each language, region and device.
- **Jargon without understanding.** Explain the trade-off in plain words, or leave the term out.

## Practice

Revisit the PAIR Guidebook for designing around AI errors, SVPG's four big risks for product judgement, and the Tech Interview Handbook's behavioural guide.

1. Answer three design questions out loud, 25 minutes each, using the eight steps. Try an AI feature for a government services app, for a hospital's patient app and for a stock-broking app. Record yourself and check the clock after step 4.
2. For each, write the stated goal, the primary metric, two guardrails, the launch bar with one critical error, and a cost-per-request sum with your assumptions labelled.
3. Ask a friend to push back with one follow-up per answer, and practise the diagnose-then-act shape.
4. Prepare five STAR stories with numbers, at least one involving an AI or data decision, and tell each in under two minutes.

**You are done when you can** answer an unseen AI product design question in 25 minutes with a clear structure, a metric that follows from the goal, a launch bar, a cost estimate and a calm answer to pushback.
