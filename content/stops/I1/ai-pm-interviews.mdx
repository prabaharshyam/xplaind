## What it is

AI product manager loops usually include:

- **Product sense / design**: "Design an AI feature for X" or "Improve Y with AI."
- **Metrics and evaluation**: "How would you measure success?", "How do you know the AI is good enough to launch?"
- **Technical depth**: enough to work with engineers: RAG versus fine-tuning, latency and cost trade-offs, why models hallucinate, what an agent is.
- **Strategy and business**: build versus buy, pricing, competition, unit economics.
- **Responsible AI**: bias, privacy, safety, regulation, when not to use AI.
- **Behavioural**: past situations showing judgement, influence and learning.

## How it works

**A structure for AI product design questions.**

1. *Clarify*: the user, the goal, constraints (market, platform, languages, regulation).
2. *User problems*: list them, pick one with reasons (frequency, pain, value).
3. *Is AI the right tool?* Say why, and what simpler options you considered.
4. *Solution*: the experience, step by step, including what happens when the AI is wrong (sources, confidence, correction, hand-over).
5. *How it works*: briefly: data needed, retrieval or not, model choice, human oversight.
6. *Metrics*: one primary metric, guardrail metrics, an offline evaluation plan and online signals.
7. *Risks*: quality, cost, privacy, fairness, misuse, and mitigations.
8. *Launch plan*: prototype, pilot, rollout, and what would make you stop.

**Metrics questions.** Separate model quality (accuracy on a golden set, faithfulness) from product outcomes (task completion, time saved, retention, revenue) and guardrails (complaints, escalations, cost per user). Explain how you set the launch bar with experts and data.

**Technical questions.** Be clear and honest: explain trade-offs, not jargon. "We would start with prompting and retrieval because the facts change weekly; fine-tuning would freeze outdated knowledge."

**Responsible AI questions.** Show you can spot harm and still ship: who could be hurt, how you would test for it, where humans stay in control, what you would not build.

**Behavioural.** Prepare STAR stories with numbers: a launch, a failure, a disagreement with engineering, a time you said no to a stakeholder, a decision with incomplete data.

**Practise out loud**, timed, with a friend or by recording yourself. Interviewers judge structure and judgement more than the "right" answer.

## A worked example

Question: "Design an AI feature for a popular Indian food-delivery app."

A strong answer by Kavya, abridged:

*Clarify*: users in metros and tier-2 cities, many languages, phone-first; goal is order frequency or satisfaction?

*Problem*: she lists choosing what to order, delivery issues and dietary needs, and picks "post-order issues" (missing items, delays): frequent, painful, costly for support.

*Why AI*: complaints are varied free text and photos; AI can understand them and resolve routine cases instantly.

*Solution*: in-app support assistant that reads the complaint and photo, checks order data, offers refunds within a limit automatically, hands over anything unusual, works in Hindi, Tamil and English, and shows exactly what will happen before confirming.

*Metrics*: primary, median time to resolution; guardrails, refund cost per order, repeat complaints, fraud rate, satisfaction; offline evaluation on 500 labelled past complaints, launch bar set with the support team.

*Risks*: refund fraud (limits, photo checks, account history), wrong refusals (easy hand-over), cost (small model for classification, larger for hard cases).

*Launch*: 5% of orders in two cities, compare with control, expand if metrics hold.

The interviewer's follow-up: "What if refund costs rise 20%?" Kavya discusses tightening limits for new accounts and checking for fraud patterns before cutting automation.

## Common mistakes

- **Jumping to a solution.** Clarify and pick a problem first.
- **AI for its own sake.** Say why AI beats simpler options.
- **Ignoring failure.** Design what happens when the AI is wrong.
- **Model metrics only.** Tie success to user and business outcomes, with guardrails.
- **Jargon without understanding.** Explain trade-offs plainly.

## Practise

Revisit the PAIR Guidebook and SVPG's four big risks, and the Tech Interview Handbook's behavioural guide.

1. Answer three design questions out loud in 25 minutes each using the eight-step structure (for example: AI for a government services app, for a hospital's patient app, for a stock-broking app).
2. For each, write the primary metric, two guardrails and the launch bar.
3. Prepare five STAR stories with numbers, and practise each in under two minutes.

**You are done when you can** answer an unseen AI product design question in 25 minutes with a clear structure, metrics and risks.
