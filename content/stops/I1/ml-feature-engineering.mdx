## What it is

**Feature engineering** creates model inputs from raw data. A transactions table does not directly tell a churn model much; features such as "orders in the last 30 days", "change in monthly spend versus the previous quarter" and "days since last order" do.

Good features capture what a domain expert would look at.

## How it works

**Aggregates over time windows.** Counts, sums, averages, maximums over the last 7, 30 or 90 days. Trends: this month versus last month. Recency: days since the last event.

**Ratios and differences.** EMI to income, spend per visit, returns per order, price relative to category average.

**Date and time parts.** Day of week, hour, month, festival periods (Diwali, Onam, Eid, Pongal), salary days at month start. Indian demand and payment patterns are strongly seasonal.

**Categories.** One-hot encoding for few values; frequency or target encoding for many (with target encoding computed only on training folds to avoid leakage); grouping rare categories into "other".

**Text.** Simple: length, keyword flags. Stronger: embeddings from a language model, used as features.

**Location.** Distance to a store or hub, PIN code characteristics, city tier.

**Leakage.** The cardinal rule: a feature must use only information available at the moment of prediction. Compute every aggregate "as of" the prediction time. Features that quietly include future data (a refund recorded after the order you are scoring) make models look brilliant in testing and fail in production.

**Training-serving consistency.** Features must be computed the same way when training and when serving. Write feature code once and reuse it, or use a feature store. Mismatches (a timezone difference, a different definition of "active") silently degrade production models.

**Selection.** More features are not always better. Remove useless or redundant ones; check importance and stability across time.

## A worked example

A Bengaluru food-delivery company predicts whether a customer will order again within 14 days.

Raw data: orders, ratings, app sessions, support tickets and promotions.

Features built "as of" each prediction date:

- orders in the last 7, 30 and 90 days; days since last order;
- average order value and its change versus the previous 30 days;
- share of orders with a delay over 15 minutes in the last 30 days;
- last rating given; support tickets in the last 30 days;
- weekend share of orders; whether the customer usually orders on payday week;
- city tier and distance to the nearest cloud kitchen cluster.

Results: the baseline with only order counts reached AUC 0.72; with engineered features, 0.81. The most valuable new features were the delay share and the change in order value.

A leakage scare: "customer contacted support in the next 7 days" had appeared in an early draft through a misaligned join; it was found because the validation score jumped to 0.95, which was too good to be true.

## Common mistakes

- **Future information in features.** Compute everything as of prediction time.
- **Different code for training and serving.** Share feature code or use a feature store.
- **Target encoding on all data.** Compute it within training folds only.
- **Ignoring Indian calendar effects.** Festivals and salary cycles matter.
- **Too-good-to-be-true scores.** Investigate them for leakage.

## Practise

Complete Kaggle's Feature Engineering course and read the feature engineering section of Google's Rules of ML.

1. From a transactions dataset, build ten time-windowed features computed as of a chosen date, with no future data.
2. Measure the model's improvement from your features over a simple baseline.
3. Deliberately add a leaky feature, see the score jump, and write a test that would catch it.

**You are done when you can** explain how each feature is computed as of prediction time, and how training and serving compute it identically.
