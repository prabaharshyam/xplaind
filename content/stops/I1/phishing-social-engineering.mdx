## What it is

**Social engineering** is attacking people instead of computers. The attacker uses trust, urgency, fear, authority or helpfulness to get someone to do what they want: click a link, open a file, type a password, read out an OTP, approve a payment.

**Phishing** is social engineering delivered by message: email, SMS, WhatsApp, LinkedIn, Teams, a phone call or even a QR code on a poster. In MITRE ATT&CK it is technique **T1566 Phishing** under Initial Access, because so many intrusions start there. Joint guidance from CISA, NSA, FBI and MS-ISAC notes that phishing usually aims at one of two things: stealing login credentials or getting malware onto a machine.

Everyone in India has seen the mass version: "Your electricity will be cut tonight, call this number", "Your KYC has expired, click here", "Your parcel is held at customs, pay a small fee". Security engineers deal with these, but also with the targeted versions aimed at companies.

| Type | Channel | How it works | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Phishing | Email, SMS, chat | Generic lure sent to many people | Fake bank KYC update link |
| Spear phishing | Email, LinkedIn | Personalised to one person or team | "Hi Priya, here is the revised Q3 vendor list you asked for" |
| Whaling | Email | Spear phishing aimed at senior leaders | Fake legal notice sent to the CFO |
| Business email compromise (BEC) | Email | Impersonate or take over a trusted account to redirect money | "Supplier" asks to pay the invoice to a new bank account |
| Vishing | Voice call | Caller poses as bank, police, IT helpdesk or boss | "IT support" asks you to read out your MFA code |
| Smishing | SMS, WhatsApp | Short message with a link or number | "Your account is blocked, update PAN here" |
| Quishing | QR code | QR leads to a fake login or payment page | Sticker over a real QR at a parking meter |

## How it works

**Why people fall for it.** Lures press on the same few buttons: **urgency** ("today", "within 2 hours"), **authority** (CEO, police, tax department), **fear** (account blocked, legal action), **reward** (refund, prize, job offer) and **familiarity** (a known supplier or colleague). Being busy, on a phone, or outside office hours makes everyone more vulnerable. This is why "just train users better" is never enough on its own.

**Defence in layers.** Good phishing defence assumes some messages will get through and some people will click, and limits the damage.

```flow
Message arrives -> Authentication checks | SPF, DKIM, DMARC on the sender's domain
Authentication checks -> Filtering | known bad links, attachments, look-alike domains
Filtering -> Inbox with warnings | external sender banner, first-time sender tag
Inbox with warnings -> User suspicious?
User suspicious? -[yes]-> Report button | one click sends it to the security team
User suspicious? -[no, clicks]-> Phishing-resistant MFA and payment checks | limit the harm
Report button -> SOC triage | real phish or harmless?
SOC triage -> Remove from all mailboxes, block IOCs | feedback to the reporter
```

**Email authentication: SPF, DKIM and DMARC.** Email was designed without proof of who sent it, so anyone could send a message "from" your domain. Three DNS records fix that for your domain:

- **SPF** (Sender Policy Framework) lists which servers may send mail for your domain.
- **DKIM** (DomainKeys Identified Mail) has your mail server sign each message; receivers check the signature using a public key in your DNS. It proves the domain sent it and the content was not changed.
- **DMARC** (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance) says: the visible From domain must match a domain that passed SPF or DKIM, and here is what to do if it does not. It also sends you reports of who is sending mail in your name.

```text
example.in.                       TXT  "v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com -all"
google._domainkey.example.in.     TXT  "v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIIBIjANBgkq...(public key)"
_dmarc.example.in.                TXT  "v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.in"
```

Roll out DMARC in steps: start with `p=none` to receive reports without blocking anything, find every legitimate service sending as you (your CRM, billing tool, HR system), add them to SPF or set up DKIM for them, then move to `p=quarantine` and finally `p=reject`. Large mailbox providers such as Gmail and Yahoo now expect bulk senders to have these records in place.

Know the limit: these records stop **spoofing of your exact domain**. They do not stop look-alike domains (examp1e.in), and they do not stop mail from a **genuine but compromised** account, which is how much BEC works.

**Business email compromise.** BEC often has no link and no attachment, so filters see nothing wrong. The attacker either takes over a real mailbox (often by phishing it first) or uses a look-alike domain, then sends a normal-looking request to change bank details, pay an urgent invoice or buy gift cards. The defence is **process**, not technology:

- Any new payee or bank change is verified by **calling back on a number already on file**, never one in the message.
- **Two people** approve payments above a set amount.
- Rules flag mail from new look-alike domains and external replies that pretend to be internal.

**Vishing and OTP theft.** Callers posing as a bank, telecom provider, courier, police or the IT helpdesk ask for OTPs, remote-access app installs or "verification" payments over UPI. Indian authorities have repeatedly warned about fake police "digital arrest" calls; no real agency arrests people over video call or asks for money to "clear" them. For companies, the rule is simple: the helpdesk never asks for your password or MFA code, and anyone asking for one is an attacker.

**Phishing-resistant MFA.** SMS OTPs and app codes help, but a fake login page can relay them to the attacker in real time. **Passkeys** and **FIDO2 security keys** are tied to the real website's domain, so they will not work on a fake one. CISA's phishing guidance recommends phishing-resistant MFA. Start with admins and finance staff, who are the most valuable targets.

**User reporting.** Make reporting one click (a "Report phishing" button in Gmail or Outlook), thank people every time, and tell them the outcome. The first report of a campaign lets the SOC search all mailboxes, remove the same message everywhere and block its links and sender. Measure **report rate**, not just click rate: a team that clicks 5% but reports 60% within minutes is well defended. Individuals in India can report fraud calls and messages through the Sanchar Saathi portal's Chakshu facility, and cyber fraud on the national helpline **1930** or cybercrime.gov.in.

**How AI changes phishing.** The old advice, "look for spelling mistakes", no longer works. Language models write fluent, personalised messages in English, Hindi, Tamil or any language, at scale, using details scraped from LinkedIn or company websites. Voice cloning can make a "CFO" call sound right, and deepfake video can join a meeting. The answer is to lean harder on controls that do not depend on the message looking wrong: email authentication, phishing-resistant MFA, callback rules for payments and a culture of reporting. The lesson **AI-powered attacks and how defenders respond**, in the "AI for defenders" stage, goes deeper on AI-generated phishing and voice cloning.

## A worked example

A Coimbatore textile exporter with 120 staff gets an email from its yarn supplier's real address: "We have moved banks. Please pay invoice 2311 for 18 lakh rupees to the new account today." The invoice looks perfect. A junior accountant is about to pay.

What saved them: a rule, added after a near miss last year, that any bank change needs a call to the supplier on the number in the vendor master, plus approval from the finance manager. The call reveals the supplier changed nothing; their mailbox had been compromised a week earlier through a fake "shared document" login page.

The company then fixes its own exposure:

| Control | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SPF | Present | Present, ends in -all |
| DKIM | Not set | Set for Google Workspace and the billing tool |
| DMARC | None | p=none for 4 weeks with reports, then p=reject |
| MFA | SMS codes | Passkeys for finance and admins; app prompts for others |
| Payee or bank change | Email approval | Callback on known number plus second approver |
| Reporting | Forward to IT | One-click report button; SOC removes copies from all mailboxes |

They also warn the supplier, share the attacker's look-alike domain and IP with their email provider as IOCs, and record the event in their incident log.

## Common mistakes

- **Relying on training alone.** People are busy and AI-written lures look perfect. Training helps; controls and process carry the load.
- **Shaming people who click.** They stop reporting, and you lose your best sensor.
- **Leaving DMARC at p=none for ever.** Reports alone do not stop spoofing. Move to quarantine and reject once legitimate senders are fixed.
- **Thinking DMARC stops BEC.** It does not stop mail from a real, compromised account or a look-alike domain.
- **SMS OTP as the only MFA for admins.** It can be phished in real time. Use passkeys or security keys.
- **Verifying a request using contact details from the request itself.** Always use a number or address you already had.

2. Look up the SPF and DMARC records of three Indian company domains you know with a DNS lookup tool or the command `dig TXT _dmarc.example.in`. Note which have p=reject.
3. Write a one-page "payment change" procedure for a small business, with the callback and second-approver steps.
