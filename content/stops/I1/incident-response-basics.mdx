## What it is

A **security incident** is an event that actually or potentially harms the confidentiality, integrity or availability of systems or data: a stolen password used to log in, ransomware on a laptop, customer data posted online, a website defaced. An alert from your SIEM is not yet an incident; it becomes one when an analyst confirms something bad really happened, or probably did.

**Incident response** (IR) is the planned way a team handles incidents: who decides, what to do first, how to stop the damage, how to keep evidence, who to tell and how to stop it happening again. Without a plan, people panic, wipe evidence, argue about who is in charge and miss legal deadlines. With a plan, the same incident is stressful but controlled.

The best-known model comes from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). Its guide **SP 800-61 Rev. 2** (2012) described a lifecycle that most courses, certifications and Indian companies still teach:

1. **Preparation**
2. **Detection and analysis**
3. **Containment, eradication and recovery**
4. **Post-incident activity** (lessons learned)

In April 2025 NIST published **SP 800-61 Rev. 3**, which replaced Rev. 2. It places incident response inside the six functions of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover), stressing that preparation and learning are part of everyday risk management, not a separate step. The practical work inside a single incident is still well described by the classic phases, so this lesson uses them and notes where Rev. 3 adds emphasis.

## How it works

```flow
Preparation | plan, roles, playbooks, logs, contacts, drills -> Detection and analysis
Detection and analysis -> Real incident?
Real incident? -[no]-> Close and tune the alert
Real incident? -[yes]-> Severity, scope, legal clock | CERT-In 6-hour clock starts when noticed
Severity, scope, legal clock -> Containment | disable accounts, isolate hosts, block IPs
Containment -> Evidence preserved | logs, images, hashes, chain of custody
Evidence preserved -> Eradication | remove malware, close the hole, reset secrets
Eradication -> Recovery | restore from clean backups, watch closely
Recovery -> Lessons learned | blameless review, fix controls and playbooks
Lessons learned -> Preparation
```

**1. Preparation.** Everything you do before the incident decides how it goes. You need:

- An **IR plan**: what counts as an incident, severity levels, who the incident lead is, who can approve shutting a system down, and how to reach legal, management and PR.
- **Playbooks** (also called runbooks) for common incident types: phishing, compromised account, ransomware, data leak, lost laptop, and, increasingly, AI-specific ones such as prompt injection.
- **Logs and tools**: a SIEM with the right sources, enough retention (CERT-In expects 180 days), and a way to isolate machines and disable accounts quickly.
- **Contacts**: CERT-In's reporting address, your cloud provider's support, your cyber insurer, outside forensics firms and legal counsel, written down where you can reach them if email is down.
- **Practice**: tabletop exercises where the team talks through a scenario. Rev. 3 puts weight on this continuous preparation and improvement.

**2. Detection and analysis.** Alerts come from the SIEM, users reporting phishing, customers, partners or even the news. The analyst asks: is it real? What is affected? How bad is it? Is it still happening? They build a **timeline** in IST, collect **IOCs** (IP addresses, file hashes, accounts) and assign a severity. This is also the moment the **legal clock** may start.

**3. Containment.** Stop it spreading. Short-term containment is fast and reversible: disable an account, revoke sessions and API keys, isolate a laptop from the network with your endpoint tool, block an IP at the firewall. Choose carefully: sometimes watching an attacker briefly reveals what else they have touched, but when data is leaving or ransomware is spreading, contain at once.

**4. Evidence.** Preserve evidence before you change too much. Collect the most volatile data first (memory, running processes, network connections), then disk images and logs. For each item, record what it is, who collected it, when, how, and its SHA-256 hash. That record is the **chain of custody**. It matters if the case goes to police, insurers, regulators or court.

**5. Eradication.** Remove the cause: delete malware and attacker-created accounts, patch the vulnerability or fix the misconfiguration they used, and reset every credential they might have seen. If you do not find how they got in, they will come back the same way.

**6. Recovery.** Bring systems back from known-clean backups or rebuilt images, in stages, with extra monitoring for a few weeks. Confirm with the business that things work.

**7. Lessons learned.** Within about two weeks, hold a **blameless** review: what happened, what went well, what slowed you down, and which controls, alerts and playbooks to change. Track those actions to completion. This feeds straight back into preparation.

**India: the CERT-In 6-hour rule.** On **28 April 2022**, CERT-In (the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team) issued directions under **section 70B(6) of the Information Technology Act, 2000**. They took effect 60 days later, in June 2022. The key points for incident responders:

| Requirement | What the directions say | What it means for you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reporting | Report the cyber incidents listed in Annexure I to CERT-In within **6 hours** of noticing them or being brought to notice of them | Your playbook needs a step, early on, to decide if the incident is reportable and send the report |
| Which incidents | Annexure I lists types such as unauthorised access to systems or data, data breaches and leaks, malicious code such as ransomware, website defacement, and identity theft, spoofing and phishing | Keep the full list in your playbook; check the official text, not a summary |
| Logs | Keep logs of ICT systems securely for a rolling **180 days** within Indian jurisdiction | Set SIEM and cloud log retention accordingly |
| Time | Sync system clocks to NTP servers of NIC or NPL, or servers traceable to them | Correlated timelines and accurate reports |
| Point of contact | Designate a point of contact to work with CERT-In | Name them in the IR plan |

The directions apply widely, to service providers, intermediaries, data centres, body corporates and government organisations, but read the official document and CERT-In's FAQ on it for exact scope and what the first report must contain. Note that the 6 hours run from when you **notice** the incident, not from when you finish investigating. You do not need the full story to report.

This is separate from the **DPDP Act, 2023**, which has its own duty to inform the Data Protection Board and affected people about personal data breaches. Sector regulators such as RBI and SEBI also have reporting rules for the firms they regulate. One incident can trigger several reports, so legal counsel belongs in the IR plan.

## A worked example

An Indore D2C clothing brand sells through its own website. At 11:20 IST on a Saturday, the on-call engineer sees a SIEM alert: an admin account exported 60,000 customer records at 10:55 from an overseas IP. The admin is on leave and says she has not logged in.

| Time (IST) | Phase | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 11:20 | Detection | Alert seen; admin confirms it was not her. Incident declared, severity high. CERT-In clock noted: report due by about 17:20 |
| 11:30 | Containment | Admin account disabled, all its sessions and API tokens revoked. A second, new admin account found and disabled |
| 11:45 | Evidence | Platform audit logs, sign-in history and SIEM events exported and hashed; the admin's laptop isolated for later forensics |
| 12:30 | Analysis | Sign-in history shows a password reset at 10:40 from the same IP. Her email shows a fake "courier delivery failed" message clicked on Thursday |
| 14:00 | Reporting | Incident lead and legal confirm it is a data breach of a type in Annexure I. Report sent to CERT-In. DPDP breach duties assessed with counsel |
| Day 1 to 3 | Eradication | All admin passwords reset; phishing-resistant MFA enforced for every admin; the phishing email's sender and links blocked |
| Day 3 to 7 | Recovery | Admin access restored to named people only; extra alerts on exports and admin changes |
| Day 14 | Lessons learned | Blameless review. Actions: MFA for every staff account, export limits, phishing reporting button, playbook updated with the CERT-In step |

The brand could not prevent the phishing click, but because it had logs, an alert, a playbook and a named lead, it contained the attack within about 35 minutes and met its reporting duty with hours to spare.

## Common mistakes

- **No plan until the day.** Writing roles and contacts during an incident wastes the hours that matter most.
- **Wiping first, asking later.** Rebuilding a server before collecting evidence destroys the record of how they got in.
- **Forgetting the clock.** Teams investigate for a day and then discover the 6-hour CERT-In window passed long ago.
- **Containing only the obvious.** Attackers create extra accounts, keys and forwarding rules. Look for all of them.
- **Not resetting secrets.** If the attacker could read a config file, every key in it is compromised.
- **Blame in the review.** People hide mistakes next time. Fix systems, not people.
- **Treating the review as optional.** Without lessons learned, the same incident happens again.

## Practice

1. Skim NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3, especially its overview of incident response within CSF 2.0, and the CISA playbooks' incident response checklist.
2. Read the CERT-In directions of 28 April 2022 yourself. Write down the reporting deadline, the log retention rule and five incident types from Annexure I.
3. Write a one-page playbook for "staff account compromised by phishing" for a small company: detection signals, first three containment steps, evidence to keep, who decides on CERT-In reporting, and who tells customers.
4. Run a 30-minute tabletop with friends: one person plays the attacker revealing clues, the others follow your playbook.

**You are done when you can** walk through the lifecycle with a real example, explain containment versus eradication, list the evidence to keep, and state the CERT-In reporting and log rules accurately with their source.
