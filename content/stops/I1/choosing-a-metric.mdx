## What it is

An **evaluation metric** turns a model's predictions into a number you can compare. Different metrics reward different behaviour, so the choice should follow from what matters in use.

```text
                      Predicted
                 positive     negative
   Actual  +------------------+------------------+
   positive|  true positive   |  false negative  |  <- recall = TP / (TP + FN)
           |  (caught)        |  (missed)        |
           +------------------+------------------+
   negative|  false positive  |  true negative   |
           |  (false alarm)   |  (correctly ok)  |
           +------------------+------------------+
              ^ precision = TP / (TP + FP)
```

This lesson follows three teams at a bank in Mumbai, each choosing a different metric for a different problem.

## How it works

**Classification.**

- *Accuracy*: share correct. Fine for balanced classes; misleading for rare events.
- *Precision*: of predicted positives, how many are right. Important when false alarms are costly (blocking genuine payments).
- *Recall*: of actual positives, how many were caught. Important when misses are costly (missed fraud, missed disease).
- *F1*: a balance of precision and recall at one threshold.
- *ROC AUC*: how well the model ranks positives above negatives across all thresholds; insensitive to class balance, which can hide poor performance on rare classes.
- *PR AUC* (average precision): focuses on the positive class; better for rare events.
- *Log loss* and calibration: whether probabilities are trustworthy.

**Regression.**

- *MAE*: average absolute error, in original units; robust and easy to explain.
- *RMSE*: punishes large errors more; use when big misses are especially bad.
- *MAPE*: percentage error; intuitive but breaks near zero values and over-penalises small actuals.
- *Quantile or pinball loss*: when you predict ranges (for example, the 90th percentile of delivery time for promises).

**Ranking and recommendation.** Precision at k, recall at k, NDCG: did the right items appear near the top?

**Cost-weighted metrics.** Put money on outcomes: cost of a false positive, cost of a false negative, value of a true positive. Choose the model and threshold that maximise expected value. This is often the clearest metric for business owners.

**Offline metric versus business KPI.** The offline metric is a proxy. Check that improving it moves the real KPI (losses, revenue, time saved) through A/B tests or pilots.

**By segment.** Report metrics by important groups (region, language, customer type) to catch models that are good on average and poor for some users.

**Matching the metric to the problem:**

| Problem | Worse error | Primary metric | Also report |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Card fraud (rare) | Missed fraud | PR AUC, recall at a fixed alert budget | Cost-weighted value |
| Spam filter | Real email in spam | Precision | Recall |
| Disease screening | Missed disease | Recall | Precision, so doctors aren't flooded |
| Delivery time promise | Late delivery | 90th percentile quantile loss | MAE |
| Sales forecast | Large misses | RMSE | MAE for reporting |
| Recommendations | Irrelevant top items | Precision at k, NDCG | A/B test on clicks or sign-ups |

**Thresholds turn scores into decisions.** Most classifiers output a score between 0 and 1. You choose a threshold above which a case is flagged. A low threshold catches more positives (higher recall) but raises more false alarms (lower precision); a high threshold does the opposite. There is no correct threshold in general: it depends on what each error costs and how many cases your team can review. A common practical method is to fix the capacity, for example "the fraud team can review 300 alerts a day", choose the threshold that produces about 300 alerts, then report recall at that point.

**Calibration.** If a model says 0.8, does that mean an 80% chance? Often it does not. A calibrated model's scores match real frequencies: of all cases scored around 0.8, about 80% are positive. Calibration matters when people act on the probability itself, such as pricing insurance or showing a risk level to a doctor. Check it with a reliability plot, and fix it with methods such as Platt scaling or isotonic regression.

## A worked example

Three teams at a Mumbai bank:

- **Fraud on card payments** (0.2% fraud): accuracy is meaningless. They use PR AUC to compare models and then a cost-weighted measure (missed fraud at the average loss versus analyst time per alert) to pick the threshold.
- **ATM cash demand forecasting**: running out of cash is far worse than holding extra, so they predict the 90th percentile of demand and evaluate with quantile loss, alongside MAE for reporting.
- **Product recommendations in the mobile app**: only the top three suggestions are seen, so they use precision at 3 and confirm with an A/B test on product sign-ups.

Each team reports its metric by customer segment. The recommendation model turns out to perform well in metros and poorly in rural branches' customers; a separate feature set for rural customers fixes much of the gap.

**Explaining the choice to the business.** The fraud team's head did not want PR AUC; she wanted to know what the model would do for the bank. So the team translated: "At 300 alerts a day, which your analysts can handle, the new model catches about 78 of every 100 fraud cases, up from 64. That is roughly ₹1.1 crore a month in losses avoided, for no extra analyst time." The model metric chose the model; the rupee figure made the decision.

| Team | Model metric | Business translation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fraud | PR AUC, recall at 300 alerts a day | Losses avoided per month |
| ATM cash | Quantile loss at the 90th percentile | ATMs running out of cash per month |
| Recommendations | Precision at 3 | Extra product sign-ups in the A/B test |

## Common mistakes

- **Accuracy on imbalanced data.**
- **ROC AUC alone for rare events.** Look at PR AUC and precision at your operating point.
- **MAPE with small or zero actuals.** Use MAE or weighted variants.
- **Optimising a metric that does not move the KPI.** Validate with pilots or A/B tests.
- **Only overall numbers.** Check segments.

- **Choosing the threshold by habit.** 0.5 is rarely the best threshold. Choose it from costs and review capacity.
- **Treating scores as probabilities without checking calibration.**
