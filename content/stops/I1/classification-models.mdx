## What it is

**Classification** predicts a category: fraud or genuine, will churn or will stay, which of five complaint types. Most classifiers output a **probability** or score; a **threshold** turns it into a decision ("flag as fraud if probability above 0.3").

Common models: **logistic regression** (a linear model that outputs probabilities, easy to explain), decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting and neural networks.

```text
  features (amount, merchant, time, device)
         |
         v
   [classifier] --> score 0.0 ........ 0.3 ......... 0.9 ..... 1.0
                                       |              |
                               review threshold   block threshold
                                       |              |
                          below: allow |  between:    | above: block,
                                       |  analyst     | fast appeal
                                       |  reviews     |
```

This lesson follows a payments company in Pune as it replaces fraud rules with a classifier.

## How it works

**Logistic regression.** Like linear regression, but the weighted sum passes through a function that squeezes it to a probability between 0 and 1. Coefficients show which features push towards each class.

**The confusion matrix.** For a chosen threshold, count true positives (fraud flagged), false positives (genuine flagged), false negatives (fraud missed) and true negatives. Every metric comes from these four numbers.

**Precision and recall.** Precision: of flagged cases, how many were really positive. Recall: of all real positives, how many were flagged. Lowering the threshold raises recall and lowers precision; raising it does the opposite.

**Choosing the threshold.** Put rupee values on errors. If a missed fraud costs ₹15,000 on average and investigating a false alarm costs ₹200 of analyst time, you should accept many false alarms to catch more fraud. Also consider capacity: if analysts can review 500 cases a day, the threshold may be set to flag about 500.

**Imbalanced classes.** When positives are rare (0.5% fraud), accuracy is useless: predicting "never fraud" scores 99.5%. Use precision, recall, the precision-recall curve and ROC AUC. Techniques: class weights that make errors on the rare class count more, sampling methods, and enough positive examples for training.

**Calibration.** If the model says 0.3, does fraud happen about 30% of the time at that score? Well-calibrated probabilities make thresholds and business rules reliable.

**Multi-class.** For more than two categories, look at the confusion matrix to see which classes are confused with which.

**Common classifiers compared:**

| Model | Strengths | Weaknesses | Good first choice when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Logistic regression | Fast, explainable, calibrated-ish probabilities | Misses complex interactions | You need to explain decisions, such as credit |
| Decision tree | Easy to draw and explain | Overfits, unstable | Teaching or simple rules |
| Random forest | Robust, little tuning | Larger, slower | A strong baseline on tables |
| Gradient boosting | Usually the most accurate on tables | Needs tuning, less explainable | Fraud, churn, risk on tabular data |
| Neural network | Text, images, very large data | Needs lots of data, hard to explain | Unstructured inputs |

**A sound workflow.** Start with a simple baseline, such as the current rules or logistic regression, so you know what "better" means. Split data by time. Train a stronger model with class weights. Compare on precision, recall and PR AUC, not accuracy. Choose a threshold from costs and capacity. Check calibration. Then report the result in rupees and in operational terms, such as alerts per day.

**Reading a model's mistakes.** After choosing a threshold, read 50 false positives and 50 false negatives by hand. Patterns appear quickly: genuine festival-season spikes flagged as fraud, or a new fraud pattern the training data never saw. Each pattern suggests a fix: a feature such as "days to Diwali", more recent training data, or a rule for a specific case. Metrics tell you how often the model is wrong; reading cases tells you why.

## A worked example

A Pune payments company has 2 million UPI merchant transactions a month, of which about 0.3% are later confirmed fraud.

1. Baseline: their rules catch 40% of fraud with 6,000 alerts a month.
2. A gradient-boosted classifier with class weights, trained on six months of data and tested on the following month.
3. At the threshold giving 6,000 alerts a month (the analysts' capacity), it catches 68% of fraud.
4. Monthly fraud is about 6,000 cases (0.3% of 2 million). Catching 68% means about 4,080 of the 6,000 alerts are real fraud: precision of about 68%, against 40% for the rules.
5. Business view: about 1,680 more frauds caught each month; at ₹9,000 average loss, roughly ₹1.5 crore saved monthly with the same analyst team.

They set a second, higher threshold that blocks transactions automatically only when the score is extremely high and precision exceeds 98%, with a fast appeal route for merchants.

**Explaining it to the risk committee.** The committee did not ask about gradient boosting. They asked three questions: will analysts be overloaded, will genuine merchants be hurt, and how much will it save? The team answered with the same number of alerts as today, a precision of about 68% instead of 40% so analysts waste far less time, an automatic block only above a score where precision exceeds 98% with appeals answered within four hours, and about ₹1.5 crore a month in fraud caught that the rules missed. They also showed the model's performance by merchant category and city, because a model that was good on average but bad for small kirana merchants would have been a fairness and business problem. It was slightly weaker for new merchants with little history, so new merchants were routed to review rather than automatic blocking for their first month.

## Common mistakes

- **Reporting accuracy on imbalanced data.** Use precision, recall and PR curves.
- **Using the default 0.5 threshold.** Choose from costs and capacity.
- **Testing on random rows of time-based data.** Test on a later period, as in real use.
- **Ignoring calibration.** Scores used for decisions should mean what they say.
- **One threshold for all actions.** Review and automatic blocking need different precision.

- **No baseline.** Without comparing to current rules or a simple model, you cannot show the new model is worth it.
