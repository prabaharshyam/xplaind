## What it is

A **voice agent** is an AI you talk to, on a phone line or in a web page, that talks back. Under the hood it chains three systems many times a second:

1. **Speech-to-text** (a transcriber) turns the caller's audio into words.
2. A **language model** decides what to say and whether to use a tool.
3. **Text-to-speech** (a voice) turns the reply into natural audio.

Building that loop yourself means handling audio streams, interruptions, silence, phone networks and latency. **Vapi** is a developer platform that does this plumbing. You configure an **assistant**, choosing the transcriber, model and voice, write its instructions, give it tools, and attach a phone number or a web-call button. Vapi runs the conversation in real time.

```flow
dir: LR
title: Vapi handles the phone line, streaming audio, turn-taking, interruptions and call logs
Caller speaks -> Transcriber -[text]-> LLM + prompt -[reply]-> Voice -> Caller hears
LLM + prompt -[tool call]-> Your server or n8n webhook | check slots, book, look up an order
Your server or n8n webhook -[result]-> LLM + prompt
```

## How it works

**The assistant.** In the Vapi dashboard you create an assistant and set:

- **Model and system prompt:** who the agent is, what it can do, the facts it needs (timings, prices) and firm rules (never give medical advice, always confirm before booking).
- **First message:** what it says when the call connects. Include that it is an AI assistant.
- **Transcriber:** the speech-to-text provider and language. Indian English, Hindi and other languages differ in quality between providers, so test with real speakers.
- **Voice:** the text-to-speech provider and voice. Pick one that sounds natural for your callers and test pronunciation of local names.

**Tools.** Tools turn talk into action. You describe a function, such as `book_slot(date, time, name, phone)`, and give Vapi a server URL to call. The model decides when to call it; your server, or an n8n workflow behind a webhook, does the work and returns a result the agent speaks.

**Phone numbers.** You can get a number through Vapi or bring one from a telephony provider such as Twilio. Inbound calls go to your assistant; you can also start outbound calls by API, for reminders or follow-ups. Check which countries and number types are supported for India before you promise a client a local number.

**Conversation behaviour.** Settings control when the agent decides the caller has finished (**endpointing**), what happens when the caller interrupts, background noise handling, maximum call length, and when to transfer to a human.

**After the call.** Vapi keeps transcripts, recordings (if enabled) and an end-of-call report that can be sent to your server: a summary, structured data you asked for, and success criteria. This is how you review quality and feed a CRM.

| Concern | What good looks like | How to get there |
|---|---|---|
| Response delay | under about 1 second | fast model, short prompt, quick tools |
| Understanding | names and numbers right | good transcriber for the language; confirm back |
| Staying on task | refuses out-of-scope requests | clear rules in the prompt; test edge cases |
| Actions | bookings are valid | validate in the tool, not just the prompt |
| Hand-off | human when needed | transfer or message-taking path |
| Trust | caller knows it is AI | say so in the first message; follow recording rules |

**Web calls too.** The same assistant can run in a website or app through Vapi's web SDK, so a "Talk to us" button starts a voice conversation in the browser with no phone line at all. This is a cheap way to prototype before buying numbers.

## A worked example

Dr Rao's dental clinic in Visakhapatnam misses about 40 calls a week, mostly appointment requests after 7 p.m. Faizan, an automation freelancer, builds a voice receptionist.

He writes a short system prompt: the clinic's name, hours, three services with fixed consultation fees, a rule to never discuss treatment, and a rule to confirm the date, time and phone number before booking. The first message: "Hello, this is the AI assistant for Rao Dental. I can book or change appointments. How can I help?"

He creates two tools pointing at n8n webhooks. `check_slots` reads free slots from the clinic's Google Sheet; `book_slot` writes the booking and sends the patient an SMS confirmation. The n8n workflow rejects times outside clinic hours, so a confused model cannot book 3 a.m.

Testing finds three problems. Average response time is 1.8 seconds, so he switches to a faster model and shortens the prompt, bringing it to about 0.9 seconds. The transcriber mishears "Gajuwaka", so he adds it as a keyword and has the agent repeat locations back. One tester asks about tooth pain medicine; the agent starts to answer, so he strengthens the rule and adds a transfer to the clinic's on-call number.

After launch, the end-of-call reports go to a sheet the receptionist checks each morning. Missed after-hours bookings drop to almost none.

## Common mistakes

**Long prompts.** Every extra paragraph adds delay and confusion. Keep the prompt focused and move reference data into tools.

**Trusting the model to enforce rules.** Validate in your tools as well: business hours, prices, permissions.

**Testing only with your own voice.** Test with different accents, background noise, interruptions, and people who change their mind mid-sentence.

**Not disclosing the AI.** Callers should know they are talking to an AI, and recording requires consent under privacy rules. Say both clearly.

**No way out.** Always offer a human, a callback or a message. Being stuck with a bot is the fastest way to lose a customer.

**Ignoring cost per minute.** Transcriber, model, voice and telephony each charge per minute or per token. Estimate monthly minutes and check current prices before you quote.
