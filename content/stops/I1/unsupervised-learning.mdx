## What it is

**Unsupervised learning** finds patterns in data that has no labels. Three common tasks:

- **Clustering**: group similar records, such as customer segments or similar stores.
- **Dimensionality reduction**: compress many features into a few that keep most of the information, for visualisation, speed or simpler models. **PCA** is the classic method.
- **Anomaly detection**: find records that look unlike the rest, such as unusual transactions or faulty sensors.

There is no "correct answer" to check against, so judging usefulness is part of the job.

```text
   ^
   |        o o              x x

   |       o o               x x       x = premium packaged

   |   + + +                           * = a possible anomaly

```

## How it works

**Prepare the features.** Unsupervised methods depend heavily on which features you use and how they are scaled. Standardise numeric features; think carefully about what "similar" should mean for the business.

**k-means clustering.** Choose k, the number of clusters. The algorithm places k centres, assigns each record to the nearest centre, moves centres to the middle of their records, and repeats. It works best for roughly round, similar-sized groups. Choose k using the elbow method or silhouette scores, but mostly by whether the clusters make business sense.

**Other clustering methods.** DBSCAN finds clusters of any shape and labels sparse points as noise; hierarchical clustering builds a tree of groupings; Gaussian mixtures give soft memberships.

**PCA.** Finds new axes (combinations of original features) that capture the most variation. Plotting the first two components often reveals groups or outliers. Keeping components that explain, say, 90% of variance can shrink 50 features to 10.

**Anomaly detection.** Methods such as Isolation Forest score how easily a record can be separated from others. Use the scores to prioritise review, not as final verdicts.

**Judging results.** Describe each cluster with averages of its features and real examples; give it a name a business user understands; check stability (do clusters stay similar when you rerun with a different sample?); and test usefulness (does targeting by segment improve a campaign?).

**Choosing a method:**

| Task | Method | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Segments of similar size and shape | k-means | Customer and store segments |
| Irregular shapes, noise | DBSCAN | Location data, finding dense areas |
| A tree of groupings | Hierarchical clustering | Small datasets, exploring structure |
| Soft membership | Gaussian mixtures | Customers who belong partly to two segments |
| Compress and visualise | PCA | Many features, quick plots |
| Find unusual records | Isolation Forest | Transactions, sensors, data quality |

**The hard part is meaning.** Algorithms always return clusters, even in random data. The real work is describing each one with averages and real examples, naming it with the business team, and proving it is useful with a test. A cluster nobody can describe or act on is not a segment.

**Choosing k with evidence, not just the elbow.**

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score, adjusted_rand_score

X, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=3000, centers=4, n_features=6, cluster_std=1.6, random_state=8)
Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

for k in range(2, 8):
    labels = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=8).fit_predict(Xs)
    print(k, "silhouette", round(silhouette_score(Xs, labels, sample_size=2000, random_state=8), 3))
```

**Check stability before naming segments.**

```python
def stability(Xs, k, runs=5, frac=0.8, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    ref = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(Xs)
    scores = []
    for r in range(runs):
        idx = rng.choice(len(Xs), int(frac * len(Xs)), replace=False)
        other = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=r + 1).fit(Xs[idx])
        scores.append(adjusted_rand_score(ref.predict(Xs[idx]), other.labels_))
    return round(float(np.mean(scores)), 3)

print("stability at k=4:", stability(Xs, 4), " at k=7:", stability(Xs, 7))
```

An adjusted Rand index near 1 means the same records keep landing together; much lower values mean the segments are artefacts of one run.

**Describe clusters in business terms.** Join cluster labels back to the original, unscaled columns and summarise:

```python
import pandas as pd

def describe(df, labels):
    d = df.assign(cluster=labels)
    summary = d.groupby("cluster").agg(["mean", "median"]).round(1)
    summary["size"] = d.cluster.value_counts().sort_index()
    return summary
```

Then read ten real records from each cluster before giving it a name such as "weekend family shoppers".

**Anomaly scores for a review queue.**

```python
from sklearn.ensemble import IsolationForest

iso = IsolationForest(n_estimators=300, contamination="auto", random_state=8).fit(Xs)
scores = -iso.score_samples(Xs)                  # higher = more unusual
queue = np.argsort(-scores)[:20]
print("top anomaly scores:", scores[queue][:5].round(3))
```

Send the top cases to a person with the features that make them stand out (for example, how many standard deviations each value is from the median), and record what they decide. Those decisions become labels for a supervised model later.

**PCA for a first look.** `PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(Xs)` gives coordinates to plot; `explained_variance_ratio_` says how much of the variation the two axes capture. If it is small, the picture may hide real structure.

## A worked example

A Kochi supermarket chain with 400,000 loyalty-card customers wants segments for marketing.

Features per customer over 12 months: visits per month, average basket in rupees, share of spend on fresh produce, on packaged foods and on household items, share of weekend visits and share of purchases on promotion. All standardised.

k-means with k from 3 to 10; silhouette scores peak around 6 and 7. Seven clusters, merged to six and named with the marketing team:

1. Weekly family stock-up (large baskets, weekends)
2. Daily fresh shoppers (frequent, small, produce-heavy)
3. Deal hunters (high promotion share)
4. Occasional top-up (rare, small baskets)
5. Premium packaged (high basket, imported brands)
6. Lapsing (visits falling sharply in recent months)

A three-month test sends segment-specific offers to half of each segment. Response rises from 3.1% to 4.6% overall, and the "lapsing" segment's offers bring back 12% of those customers. The segment label also becomes a useful feature in the churn model.

| Measure | Before segments | With segment-specific offers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Offer response rate | 3.1% | 4.6% |
| Lapsing customers brought back | Not measured | 12% |
| Extra use | None | Segment label added as a feature in the churn model |

**Naming the clusters with marketing.** The first output, with k = 7, was seven columns of averages that nobody outside the data team could read. In a one-hour workshop, the team showed marketing each cluster's averages next to five real, anonymised customers from it. Marketing named the segments in their own words and pushed back where it mattered. They merged two clusters that the algorithm had split over a small difference in weekend visits, because they would treat both groups the same way. Six clusters became the final segments only after that conversation. The team also reran the clustering on a fresh three-month sample. Five of the six segments reappeared at similar sizes. The sixth and smallest came back smaller, so the team rechecks it every quarter.

## Common mistakes

- **Unscaled features.** Large-valued features dominate distances.
- **Choosing k only by a score.** Business meaning matters.
- **Treating clusters as truth.** They are a useful view, not a fact about customers.
- **Not checking stability.** Unstable clusters change with every rerun.
- **Anomaly scores as verdicts.** Use them to prioritise human review.

- **Clustering on features that cannot be acted on.** Segments defined by data the business cannot use for targeting rarely change anything.
- **Choosing k from the elbow alone**, without stability or business meaning.
- **Naming clusters from averages only**, without reading real records.
- **Treating anomaly scores as verdicts** rather than a review order.

## When not to use unsupervised methods

- **You have labels for what you care about**: supervised models answer the question directly.
- **Segments must follow fixed rules**, such as regulatory customer categories.
- **Too few records or features** to form meaningful groups.

## From demo to production

- **Fix seeds and record settings**, so segments are reproducible.
- **Check stability** before each release of new segments.
- **Re-cluster on a schedule** and map new clusters to old names deliberately.
- **Track cluster sizes over time**; big shifts mean behaviour or data changed.
- **Feed review decisions on anomalies** back as labels.
