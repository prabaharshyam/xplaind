## What it is

Attackers use AI the way everyone else does: as a fast assistant. It writes, translates, summarises, codes and imitates voices and faces. That does not create brand-new kinds of attack so much as make old ones cheaper, faster and more convincing.

National cyber agencies are careful about the size of the change. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre assessed that AI will almost certainly increase the volume and impact of cyber attacks, with the clearest uplift in social engineering and reconnaissance, and that it lowers the barrier for less skilled criminals. The FBI's 2024 public warning lists the same patterns: AI-written messages, fake profile photos and documents, cloned voices of relatives in a "crisis", and fake live video calls with "company executives" or officials.

This lesson looks at five uses and, for each, what defenders change. It is written so you can defend: it explains patterns, not how to run an attack. The beginner lesson **Deepfakes and misinformation**, later in this stage, covers family and personal scams; this one is about companies.

```flow
title: Defending against a convincing impersonation
Urgent request | voice note, video call or email from "the CFO" -> Does it move money, data or access?
Does it move money, data or access? -[no]-> Normal handling
Does it move money, data or access? -[yes]-> Pause | urgency and secrecy are warning signs
Pause -> Call back on a known number | from the directory, not the message
Call back on a known number -> Confirmed by the real person?
Confirmed by the real person? -[no]-> Report to security | and to the bank if money moved
Confirmed by the real person? -[yes]-> Second approver signs off | two people for payments and access
```

## How it works

### 1. Phishing at scale, in every language

Phishing used to have tells: clumsy English, generic greetings, odd formatting. A language model removes them. An attacker can produce thousands of variants of a fake KYC update, electricity disconnection notice or courier message, each personalised with the target's name, employer and city, and translated into fluent Hindi, Marathi, Tamil, Telugu or Bengali. For businesses, the same applies to **business email compromise**: a believable message from a "vendor" asking to update bank details, written in the vendor's usual tone.

**What defenders change:**

- **Training** stops saying "look for spelling mistakes". It teaches people to check the request: does it ask for money, credentials, a new bank account or urgency and secrecy? Is it on the expected channel?
- **Email authentication**: publish SPF, DKIM and a DMARC policy that moves to `p=reject`, so nobody can send mail from your exact domain. Monitor for lookalike domains.
- **Phishing-resistant MFA** such as passkeys or security keys, which will not work on a fake login page, however perfect it looks.
- **An easy report button** and fast feedback, so one person's report protects everyone.

### 2. Voice cloning and deepfake video

A few seconds to minutes of recorded speech, from a conference talk, a podcast or a social media video, can be enough for current tools to produce a convincing clone. Video face-swaps can now run live on a call.

The best-documented company case is from 2024. An employee at the Hong Kong office of Arup, the UK engineering firm, received a message about a confidential transaction from someone posing as the UK-based chief financial officer. A video call followed in which the "CFO" and other colleagues were AI-generated. The employee made 15 transfers totalling about HK$200 million (around US$25 million). Hong Kong police described the case in February 2024, and Arup confirmed it was the victim in May 2024.

The lesson is not "spot the fake". The employee saw familiar faces and heard familiar voices. The lesson is that **process must not depend on recognising a person**.

**What defenders change:**

- **Call-backs** on a number from the directory for any request to move money, change bank details, share data or grant access.
- **Codewords** agreed in person for the most sensitive requests, especially for senior leaders' assistants and finance teams.
- **Two-person approval** for new beneficiaries and for payments above a limit, with no exception for "urgent and confidential".
- **Waiting periods** on changes to supplier bank details, confirmed with the supplier on a known number.
- **Help desk rules**: password resets and MFA re-enrolment need checks a voice cannot pass, such as a manager's approval in a ticket or an in-person ID check.

### 3. Faster reconnaissance

Before an attack, criminals research: who works in finance, who approves payments, which vendors a company uses, which technologies its job posts mention. AI tools summarise public profiles, press releases and filings in minutes and suggest who to target and what story to tell.

**What defenders change:** review what your company and staff publish. Org charts, named approvers on websites, detailed tech stacks in job ads and executives' schedules on social media all help an attacker build a story. Run the same research on yourself, as a red-team exercise, and remove what you do not need to share.

### 4. Help writing malware

General-purpose assistants refuse obvious malware requests, but attackers can still use them to write ordinary code (a script to collect files, a loader) or to translate and tidy existing tools. Several AI providers publish reports on accounts they have banned for this kind of misuse. The NCSC assessment above gives the sober summary: AI lowers the barrier for less skilled attackers and speeds up work for skilled ones, with the biggest gains in social engineering rather than in brand-new kinds of malware.

**What defenders change:** less than you might think. Malware written with AI help still has to run on your machines, talk to the internet and steal data. Endpoint detection based on behaviour, application allow-listing, least privilege, patching and network egress controls work regardless of who wrote the code. Detection based only on exact file hashes was already weak and becomes weaker as variants get cheaper.

### 5. Automated vulnerability discovery

AI systems can now help find bugs in code. This cuts both ways. DARPA's AI Cyber Challenge, whose final ran in August 2025, was a public competition in which teams built AI systems that found and patched vulnerabilities in open-source software, including some real, previously unknown bugs. The same capability, in attackers' hands, shortens the time between a bug existing and someone exploiting it.

**What defenders change:** use the same tools first. Run AI-assisted code review and fuzzing on your own code, patch faster, keep an accurate inventory of what you run, and assume that a published vulnerability will be exploited sooner than it used to be.

### Summary table

| Attacker use | Old warning sign that fails | Defence that still works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phishing in many languages | Bad grammar, generic greeting | Check the request and channel; DMARC; passkeys; report button |
| Voice and video impersonation | "I know her voice" | Call-back on a known number; codewords; two-person approval |
| Faster reconnaissance | "Attackers will not know who approves payments" | Publish less; red-team your own public footprint |
| Help writing malware | Known file hashes | Behaviour-based detection; least privilege; egress controls |

## A worked example

**The setup.** A Chennai auto-parts maker with 900 staff pays suppliers by RTGS and NEFT. Its accounts executive, Priya, receives a WhatsApp voice note at 4:40 p.m. on a Friday. It sounds exactly like the managing director, Mr Raghavan: "Priya, I am in a meeting with the bankers, we are closing an acquisition. Please pay ₹48 lakh to the new supplier in the email I am sending. Keep this between us until Monday." The email arrives from md@chennai-autoparts.co. The real domain ends in .in.

**What the old process would have done.** The company had one approver for payments under ₹50 lakh. The amount was chosen to sit just below that limit. A convincing voice and a plausible story would have been enough.

**What the redesigned process does.**

1. **Pause on the pattern.** Urgency, secrecy, a new beneficiary and a request outside the normal flow: each is a warning sign on its own.
2. **Call back.** Priya calls Mr Raghavan on the number in the company directory. He is at the factory, not with bankers, and sent nothing.
3. **Two people for new beneficiaries.** Even if Priya had been convinced, adding a new supplier needs a second approver from procurement, and bank details are confirmed with the supplier on a number already on file.
4. **Email controls.** The company's real domain has DMARC at `p=reject`, so an exact spoof would have bounced. The lookalike domain gets an "external sender" banner, and a lookalike-domain alert reaches IT.
5. **Report.** IT logs the incident, warns the finance team, blocks the lookalike domain and reports it on the national cybercrime portal.

**After the incident** the company moves its MD's public talks and podcast appearances onto the threat list. It does not take them down, because a voice clone needs only a little audio, but it briefs his assistant and finance team on the codeword check.

## Common mistakes

- **Training people to spot fakes by eye or ear.** Detection skill fades as tools improve. Train the process instead: verify on a known channel.
- **Exceptions for senior people.** "The CEO is in a hurry" is exactly the story attackers tell. The process must apply to everyone.
- **Calling back the number in the message.** It belongs to the attacker. Use the directory.
- **DMARC stuck at `p=none`.** Monitoring is a start; until the policy is quarantine or reject, your domain can still be spoofed.
- **SMS or voice OTP as the only MFA for critical systems.** Phishing kits relay them in real time. Prefer passkeys or security keys.
- **Panic about AI malware.** The basics of endpoint detection, least privilege and patching still do most of the work.
- **No report path.** People who feel foolish do not report. Thank reporters, every time.

## Practice

Read the FBI's public service announcement linked below, then the UK NCSC's assessment for a sober view of the threat.

1. Look up your own company's or college's domain with a free DMARC checker. What is the policy?
2. Write a one-page payment verification rule for a 200-person company: thresholds, call-back, two-person approval, waiting period for bank-detail changes.
3. Search for the public footprint of one senior person in your organisation (with permission): talks, podcasts, job titles of their assistants. Note what an attacker would use.
4. Rewrite one security awareness slide that says "look for spelling mistakes" into one that teaches checking the request and the channel.

**You are done when you can** explain why a perfect deepfake should still fail against your company's payment and access processes, and name the controls that make that true.
