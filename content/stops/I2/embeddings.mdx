## What it is

An embedding is a list of numbers that stands for the meaning of a piece of text. A sentence goes into an embedding model and comes out as, say, 384 or 1,536 numbers. The numbers on their own mean nothing to a person. What matters is how two lists compare: texts that mean similar things get lists that point in nearly the same direction, and unrelated texts get lists that point apart.

That is the trick behind semantic search. "How do I get my money back?" and "refund policy" share no words, yet their embeddings sit close together, so a search built on embeddings finds one from the other. A keyword search would miss it.

You will use embeddings for search, RAG, finding duplicate tickets, grouping customer feedback by theme and recommending similar items.

```text
  "How do I get my money back?"  --[embedding model]-->  [0.12, -0.40, 0.88, ...]  \
                                                                                      > close: similar meaning
  "Refund policy"                --[embedding model]-->  [0.10, -0.37, 0.91, ...]  /

  "Video buffers on mobile data" --[embedding model]-->  [-0.65, 0.22, 0.05, ...]     far: different meaning

        meaning space (squashed to 2-D)
            refund *  * money back
                        * double charge
                                            * buffering
```

This lesson follows a support team at an edtech company in Chennai that wants agents to see similar solved tickets.

## How it works

1. **An embedding model reads the text.** It is a neural network trained on huge numbers of text pairs that should match (a question and its answer, a title and its article) and pairs that should not. Training pushes matching pairs closer together.
2. **It outputs a vector.** Every text, short or long, becomes a vector of the same length for a given model. Different models give different lengths, and vectors from different models cannot be compared with each other.
3. **You compare vectors with a similarity score.** The usual one is cosine similarity: 1 means pointing the same way, 0 means unrelated. Many libraries normalise vectors so a simple dot product gives the same ranking.
4. **You store vectors to search later.** For a few thousand texts, a NumPy array is enough. For millions, a vector database such as pgvector, Qdrant or Pinecone finds the nearest vectors quickly using an approximate index.

Two practical points. First, embed the query and the documents with the same model. Second, an embedding model has its own input limit, often a few hundred tokens, and quietly cuts off anything longer. That is one reason long documents are split into chunks before embedding.

**Choosing a model:**

| Option | Size and cost | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small open model (such as MiniLM) | Runs on a laptop, free | Prototypes, English text, thousands of items |
| Larger open multilingual model | Needs a GPU for speed, free to run | Indian languages, Hinglish, better quality |
| Hosted embedding API | Pay per token, no servers | Fast start, large scale, managed updates |

**Testing before you choose.** Collect twenty real queries with the ticket or passage that should come first for each. Embed everything with two or three candidate models and count how often the right item lands in the top three. This takes an afternoon and beats any public leaderboard, because it uses your users' words, spellings and languages. Repeat the test whenever you consider switching models, since a switch means re-embedding the whole collection.

## A worked example

Say an edtech support team in Chennai has 2,000 past tickets and wants to show agents similar solved tickets.

```python
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np

model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")   # small, free, runs on a laptop
tickets = [
    "Payment deducted but course not unlocked",
    "Money taken twice for one order",
    "Video keeps buffering on mobile data",
    "Certificate shows the wrong name",
]
vectors = model.encode(tickets, normalize_embeddings=True)

query = model.encode(["UPI debited, no access to the course"], normalize_embeddings=True)
scores = vectors @ query.T            # dot product = cosine similarity here
for i in np.argsort(-scores[:, 0])[:2]:
    print(round(float(scores[i, 0]), 2), tickets[i])
```

The top result is the first ticket, even though the query says "UPI debited" and "no access" while the ticket says "payment deducted" and "not unlocked". The second should be the double-charge ticket: also about money, so close, but less so. The buffering and certificate tickets should score lower. Run it and check: exact scores depend on the model version.

Now try "certificate name spelling mistake". It should match the certificate ticket strongly and everything else weakly. If it does not, something is wrong with how you encode or compare.

**What the team measured.** On 30 real agent queries, the small English model put the right ticket in the top three 22 times. A multilingual model did it 27 times, mostly by catching queries written in Tamil-English mix such as "payment cut aachu, course open aagala". The team chose the multilingual model and kept a keyword search for order ids, which neither model handled well.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing models.** Vectors from two different embedding models live in different spaces. Re-embed everything when you switch models.
- **Embedding whole documents.** A 30-page PDF squeezed into one vector loses detail, and text past the model's limit is dropped. Chunk first.
- **Trusting the score as a probability.** A score of 0.62 is not "62% relevant". Scores are only useful for ranking, and good thresholds differ by model, so set any cut-off from your own data.
- **Ignoring exact terms.** Embeddings are weak on order numbers, product codes and rare names. Pair them with keyword search when those matter.
- **Not testing on your own text.** A model that tops a public leaderboard may do worse on Indian names, Hinglish or your domain's jargon. Check on twenty real queries before you choose.
