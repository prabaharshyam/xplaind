## What it is

A **convolutional neural network (CNN)** processes images with small learned filters that slide across the picture. Early layers learn edges and textures; later layers combine them into shapes and objects. Because the same filter is used everywhere in the image, CNNs learn efficiently and recognise patterns wherever they appear.

**Transfer learning** starts from a CNN (or vision transformer) already trained on millions of general images and adapts it to your task. It needs far less data and time than training from scratch and usually gives better results.

```text
  image  -->  [conv: edges]  -->  [conv: textures]  -->  [conv: parts]  -->  [classifier]
  224x224     many small maps      smaller maps          small maps         "slub defect" 0.91
              (3x3 filters)        (pooling shrinks)     (larger regions)

  Transfer learning:
  [pre-trained layers, frozen]  ------------------------->  [new final layer, trained]
         then optionally unfreeze the last blocks with a small learning rate
```

This lesson follows a fabric-defect project at a textile factory in Pune.

## How it works

**Convolution and pooling.** A filter (for example 3 × 3 pixels) multiplies and sums the pixels under it, producing a map of where its pattern appears. Many filters per layer detect many patterns. Pooling or strided convolutions shrink the maps, so deeper layers see larger regions.

**Classic architectures.** ResNet, EfficientNet and others are well tested and available pre-trained. Vision transformers are a newer alternative; both are used in practice.

**Transfer learning steps.**

1. Load a pre-trained model.
2. Replace its final layer with one matching your classes.
3. Train the new layer first with earlier layers frozen; then optionally unfreeze some or all layers and train with a small learning rate (fine-tuning).

**Data augmentation.** Random flips, crops, rotations, brightness and colour changes create varied versions of training images, reducing overfitting. Choose augmentations that match reality: rotate leaf photos, but do not flip text documents.

**Image data quality.** Real images vary in lighting, angle, background and camera. Collect training images that look like production images (phone photos in a shop are not studio photos). Check labels; mislabelled images hurt more than you expect.

**Evaluation.** Hold out images from different days, devices or locations to test generalisation. Look at the confusion matrix and at misclassified images; they often reveal label errors or missing conditions (night, rain, blur).

**Beyond classification.** Object detection (where things are), segmentation (which pixels belong to what) and OCR use related architectures, also usually fine-tuned from pre-trained models.

**From scratch versus transfer learning:**

| Approach | Data needed | Time | Typical result on small datasets |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Train from scratch | Tens of thousands of images or more | Long | Weaker |
| Transfer learning, frozen backbone | Hundreds to a few thousand | Short | Good |
| Fine-tuning the last blocks | A few thousand | Medium | Often best |

**What a filter computes.** A 3 × 3 filter holds nine learned weights. At each position it multiplies them with the nine pixels underneath and adds the results into one number. Sliding it across the image gives a map that is bright wherever the filter's pattern appears, such as a vertical edge. A layer with 64 filters produces 64 such maps, and the next layer combines them into more complex patterns.

**Reading the confusion matrix.** Accuracy alone hides which classes are confused. A confusion matrix shows, for each true class, how often the model predicted each class. In the fabric project it showed that "slub" and "thick place" defects were swapped often, while "hole" was almost never missed. That told the team where to collect more examples and where an inspector's second look mattered most. Looking at twenty misclassified images by eye, sorted by confidence, usually teaches more than any single number.

## A worked example

Continuing the Pune fabric-defect project: 4,800 labelled images across six defect types from two factory lines.

- Small CNN trained from scratch: 71% validation accuracy.
- Pre-trained ResNet-50, new final layer, frozen backbone, 5 epochs: 86%.
- Unfreezing the last two blocks with a learning rate ten times smaller, plus augmentations (flips, small rotations, brightness changes): 93%.

Validation used images from a different week than training. The confusion matrix showed most errors between "slub" and "thick place" defects; reviewing 60 misclassified images found 14 mislabelled ones. After fixing labels and retraining, 94.5%.

Before deployment, the team tested on 300 images from the second factory line with different lighting: 88%. They added 400 images from that line to training, reaching 93% there too, and set a confidence threshold below which images go to a human inspector.

| Step | Validation accuracy |
| --- | --- |
| Small CNN from scratch | 71% |
| Pre-trained ResNet-50, frozen, new final layer | 86% |
| Fine-tuned last two blocks with augmentation | 93% |
| After fixing 14 mislabelled images | 94.5% |
| Second factory line, before adding its images | 88% |
| Second line, after adding 400 of its images | 93% |

The labels and the second line's lighting mattered as much as the architecture.

## Common mistakes

- **Training from scratch with little data.** Use transfer learning.
- **Unrealistic augmentations.** Augment in ways real images vary, such as lighting, angle and blur, not in ways they never do.
- **Validating on near-duplicates of training images.** Split by day, device or location.
- **Ignoring misclassified images.** They reveal label errors and missing conditions.
- **No confidence threshold in production.** Route uncertain images to an inspector instead of forcing a guess.
