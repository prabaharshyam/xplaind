## What it is

Vector search finds passages with similar meaning. Keyword search finds passages with the same words. Each fails where the other succeeds:

- "How do I claim for a cracked phone screen?" Vector search finds the accidental damage clause even though it says "breakage of display". Keyword search may not.
- "What does clause 7.3(b) say?" or "error E-4012" Keyword search nails it. Vector search returns vaguely similar clauses.

**Hybrid search** runs both and merges the results. **Reranking** then takes the top 20 to 50 merged results and reorders them with a slower, more accurate model. **Metadata filters** restrict the search to the right slice first, such as one product, one state or documents in force after a date.

```text
                       query
                  /            \
       [BM25 keyword]        [vector search]       (both filtered: product, state, in force)
         top 40                 top 40
                  \            /
              [fusion: reciprocal rank]
                       |
                    top 40
                       |
              [cross-encoder reranker]   reads query + passage together
                       |
                    top 5  -->  prompt
```

This lesson follows an insurance company in Chennai whose agents ask detailed questions about policy wordings.

## How it works

**Keyword side.** Most systems use BM25, a formula that scores a passage higher when it contains the query's words, especially rare words, and does not over-reward long passages. Elasticsearch, OpenSearch and many vector databases offer it. PostgreSQL's built-in full-text search ranks with its own functions (`ts_rank`), not BM25; extensions add BM25.

**Vector side.** Embed the query and find the nearest chunk embeddings.

**Fusion.** The two scores are on different scales, so you cannot just add them. Two common fixes: normalise each to 0 to 1 and take a weighted sum (an "alpha" of 0.5 weights them equally), or use reciprocal rank fusion, which ignores raw scores and rewards items ranked high in either list. RRF is simple and hard to break, so it is a good default.

**Reranking.** An embedding model encodes query and passage separately, which is fast but loses detail. A **cross-encoder** reads them together and scores relevance directly. It is too slow to run on a million passages, but fine on 40. Retrieve wide with hybrid search, rerank, keep the top 5 for the prompt.

**Filters.** Store fields such as `doc_type`, `state`, `effective_from` and `access_group` with every chunk. Apply them in the query so irrelevant or out-of-date documents never compete. Filters are also where per-user permissions are enforced, which a later lesson covers.

**Reciprocal rank fusion in one line.** Each passage gets a score of 1 divided by (60 plus its rank) from each list it appears in, and the scores are added. A passage ranked 1st by BM25 and 3rd by vectors scores about 1/61 + 1/63; one ranked 2nd by only one list scores 1/62. Passages that both methods like rise to the top, and nothing depends on raw score scales.

**Choosing settings:**

| Setting | Typical value | Raise it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Candidates from each search | 20 to 50 | The right passage is often just outside the list |
| Candidates sent to the reranker | 20 to 50 | Recall at 50 is much higher than recall at 5 |
| Passages kept for the prompt | 3 to 8 | Questions need several passages combined |
| Weight in weighted fusion | 0.5 | Your golden set shows one side helping more |

Tune these on the golden set, not by feel, and record latency alongside recall each time.

## A worked example

An insurance company in Chennai has 4,000 pages of policy wordings, endorsements and circulars. Agents ask questions such as "Is flood covered under HOME-PLUS for Chennai after the 2026 revision?"

Your golden set has 60 questions with known source passages. You measure recall at 5 (the share of questions whose correct passage is in the top five):

- vector search alone: 68%
- BM25 alone: 61%
- hybrid with RRF: 79%
- hybrid, then a cross-encoder reranker over the top 40: 88%
- the same, filtered to `product = HOME-PLUS` and `effective_from` after the revision: 93%

Looking at failures shows why. Vector search missed questions naming endorsement codes like "END-17". BM25 missed paraphrases. The reranker fixed cases where the right passage was ranked eighth. The filter removed the superseded 2024 wording that had been outranking the current one.

Reranking added about 180 milliseconds per query. The client accepted that for a 9-point gain in recall, and you wrote both numbers in the eval report.

| Set-up | Recall at 5 | Added latency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vector only | 68% | Baseline |
| BM25 only | 61% | Lower |
| Hybrid with RRF | 79% | About 20 ms |
| Hybrid plus reranker over 40 | 88% | About 180 ms |
| Plus filters for product and effective date | 93% | No extra |

Each step fixed a different kind of failure, which is why reading the failed questions mattered as much as the totals.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding raw scores.** BM25 and cosine scores are on different scales; the larger one silently dominates. Normalise or use RRF.
- **Reranking too few.** If the right passage is not in the candidate list, the reranker cannot rescue it. Retrieve 20 to 50, keep 3 to 8.
- **No golden set.** Without measured recall you cannot tell whether hybrid search helped or just felt better.
- **Filtering after retrieval.** Taking the top 5 and then dropping out-of-scope ones can leave you with nothing. Filter inside the search.
- **Forgetting tokenisation for Indian text.** Keyword search on Tamil or Hindi needs an analyser that handles those scripts, or matches fail silently.

Read the Weaviate article on hybrid search and Pinecone's piece on rerankers.

1. Take your RAG app from the earlier lesson and build a golden set of 30 questions, a third of them with exact codes, names or numbers.
2. Measure recall at 5 for vector only, BM25 only (the `rank_bm25` package is enough), and both merged with RRF.
