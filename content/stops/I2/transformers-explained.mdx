## What it is

A **transformer** is a neural network architecture, introduced in 2017, built around a mechanism called **attention**. It processes a sequence (words, image patches, audio frames) and lets every position look at every other position to decide what matters.

Three families use it:

- **Decoder-only** models generate text one token at a time: today's chat and coding models.
- **Encoder-only** models read text and produce representations: embedding models for search and classification.
- **Encoder-decoder** models map one sequence to another: translation and summarisation.

Vision transformers split images into patches and treat them like tokens.

## How it works

**Tokens and embeddings.** Text is split into tokens; each token becomes a vector (an embedding). Positional information is added so the model knows the order.

**Attention.** For each token, the model computes three vectors: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I contain?) and a value (what do I pass on?). It compares the query with every key to get attention weights, then takes a weighted mix of the values. In "The bank approved the loan because it trusted the applicant", attention helps "it" focus on "the bank".

**Multi-head attention.** Several attention heads run in parallel, each free to learn different relationships (grammar, references, topics).

**Layers.** Each transformer layer has attention followed by a small feed-forward network, with shortcuts and normalisation that make deep stacks trainable. Large models stack dozens of layers.

**Generation.** A decoder-only model predicts a probability for every possible next token, picks one (sampling), appends it, and repeats. A mask stops each position from seeing future tokens during training.

**Cost.** Attention compares every token with every other, so cost grows quickly with sequence length. This is why context windows have limits and long prompts are slower and pricier. Engineering tricks (efficient attention kernels, caching keys and values) reduce the cost.

**Pre-training and adaptation.** Models are pre-trained on huge data to predict missing or next tokens, then adapted: instruction tuning and preference training for chat models, fine-tuning for specific tasks, or used as they are with prompts and retrieval.

**Why it won.** Attention handles long-range relationships well and trains efficiently in parallel on GPUs, which allowed scaling to very large models and datasets.

## A worked example

A Chennai legal-tech startup classifies Indian court judgments by area of law (property, family, criminal, tax, labour) and also wants semantic search over them.

- **Classification**: they fine-tune a small encoder-only transformer on 12,000 labelled judgment summaries. It reaches 91% accuracy against 82% for a gradient-boosted model on keyword features, because attention captures context such as "the appellant's claim to ancestral property".
- **Search**: they use an embedding model (encoder) to embed paragraphs and retrieve similar passages.
- **Summaries**: they use a decoder-only language model through an API, with retrieved passages in the prompt.

When judgments exceed the encoder's 512-token limit, they classify the first and most relevant sections rather than the whole text, a direct consequence of attention's cost with length.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating all transformers as chatbots.** Encoders, decoders and encoder-decoders suit different jobs.
- **Ignoring sequence limits.** Long inputs are truncated or expensive.
- **Fine-tuning a huge model when a small encoder would do.** Match model size to task.
- **Assuming attention weights are explanations.** They are not reliable explanations of decisions.
- **Forgetting tokenisation.** Indian languages often use more tokens per word, affecting limits and cost.
