## What it is

Language models learn from text on the web, and the web is mostly English. In one widely used training dataset, English is about 46% of the text. Indian languages are tiny by comparison:

| Language | Speakers (millions) | Share of the dataset | Under-represented by about |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Punjabi | 113 | 0.006% | 230 times |
| Kannada | 64 | 0.012% | 66 times |
| Telugu | 95 | 0.018% | 65 times |
| Gujarati | 62 | 0.013% | 62 times |
| Marathi | 99 | 0.021% | 58 times |
| Bengali | 272 | 0.093% | 37 times |
| English (for comparison) | 1,452 | 45.9% | over-represented |

The last column compares each language's share of the world's population with its share of the dataset. The figures are from a 2023 study of the Common Crawl web dataset, cited in Chip Huyen's *AI Engineering*. Languages like these, with little training text compared with how many people speak them, are called **low-resource languages**.

This causes three practical problems:

1. **Lower quality.** On a well-known test of general knowledge, a leading model scored worst in Telugu, Marathi and Punjabi, the most under-represented languages tested.
2. **Higher cost and delay.** A **tokenizer** splits text into tokens, and many tokenizers were designed mainly for English. They can need several times more tokens for the same sentence in Hindi or Tamil. This is called high **token fertility**. Since you pay per token and models write one token at a time, the same answer costs more and arrives more slowly.
3. **Real usage is messy.** Indians mix languages ("mera order kab aayega?"), switch scripts, and type Indian languages in Roman letters. Test sets made only of clean, formal text miss all this.

The picture is improving: newer tokenizers handle Indian scripts far better, and Indian groups build models and data for Indian languages. But you still need to measure, language by language, for your users.

```flow

Real user questions in each language -> Score general models | quality, tokens, cost

Good enough? -[yes]-> Ship with monitoring per language
Good enough? -[no]-> Try translate to English and back | Indian translation model
Try translate to English and back -> Try an Indian-language model
Try an Indian-language model -> Pick the best on quality and cost
Pick the best on quality and cost -> Ship with monitoring per language
```

## How it works

**Measure token fertility yourself.** Count tokens for the same message in English and your users' languages, with the tokenizer of each model you are considering. Write the same meaning, not a word-for-word translation.

```python
# pip install tiktoken
import tiktoken

texts = {
    "English": "When will my order arrive? I paid yesterday.",
    "Hindi": "मेरा ऑर्डर कब आएगा? मैंने कल भुगतान किया था।",
    "Hinglish": "Mera order kab aayega? Maine kal payment kiya tha.",
    "Tamil": "என் ஆர்டர் எப்போது வரும்? நான் நேற்று பணம் செலுத்தினேன்.",
}
for name in ["cl100k_base", "o200k_base"]:          # an older and a newer tokenizer
    enc = tiktoken.get_encoding(name)
    counts = {lang: len(enc.encode(t)) for lang, t in texts.items()}
    base = counts["English"]
    print(name, {lang: f"{n} ({n / base:.1f}x)" for lang, n in counts.items()})
```

Run it and you will usually see the newer tokenizer cut the Hindi and Tamil counts sharply, while Romanised Hinglish stays close to English. Other providers use different tokenizers, so check each one you consider.

**Three ways to serve a language.**

- **A general model, directly.** Simplest. Strong recent models are often good in Hindi, and weaker in lower-resource languages. Test before you assume.
- **Translate, answer, translate back.** Translate the question to English with a strong Indian-language translation model (such as AI4Bharat's open IndicTrans2), answer in English, translate back. It can lift quality for weak languages, but adds steps, delay and errors, and loses nuance: respect markers like *aap* and *tum* collapse into "you", and local terms may be mistranslated.
- **A model built for Indian languages.** Indian startups and research groups train models and tokenizers on far more Indian-language text. The government's Bhashini platform offers translation and speech services. These can be stronger and cheaper for Indian languages, but compare them on your own test set, as you would any model.

**Speech matters too.** Many users would rather speak than type, especially outside cities. Speech recognition quality varies by language, accent and background noise, so test it with real recordings from your users.

| Approach | Quality in weaker languages | Cost and speed | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| General model, directly | Varies; check per language | Can be high where tokenizers are inefficient | Stiff language, wrong local terms |
| Translate to English and back | Often better for weak languages | Extra steps and delay | Lost nuance, mistranslated names |
| Indian-language model | Often strong in Indian languages | Often efficient tokenizers | Narrower general knowledge; check |
| Retrieval of local facts, with any of the above | Better on schemes, prices, names | Small extra cost | Must keep sources current in each language |

**Try it in your browser.** The same message takes more bytes in Indian scripts, and tokens often follow bytes. Try your own sentences.

```python
samples = {
    "English": "Please send the electricity bill",
    "Hindi": "कृपया बिजली का बिल भेजें",
    "Tamil": "தயவுசெய்து மின்சார கட்டணத்தை அனுப்புங்கள்",
}
for lang, text in samples.items():
    chars, utf8 = len(text), len(text.encode("utf-8"))
    print(f"{lang:<8} {chars:>3} characters, {utf8:>3} UTF-8 bytes, {utf8 / chars:.1f} bytes per character")
```

## A worked example

An agritech company in Pune is building a helpline for farmers in Marathi and Hindi, by chat and voice, at about 50,000 questions a month. Many farmers type Marathi in Roman script.

They build a test set of 300 real questions: Marathi and Hindi, Devanagari and Roman script, about crops, weather and schemes, checked by native-speaking agronomists. Then they compare three options.

| Option | Correct and natural (Marathi) | Correct and natural (Hindi) | Average tokens per answer | Cost per 1,000 answers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| General model, direct | 68% | 82% | 410 | ₹310 |
| Translate to English, answer, translate back | 76% | 81% | 190 (English) plus translation | ₹260 |
| Indian-language model with retrieval | 84% | 86% | 230 | ₹150 |

The numbers are for illustration, to show how to compare. Scheme names were the biggest source of errors in every option, so the team adds retrieval of official scheme pages in Marathi and Hindi and a glossary of exact names. They keep monitoring quality per language, because a model update can improve Hindi and quietly hurt Marathi.

## Common mistakes

- **Testing only in English**, then launching in five languages.
- **Testing only formal, Devanagari text.** Real users mix languages and type in Roman script.
- **Ignoring token counts.** A feature that is cheap in English can cost several times more in Tamil.
- **Translating everything word for word.** Names, schemes and polite forms often break.
- **Treating "Indian languages" as one.** Quality in Hindi says little about Odia or Assamese.
- **Skipping native speakers** in evaluation. Fluent-looking text can still be wrong or unnatural.

## When English is fine

- **Professional users who work in English**, such as developers or analysts, may prefer it.
- **Highly technical content**, where English terms are standard, can stay in English with local-language explanations.
- **Early prototypes** can start in one language, as long as you plan and budget for the others.

## From demo to production

- **Report every metric per language and script**, not only overall.
- **Budget per language**, using measured tokens per answer.
- **Keep glossaries** of product names, schemes and terms in each language, and retrieve them.
- **Let users choose the language**, and reply in the one they wrote in.
- **Re-test after every model change**, since gains and losses differ by language.
