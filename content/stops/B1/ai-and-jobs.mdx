## What it is

"Will AI take my job?" is the most common question people in India ask about AI, from final-year students to senior managers. The honest answer is: it depends on your **tasks**, not your job title.

A job is a bundle of tasks. A customer-support executive answers routine queries, handles angry customers, coordinates with couriers and writes notes. A teacher plans lessons, explains ideas, marks homework and looks after a class. AI is very good at some of these tasks, helpful for some, and useless for others.

```text
         A job = a bundle of tasks
+-------------------------------------------+
| Task A  routine, text based   -> exposed  |
| Task B  needs checking         -> helped  |
| Task C  trust, judgement       -> safe    |
| Task D  physical, messy        -> safe    |
+-------------------------------------------+
   More A tasks = more change to the job
```

Research by the International Labour Organization (ILO) on generative AI and work found that **clerical work** (data entry, routine drafting, record keeping) is the most exposed, and that for most other occupations the likely effect is **augmentation**, where AI helps with part of the work, rather than full **automation**, where AI does the work instead of a person.

This lesson follows Arun, a customer-support executive in Hyderabad, as his company brings in an AI assistant. By the end you will be able to break any job into tasks, judge how exposed each one is, and make a plan to stay useful.

## How it works

### Exposure is about tasks

To judge any job, list its tasks and ask three questions of each:

1. Could AI do this task today, at good enough quality?
2. Could AI do part of it, with a person checking?
3. Does it need things AI does not have: a body, trust, accountability, judgement in a messy situation?

Tasks that are routine, rule-following and mostly text or numbers are the most exposed. Tasks that involve people, physical work or responsibility are the least.

| Task type | Examples | Exposure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routine text and data | Data entry, standard emails, simple translation, form filling | High |
| Drafting and summarising | Reports, meeting notes, first drafts of code | High, but output must be checked |
| Analysis with judgement | Choosing a supplier, diagnosing a problem | Medium: AI helps, a person decides |
| People and trust | Negotiating, counselling, managing a team, selling to a client | Low |
| Physical work | Electrical repairs, nursing care, construction | Low |

### Three outcomes for a role

When you add up the tasks, a role tends to go one of three ways:

- **Automated.** Most tasks can be done by AI, so fewer people are needed. This happens mainly to routine, text-based, rule-following work.
- **Augmented.** AI handles parts of the work and people do more, faster or better. This is the most common outcome for professional jobs.
- **Created.** New roles appear around AI: engineers who build AI applications, people who deploy AI inside businesses, evaluators who test it, AI product managers, automation specialists and trainers.

```text
               Share of tasks AI can do
   low  <-------------------------------------->  high
   |  Mostly safe   |   Augmented    | Automated |
   |  nurse, sales  | analyst, coder | data entry|
```

### Why change is slower than headlines

Even when AI can do a task, organisations take time to adopt it. They must change processes, train staff, satisfy regulators, check quality, protect customer data and justify the cost. Trust builds slowly, especially in banking, healthcare and government. So the change usually arrives over years, team by team, not overnight.

### What this means in India

India has a large IT services, business-process and back-office workforce. Parts of that work, such as routine coding, support and data processing, are exposed. The same workforce is also well placed to build and deploy AI for companies around the world, and demand for AI skills in Indian job postings has been growing fast. Both things are true at once.

### Staying useful

Four moves help in almost every outcome:

1. **Use AI well in your current job.** People who use AI tools well often get more done than colleagues who do not.
2. **Move towards judgement, relationships and responsibility.** These tasks stay with people.
3. **Build skills that complement AI.** Checking AI output, domain knowledge, clear communication and working with data.
4. **Consider an AI-focused role** if it fits you. The paths on Xplaind show what each role needs.

## A worked example

Arun works in customer support for an e-commerce company in Hyderabad. His company introduces an AI assistant that answers order-status and return-policy questions in chat.

Here is his week before and after, as a share of his time:

| Task | Before | After six months |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routine status and return queries | 60% | 10% (only the ones the assistant escalates) |
| Complex complaints | 25% | 45% |
| Coordinating with warehouses and couriers | 15% | 20% |
| Reviewing and improving the assistant's answers | 0% | 25% |

The assistant took over most routine queries, so Arun's team grew smaller through people leaving and not being replaced, not through layoffs. Arun's work did not disappear; it moved. He now spends most of his time on difficult cases and on making the assistant better: when it quotes an old return policy, he writes the correct answer into its knowledge base.

He took the company's internal course on AI tools, learned how the assistant decides when to escalate, and six months later moved into a new role, "support automation specialist", improving the assistant and the workflows around it.

His colleague Divya refused to use the assistant. Her work became limited to the shrinking set of cases nobody had automated yet. The difference between them was not talent. It was that Arun moved towards the tasks that grew.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking in job titles.** "Accountants will be replaced" tells you nothing. Break the job into tasks and look at each one.
- **Believing either extreme.** "AI takes all jobs" and "nothing will change" are both wrong for most roles. The likely story is change.
- **Waiting to see what happens.** Learning to use AI now helps whether your role is automated, augmented or untouched.
- **Ignoring new roles.** Many new AI roles want domain knowledge plus AI skills, not a computer science degree.
- **Learning tools without judgement.** Employers value people who can check AI output and take responsibility for the result, not just people who can type prompts.
- **Trusting confident predictions.** Nobody knows exact numbers for job losses or gains. Be wary of anyone who claims to.

## Practise

Read the ILO's summary of its research on generative AI and jobs, linked below, and try one OpenAI Academy session on AI at work.

1. **Map your own job.** List the main tasks of your current or target job, with a rough share of time for each. Mark each as likely automated, helped or untouched.
2. **Test the most helped task.** This week, do that task with an AI assistant. Note the time saved and what you had to check or fix.
3. **Pick one skill.** Name one skill to build in the next three months that makes you more valuable alongside AI, and the first resource you will use.

**You are done when you can** break your own job into tasks, explain which are likely to change and why, and describe your plan to stay ahead.
