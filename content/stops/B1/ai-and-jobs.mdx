## What it is

AI does not replace jobs whole; it changes **tasks**. A job is a bundle of tasks. Some are easy for AI (drafting routine emails, summarising documents, first-level customer queries, basic code, translation of simple text); others are hard (building trust with a client, physical work, judgement in messy situations, accountability, leadership).

Research by the International Labour Organization finds that clerical work is the most exposed to generative AI, and that for most occupations the likely effect is **augmentation** (AI helps with part of the work) rather than full **automation**.

## How it works

**Exposure is about tasks.** Take any job and list its tasks. For each, ask: could AI do it, help with it, or not touch it? A call-centre agent's routine queries are highly exposed; handling an angry, complex complaint much less. A teacher's lesson planning is helped; classroom relationships are not.

**Three outcomes for a role.**

- *Automated*: most tasks done by AI; fewer people needed. Mostly routine, text-based, rule-following work.
- *Augmented*: AI handles parts; people do more, or better. The most common outcome for professional work.
- *Created*: new roles appear: AI engineers, people who deploy AI inside businesses, evaluators, AI product managers, automation specialists, trainers.

**Speed varies.** Adoption depends on cost, regulation, trust and how well AI actually works for a task. Many organisations take years to change processes.

**India-specific points.** India has a large IT services and back-office workforce, which is both exposed (routine coding, support, data processing) and well placed to build and deploy AI for the world. Demand for AI skills in Indian job postings has been growing quickly.

**Staying useful.**

- Learn to use AI well in your current job; people who do outperform those who do not.
- Move towards tasks that need judgement, relationships and responsibility.
- Build skills that complement AI: checking AI output, domain knowledge, communication, data skills.
- Consider an AI-focused role if it fits you; the next lessons help you choose.

## A worked example

Arun is a customer-support executive at an e-commerce company in Hyderabad. His company introduces an AI assistant that answers order-status and return-policy questions.

His task list before: 60% routine status and returns queries, 25% complex complaints, 15% coordinating with warehouses and couriers.

After six months, the assistant handles most routine queries. Arun's team is smaller through attrition, not layoffs. Arun now spends his time on complex complaints, on reviewing the assistant's escalated cases, and on writing improvements to its answers when it gets policies wrong. He takes the company's internal course on AI tools and later moves into a new role, "support automation specialist", improving the assistant and the workflows around it.

His colleague who refused to use the assistant found her work increasingly limited to cases that were also shrinking.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking in job titles.** Think in tasks.
- **Assuming either "AI takes all jobs" or "nothing changes".** The truth for most roles is change.
- **Waiting to see what happens.** Learning to use AI now helps in almost any outcome.
- **Ignoring new roles.** Many are open to people with domain knowledge plus AI skills.
- **Learning tools without judgement.** Checking and responsibility are what employers value.

## Practise

Read the ILO's summary of its research on generative AI and jobs, and try one OpenAI Academy session on AI at work.

1. List the main tasks of your current or target job, and mark each as likely automated, helped or untouched by AI.
2. Pick the most helped task and try doing it with an AI assistant this week. Note the time saved and what you had to check.
3. Name one skill to build in the next three months that makes you more valuable alongside AI.

**You are done when you can** describe how your own job's tasks are likely to change, and your plan to stay ahead.
