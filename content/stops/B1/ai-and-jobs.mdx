## What it is

"Will AI take my job?" is one of the most common questions people in India ask about AI, from final-year students to senior managers. The honest answer is: it depends on your **tasks**, not your job title.

A job is a bundle of tasks. A customer-support executive answers routine queries, calms angry customers, chases couriers and writes notes. A teacher plans lessons, explains ideas, marks homework and looks after a class. AI is very good at some of these tasks, helpful for some, and of little use for others.

Three terms carry this lesson:

- **Exposure** is how much of a task AI could do or help with today. It is a measure of possibility, not a prediction.
- **Automation** means AI does the task instead of a person.
- **Augmentation** means AI does part of the task and the person does more, faster or better.

```flow
title: A job is a bundle of tasks
A job -> Task A | routine, text based
A job -> Task B | drafting that needs checking
A job -> Task C | trust and judgement
A job -> Task D | physical, messy
Task A -> High exposure | AI can do most of it
Task B -> High exposure, checked | AI drafts, a person checks
Task C -> Low exposure | AI prepares, a person decides
Task D -> Low exposure, physical | needs a person on site
```

The best global evidence on exposure comes from the International Labour Organization (ILO). Its refined index, published in May 2025 with Poland's research institute NASK, scored nearly 30,000 work tasks. It found that about **one in four workers** worldwide are in an occupation with some exposure to generative AI, but only **3.3%** are in the highest-exposure group. **Clerical work** (data entry, routine drafting, record keeping) is the most exposed. Women are more exposed than men: 4.7% of women's jobs fall in the highest group, against 2.4% of men's. The ILO's main conclusion is that, because people are still needed, most jobs are more likely to be **transformed** than made redundant.

This lesson follows Arun, a customer-support executive in Hyderabad, as his company brings in an AI assistant. By the end you will be able to break any job into tasks, judge how exposed each one is, read job headlines critically and make a plan to stay useful.

## How it works

### Exposure is about tasks

To judge any job, list its tasks with a rough share of time, and ask three questions of each:

1. Could AI do this task today, at good enough quality?
2. Could AI do part of it, with a person checking?
3. Does it need things AI does not have: a body, trust, accountability, or judgement in a messy situation?

| Task type | Examples | Exposure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routine text and data | Data entry, standard emails, simple translation, form filling | High |
| Drafting and summarising | Reports, meeting notes, first drafts of code | High, but output must be checked |
| Analysis with judgement | Choosing a supplier, diagnosing a problem | Medium: AI helps, a person decides |
| People and trust | Negotiating, counselling, managing a team, selling to a client | Low |
| Physical work | Electrical repairs, nursing care, construction | Low |

### Exposure is not the same as job loss

An exposed task is only handed to AI when several other things are also true. Experts check four filters:

```flow
title: Will an exposed task really be handed to AI?
Exposed task -> Good enough quality?
Good enough quality? -[no]-> Stays with people | AI may help a little
Good enough quality? -[yes]-> Cheaper than a person?
Cheaper than a person? -[no]-> Stays with people
Cheaper than a person? -[yes]-> Safe if it goes wrong?
Safe if it goes wrong? -[no]-> AI drafts, a person checks
Safe if it goes wrong? -[yes]-> Allowed and trusted?
Allowed and trusted? -[no]-> AI drafts, a person checks
Allowed and trusted? -[yes]-> Handed to AI
```

Cost matters more in India than in many countries, because people cost less. A task worth automating in London may still be cheaper to do by hand in Lucknow. Risk matters too: a wrong order-status reply is an annoyance, a wrong loan decision or medicine dose is not. And rules and trust move slowly, especially in banking, healthcare and government, where regulators and customers expect a named person to be responsible.

Organisations also need time. They must change processes, train staff, protect customer data and prove the savings. So change usually arrives over years, team by team, not overnight.

### Three outcomes for a role

A role's outcome is not decided by the share of exposed tasks alone. It depends on what is left after AI takes the exposed work, and on whether the amount of work grows.

- **Automated.** Almost all the tasks are exposed and what is left is small, so the role disappears or merges into another one. This happens mainly to pure routine roles, such as a data-entry operator who only keys in forms.
- **Augmented.** Many tasks may be exposed, but what is left is the core of the job: judgement, trust and responsibility. People spend more time on that core. This is the most common outcome.
- **Created.** New roles appear around AI: engineers who build AI applications, people who deploy it inside businesses, evaluators who test it, automation specialists who redesign workflows, and AI product managers.

The expert's gotcha: **a role can be augmented while the team still shrinks.** For example, suppose a team of 10 spends 60% of its time on routine queries and an assistant takes most of them. If the number of queries stays flat, the same work might need 6 or 7 people. If the company uses the freed time to grow, answer faster or offer new services, it may keep all 10. Watch demand, not only exposure.

### Who gains: experience and skill

AI help does not lift everyone equally. In a large field study, economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond followed more than 5,000 customer-support agents at a Fortune 500 company that sells business software as an AI assistant was rolled out. In the working-paper version, agents with the assistant resolved about **14%** more issues per hour on average. The newest and least skilled agents gained about **a third**, while the most experienced gained little. The study was published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2025.

The reason is useful to understand. The authors suggest the assistant spread the practices of the most productive agents to beginners. Experts already worked that way. So AI tends to narrow the gap between new and experienced staff on routine work, and the value of experience moves to the cases the assistant cannot handle.

### The entry-level squeeze

That same pattern creates the biggest risk for students. The routine tasks AI does best are the ones freshers used to learn on.

Researchers at the Stanford Digital Economy Lab (Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen) studied US payroll data from millions of workers. In the August 2026 version of their paper, employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed jobs was about **19%** below where it would be if it had kept pace with similarly aged workers in less exposed jobs, up from 15% in the authors' first wave of data. Four details matter:

- The drop came mostly from **fewer hires**, not from more young workers leaving their jobs.
- Older workers in the same jobs held steady or grew.
- In earlier versions of the paper, the change came through the number of jobs, not through pay.
- The drop was concentrated in jobs where AI is used to **automate** tasks, not where it is used to **augment** them.

Read it with care. This is US data, and the authors describe it as an early warning rather than proof that AI caused the drop. They also say they do not see widespread, economy-wide job displacement linked to AI. But the direction is a clear signal for Indian freshers: you need to show judgement earlier, because fewer employers will pay you to learn it on routine work. Teams that stop hiring juniors completely also hurt themselves, because today's juniors are tomorrow's seniors.

### What this means in India

India has a large IT services, business-process and back-office workforce, and much of its entry-level work, such as routine coding, testing, support and data processing, is exposed. In October 2025, NITI Aayog published a *Roadmap for Job Creation in the AI Economy*, prepared with NASSCOM and BCG. It warns that India's tech services sector faces the threat of significant job losses by 2031, but also has the opportunity to create up to 4 million new jobs in the next five years, and it proposes a National AI Talent Mission. These are possibilities, not forecasts. Which way it goes depends a lot on how fast people and companies reskill.

### How to read a headline

Most AI-and-jobs headlines rest on one of five kinds of evidence. Each answers a different question.

| Kind of evidence | Example | What it tells you | What it does not tell you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Exposure index | ILO, May 2025 | Which tasks AI could help with | Whether jobs will go, or when |
| Field study | Support-agent study, 2025 | What one tool did in one company | Whether it holds in your company |
| Payroll data | Stanford young-worker study | What is happening to hiring now | The cause, for certain |
| Employer survey | WEF Future of Jobs Report, January 2025 | What over 1,000 employers expect by 2030 (170 million jobs created, 92 million displaced) | Whether it will happen; the headline totals mix all causes, not only AI |
| Scenario report | NITI Aayog, October 2025 | The range of outcomes for a sector | What will actually happen |

When a headline says "AI will kill X jobs", ask which kind it is. Exposure is not loss, a survey is not a measurement, and a scenario is not a promise.

### Signals to watch in your own company

Change usually shows up in small signals before it reaches your role. Watch for:

- A pilot of an AI tool on your team's tasks, with targets for "time saved" or "tickets deflected".
- Leavers in your role not being replaced, or a hiring freeze for one role only.
- Targets per person rising while the team size stays flat.
- New tasks such as "review AI output" or "maintain the knowledge base" appearing in job descriptions.
- Managers talking about "productivity per head" in reviews.

None of these means your job is going. Each means the task mix is shifting, and the people who move first get to choose their new tasks.

### Staying useful

Four moves help in almost every outcome:

1. **Use AI on your own work first.** Start with a task you do often, that is routine and is easy to check, where a mistake is cheap. Do not start with your biggest task if it needs judgement.
2. **Get good at checking.** Learn where the tool fails and catch its errors. People who can take responsibility for AI output are worth more than people who can only type prompts.
3. **Move towards judgement, relationships and responsibility.** Volunteer for exceptions, escalations, customers and decisions.
4. **Consider an AI-focused role** if it fits you. Many want domain knowledge plus AI skills, not a computer science degree. The paths on Xplaind show what each role needs.

### Talking to your manager

Ask for a short conversation and bring evidence: "I tried the assistant on routine replies for two weeks. It saved me about an hour a day, and I caught two wrong policy answers. I would like to spend that time on escalations and help improve the assistant's answers." You show that you use the tool, that you can check it, and that you have a plan for the freed time. That is a much stronger position than waiting to be told.

## A worked example

Arun works in customer support for an e-commerce company in Hyderabad, in a team of 12. The company introduces an AI assistant that answers order-status and return-policy questions in chat.

Here is his week before and after, as a share of his time. The figures are illustrative, not measured.

| Task | Before | After six months |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routine status and return queries | 60% | 10% (only the ones the assistant escalates) |
| Complex complaints | 25% | 45% |
| Coordinating with warehouses and couriers | 15% | 20% |
| Reviewing and improving the assistant's answers | 0% | 25% |

Sixty percent of Arun's week was exposed, yet his role was augmented, not automated. What was left (complaints, coordination, judgement about exceptions) was the core of the job, and it grew. When the assistant quoted an old return policy, Arun wrote the correct answer into its knowledge base. He also asked his manager to let him review the assistant's escalations, using the evidence he had collected about its mistakes.

The team did shrink. Order volume stayed flat, so when four people left over the year, only one was replaced, and the team went from 12 to 9. No one was laid off, but the hiring of new support executives slowed.

He took the company's internal course on AI tools, learned how the assistant decides when to escalate, and moved into a new role: **support automation specialist**, improving the assistant and the workflows around it.

The harder case was Sana, the one fresher hired that year, who joined in the seventh month. Arun had learned the job on hundreds of easy queries. Sana had almost none, because the assistant now handled them. In her first weeks she was given only complex complaints and struggled. The team fixed this in three ways:

- She spent two weeks **shadowing** Kiran, the team's most experienced agent, on escalations before taking her own.
- Each day she **reviewed 20 assistant answers** with a senior and marked the wrong ones, which taught her the policies faster than answering easy queries would have.
- The team kept a shared log of "why this was escalated", so every tricky case became a lesson for the next person.

The lesson for teams: when AI removes the training ground, you have to build a new one on purpose.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking in job titles.** "Accountants will be replaced" tells you nothing. Break the job into tasks and look at each one.
- **Treating exposure as job loss.** Exposure is what AI could do. Quality, cost, risk, rules and demand decide what actually happens.
- **Believing either extreme.** "AI takes all jobs" and "nothing will change" are both wrong for most roles. The likely story is change, sometimes with fewer people.
- **Forgetting the team.** Your own role can be augmented while your team stops hiring. Watch the signals, not only your own tasks.
- **Starting with the biggest task.** Start with a routine, frequent, easy-to-check task, not the one that takes the most time if it needs judgement.
- **Letting AI do the learning.** Freshers who copy AI answers without checking them never build the judgement employers now pay for.
- **Trusting confident predictions.** Nobody knows exact numbers for future job losses or gains. Ask what kind of evidence a number comes from.

## Practice

Read the ILO's summary of its 2025 index, linked below, and try one OpenAI Academy session on AI at work.

1. **Map your own job.** List the main tasks of your current or target job, with a rough share of time for each, adding up to 100%. Mark each as high, medium or low exposure, and say which outcome (automated or augmented) fits what is left.
2. **Test one task.** This week, do your most routine, easy-to-check task with an AI assistant. Note the time saved and every error you had to fix.
3. **Spot the signals.** Write down any of the signals above that you can see in your company or the companies you want to join.
4. **Pick one skill.** Name one skill to build in the next three months that makes you more valuable alongside AI, and the first resource you will use.

**You are done when you can** break your own job into tasks, explain which are likely to change and why, say what could happen to your team, and describe your plan to stay ahead.
