## What it is

Artificial intelligence feels new, but it is older than most of our parents. The phrase was coined in **1956** for a summer workshop at Dartmouth College in the United States, where researchers believed machines could soon be made to reason like people. They were wrong about "soon", but the field they started has run for seventy years.

AI has gone through waves of excitement and disappointment, and three broad approaches:

```text
 1956        1980s           1997      2012       2017        2022
  |            |               |         |          |           |
  v            v               v         v          v           v
 "AI" named  Expert systems  Chess     Deep       Transformer  Chat assistants
             boom, then      champion  learning   design       reach the public
             AI winter       beaten    for images published
  |-------- Rules era --------|-- Learning era ---|-- Large model era -->
```

| Era | Main idea | Typical examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rules written by people** (1950s to 1980s) | Experts write the logic by hand | Chess programs, expert systems for diagnosis |
| **Learning from data** (1990s to 2010s) | Programs find patterns in examples | Spam filters, recommendations, credit scores, image recognition |
| **Large models on huge data** (2017 onwards) | Very large neural networks learn general skills from enormous amounts of text and images | Chat assistants, image generators, coding assistants |

Knowing this story helps you in two practical ways. You can explain AI to anyone in a few minutes, and you can judge today's claims better, because every era has over-promised before.

This lesson follows one example throughout: how a bank in India has checked loan applications over thirty years.

## How it works

### The rules era

Early AI tried to write knowledge down as rules: "if the patient has fever and a rash, consider measles". In the 1970s and 1980s, **expert systems** with hundreds or thousands of such rules were built for medicine, chemistry and business.

They worked in narrow areas but broke easily. The world has too many exceptions to write down, and rules written for one hospital failed in another. Twice, promises ran ahead of results and funding fell. These slow periods, roughly in the 1970s and late 1980s, are called **AI winters**.

### The learning era

The big shift was to stop writing rules and start learning them. Give the computer many examples with answers, and let it work out the pattern. This is **machine learning**.

- Spam filters learned from millions of emails marked spam or not spam.
- Recommendation systems learned what people like from what they clicked and bought.
- Credit scores learned which patterns predicted repayment.

In 1997, IBM's Deep Blue beat world chess champion Garry Kasparov. It mostly used fast search and hand-tuned evaluation, but it showed computers could beat the best humans at a task once thought to need intelligence.

In the 2010s, **deep learning**, using large neural networks with many layers, made huge gains in recognising images and speech. In 2012 a deep network won a major image-recognition competition by a wide margin, and the field turned towards neural networks. Two things made this possible: much more data, and fast chips called **GPUs** that could do the arithmetic in parallel.

### The large model era

In **2017**, researchers at Google published a new model design called the **transformer**, which handles long passages of text well and can be trained efficiently on huge datasets. Companies trained ever larger transformer models on enormous amounts of text with one task: predict the next word.

At a large enough size, these models could write, summarise, translate, answer questions and write code, skills nobody had programmed directly. When chat assistants built on them were released to the public in **late 2022**, millions of people started using AI directly within weeks.

### Why it sped up

```text
     More data            More computing         Better designs
   (the internet)        (GPUs, data centres)    (transformers)
          \                     |                     /
           +---------> Bigger, better models <-------+
                               |
                               v
                    Useful products, more users
                               |
                               v
                  Large investment -> even more computing
```

Three things grew together and fed each other. Better results attracted investment, which paid for more computing, which produced better results.

### What has not changed

Every era over-promised. Experts in the 1960s predicted human-level AI within twenty years. Today's models are far more capable than anything before, but they still make confident mistakes, as later lessons explain. A good habit from history: be impressed by what systems can do today, and sceptical of confident predictions about what they will do soon.

## A worked example

Think about how a bank in India has checked a personal loan application over the years.

| Period | How it worked | Which era |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1990s | A loan officer used a rulebook: minimum income, a guarantor, a fixed ratio of loan to income, a check of documents by hand | Rules |
| 2000s and 2010s | Credit bureaus and scoring models learned from millions of past loans which patterns predicted repayment; decisions became faster and more consistent | Learning |
| Today | A language model reads the applicant's bank statements and salary slips and writes a one-page summary for the officer in seconds, next to the credit score | Large models |

Each step built on the one before. The rulebook did not vanish; its rules became checks inside the system. The credit score did not vanish; the summary sits next to it.

And in all three periods, a person makes the final decision on difficult cases. What changed is how much reading, calculating and checking that person does by hand. That pattern, tools changing the work around a human decision, repeats across most of AI's history.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking AI began with chat assistants.** It is a seventy-year-old field; chat assistants are its latest and most visible form.
- **Assuming progress is steady.** It came in bursts, with long slow periods between.
- **Believing every prediction.** Experts have often been wrong about timelines, in both directions.
- **Mixing up the terms.** AI is the broad field, machine learning is one approach to it, and language models are one kind of machine learning.
- **Thinking old approaches are dead.** Rules and classical machine learning still run inside banks, factories and apps, often next to language models.
