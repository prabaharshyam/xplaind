## What it is

Artificial intelligence feels new, but it is older than most of our parents. In **1950** the British mathematician Alan Turing asked whether machines could think. In **1956** the phrase "artificial intelligence" was coined for a summer workshop at Dartmouth College in the United States. The field has now run for more than seventy years.

The story in brief: people first wrote intelligence down as rules, then let computers learn rules from examples, then trained very large models on huge amounts of text, images and sound. More recently those models have begun to reason through problems and take actions.

| Approach | Main idea | Good at | Breaks when | Still used today in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Rules written by people** (strongest 1950s to 1980s) | Experts write the logic as if-then rules and search steps | Clear, checkable decisions in a narrow, fixed area | A case arrives that nobody wrote a rule for | Eligibility checks, billing and tax rules, safety checks |
| **Learning from data** (1990s to 2010s) | The program finds the pattern in many examples with known answers | One well-defined prediction, millions of times a day | New data looks different from the training data | Spam filters, fraud alerts, credit scores, recommendations |
| **Large models on huge data** (2017 onwards) | A very large neural network learns general skills from huge amounts of text, images and audio | Reading, writing, coding, conversation and, more recently, planning and using tools | A fluent guess is wrong, and it states the guess confidently | Chat assistants, coding assistants, agents |

Read the dates as rough. An era means the approach that most new work used, not a hard boundary. Old approaches never disappear. IBM's chess computer Deep Blue beat the world champion in 1997 but used the rules approach, and banks still run rule checks next to their newest models.

## How it works

```flow
title: Seventy years of AI in eight steps
1950 | Turing asks if machines can think -> 1956 | the field is named -> 1970s and 1980s | expert systems and two AI winters -> 1997 | Deep Blue wins at chess
1997 -> 2012 | deep learning wins at images -> 2017 | the transformer -> 2022 | chat assistants reach the public -> 2024 to 2026 | models that reason and act
```

### The question and the name (1950 to 1956)

Turing did not try to define "thinking". Instead he proposed a test he called the **imitation game**, now known as the Turing test. A judge chats by text with a person and a machine. If the judge cannot reliably tell which is which, the machine has done something that looks intelligent.

Today's chat assistants talk in a way many people find hard to tell from a person, yet they still make basic mistakes. Sounding human and being right are different things.

In 1955 John McCarthy and three colleagues proposed the Dartmouth workshop. They wrote that a "significant advance" on making machines use language and solve problems could come from one summer of work together. That was the first of many hopeful timelines.

### The rules era

Early AI had two main tools. **Rules**, such as "if the patient has fever and a rash, consider measles". And **search**, which means trying many possible moves or steps and picking the best one by a score.

In the 1970s and 1980s, **expert systems** packed an expert's knowledge into hundreds or thousands of rules. The best known, MYCIN, was built at Stanford University in the 1970s to suggest antibiotics for blood infections. In a test, specialists rated its advice about as acceptable as that of Stanford's own doctors, yet it was never used routinely on patients. Fitting it into hospital work, and deciding who was responsible when it erred, proved hard.

Raj Reddy, born in a village in today's Andhra Pradesh, pioneered computers that understand continuous speech. He shared the 1994 Turing Award, computing's highest honour, with Edward Feigenbaum, a leader of expert systems.

Why did rules break? You will meet the same three reasons in any rule-heavy system at work:

- **Experts cannot write down everything they know.** Much skill is judgement, and judgement does not turn into tidy rules.
- **The world has endless exceptions.** Rules written for one hospital failed in another.
- **Rules clash as they grow.** Adding one rule can quietly break another, so updates become slow and risky.

In 1997 Deep Blue beat the world chess champion Garry Kasparov. It searched huge numbers of positions each second and scored them with methods tuned mostly by people. It was the peak of rules and search. But chess is a closed world with fixed rules. A hospital or a bank is not.

Twice, promises ran ahead of results and funding fell. In 1973 a report by the mathematician James Lighthill told the UK's Science Research Council that AI had failed to reach its grand goals, and research money dried up. In the late 1980s the expert-system business collapsed. These slow periods are called **AI winters**: big promises, narrow results, then the money moved elsewhere.

### Neural networks: an old idea waiting for its moment

A different idea ran quietly alongside the rules. In the late 1950s Frank Rosenblatt built the **perceptron**, a simple artificial neuron that learned from examples by adjusting the weights on its inputs. In 1969 a famous book by Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert showed how limited a single layer of such neurons was, and interest faded.

The fix was to stack many layers. In 1986 David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams popularised **backpropagation**, a way to train many layers at once. The network makes a guess, measures how wrong it was, and passes that error backwards to adjust every weight a little. In 2024 Hinton and John Hopfield shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for discoveries that made machine learning with neural networks possible.

So why did neural networks not take over in the 1990s? They needed far more examples and arithmetic than computers then could supply. The idea was ready long before the data and hardware were.

### The learning era

The big shift of the 1990s and 2000s was to stop writing rules and start learning them. Give the computer many examples with known answers, and let it work out the pattern. This is **machine learning**. The internet helped by producing data at a new scale.

- Spam filters learned from millions of emails marked spam or not spam.
- Recommendation systems learned what people like from what they clicked and bought.
- Credit scores learned which patterns in past loans predicted repayment.

The engineer's job changed from writing rules to collecting good examples. The weaknesses changed too: a model needs labelled examples, usually does one task, fails quietly when new data looks different, and copies any bias in its examples.

In 2012 a deep neural network now known as AlexNet entered ImageNet, a major contest for recognising objects in photos. It missed the right answer among its top five guesses 15.3% of the time. The next best entry missed 26.2% of the time. That gap turned the field towards **deep learning**. Three things met: the old backpropagation idea, more than a million labelled photos, and **GPUs**, graphics chips built for games that do the needed arithmetic in parallel.

In 2016 DeepMind's AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol, a top player of the board game Go, four games to one. It combined learning with search: the approaches mix rather than replace each other.

### The large model era

In **2017** researchers at Google published a new design called the **transformer**. Two ideas made it special. First, **attention**: each word looks at every other word in the passage to work out what it means, so "bank" reads differently in "river bank" and "bank loan". Second, it reads a whole passage at once rather than word by word, which suits GPUs and makes training on huge datasets practical.

Companies then trained transformers on one simple task: predict the next word. This needs no labels, because any text is already a worked example. That removed the learning era's biggest limit, the cost of labelling data.

In 2020 researchers found that these models improved smoothly and predictably as model size, data and computing power grew together. This is called **scaling**. It turned AI spending into a calculated bet. The same year GPT-3, with 175 billion adjustable weights, did new tasks from a few examples in the prompt.

A model trained only to predict text just continues whatever you type. To make it an assistant, companies trained it further on good example answers and people's ratings of its replies. When ChatGPT, built this way, was released on 30 November 2022, analysts at the bank UBS estimated that it reached 100 million monthly users in about two months.

### 2023 to 2026: models that see, reason and act

Since 2023 the story has moved in three directions, which explain the tools you use today.

- **See, hear and speak.** Models began to take images and voice as well as text. In May 2024 OpenAI's GPT-4o accepted text, audio and images and could reply aloud in roughly the time a person takes in conversation.
- **Reason.** In September 2024 OpenAI released o1, a model trained to spend more time working through a problem before it answers. This added a new lever: spend computing power at answer time, not only in training. In January 2025 the Chinese company DeepSeek released R1, a reasoning model with open weights that anyone could download. Reasoning helps with maths, code and multi-step problems, but it is slower and costs more per answer, so it is wasted on simple questions.
- **Act.** In October 2024 Anthropic let its model operate a computer screen, moving the pointer and typing. In November 2024 it released the Model Context Protocol, an open standard for connecting assistants to tools and data. By 2026 the major labs offered **agents** that work for long stretches in their own sandboxed computer, even while you are away or on a schedule. They still make mistakes on long tasks, so people limit what they may do and check the results.

AI also changed science: half of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper of Google DeepMind for AlphaFold, which predicts the shape of proteins.

India entered the story directly. On 7 March 2024 the Union Cabinet approved the IndiaAI Mission with ₹10,371.92 crore for computing, datasets, Indian models and skills. In February 2026 India hosted the AI Impact Summit in New Delhi, which ended with the New Delhi Declaration, and the Bengaluru company Sarvam AI unveiled two models, Sarvam-30B and Sarvam-105B, built for Indian languages, and later released them as open models.

### Why it sped up

```flow
title: The loop that sped up AI
More data | the internet, no labels needed -> Bigger, better models
More computing | GPUs and data centres -> Bigger, better models
Better designs | transformers, reasoning -> Bigger, better models
Bigger, better models -> Useful products | millions of users
Useful products -> Large investment
Large investment -> More computing
```

Three inputs grew together and fed each other. Better results attracted investment, which paid for more computing, which produced better results.

Scaling has limits, and knowing them separates an informed view from hype:

- **Cost.** Each big step in size has needed many times more computing.
- **Data.** The supply of good, public, human-written text is limited, and it does not grow as fast as computing does.
- **Running cost.** A bigger model costs more for every answer it gives, not just once.

This is one reason recent gains also come from other levers: thinking longer on hard questions, and tools such as search or a code runner. When someone says AI will keep improving at the same pace, ask which input will keep growing, and who will pay.

### What has not changed

Every era over-promised. The Dartmouth proposal hoped for a big advance in one summer. In 1960 the economist and AI pioneer Herbert Simon wrote that within twenty years machines would be able to do any work a person can do. Today's systems are far more capable than anything before, but they still make confident mistakes.

History gives you three questions for any new claim:

1. **What does it do today**, on tasks like mine, as checked by someone other than the seller?
2. **What does the claim depend on**: more data, more computing, more money? Can that keep growing?
3. **What happens when it is wrong**, and who checks?

## A worked example

Follow one loan application through thirty years. Meera, a teacher in Pune, applies for a personal loan of ₹3 lakh over three years. All numbers here are for illustration.

Suppose the interest rate is 14% a year. Her monthly instalment (EMI) works out to about ₹10,253. She already pays ₹8,000 a month on a scooter loan, and her take-home pay is ₹45,000. Suppose the bank's own rulebook says all her EMIs together must stay under 50% of take-home pay. Her total would be ₹18,253, about 41% of her pay, so she passes that rule.

| Period | What does the work | Good at | Typical failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1990s | An officer applies the rulebook by hand: income, a guarantor, the EMI-to-pay ratio, documents | Clear reasons for every decision | Turns away a good self-employed applicant who has no salary slip |
| 2000s and 2010s | A credit bureau score and a scoring model learned from millions of past loans | Fast, consistent decisions at scale | Struggles with first-time borrowers who have no credit history |
| Today, at some lenders | A language model reads bank statements and salary slips and writes a one-page summary for the officer | Reads messy documents in seconds | Can misread a number and state it confidently |
| 2026, a bank might add | An agent asks Meera for missing documents, fills the checklist and flags anything odd | Saves the officer hours of follow-up | Can act on a wrong reading if nobody checks |

Now watch a failure being caught. Suppose the summary counts a one-time Diwali bonus of ₹60,000 as monthly income and reports ₹1,05,000, which would make her EMIs look like only 17% of income. The rule check still uses her salary slips, which show ₹45,000, so the officer sees the mismatch. The newest layer sped up the reading; the older layers caught its mistake.

Each step built on the one before. The rulebook became automatic checks. The credit score sits next to the summary. In every period a person makes the final decision on difficult cases; what changed is how much reading, calculating and chasing they do by hand. That pattern, tools changing the work around a human decision, repeats across AI's history.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking AI began with chat assistants.** It is a seventy-year-old field. Chat assistants are its newest and most visible form.
- **Treating eras as hard dates.** Deep Blue won in 1997 but used the rules approach. Eras describe the main approach of the time, and older approaches keep running.
- **Thinking neural networks are new.** The idea dates from the late 1950s. What arrived after 2010 was enough data and computing power to make large ones work.
- **Assuming progress is steady.** It came in bursts, with winters whenever promises ran far ahead of results.
- **Assuming bigger is always the answer.** Cost, data and running cost limit scaling, which is why reasoning and tools became new levers.
- **Believing every prediction.** Experts have often been wrong about timelines, in both directions. Judge what a system does today.
- **Mixing up the terms.** AI is the field, machine learning is one approach, language models are one kind of machine learning, and an agent is a language model given tools and a goal.
- **Thinking old approaches are dead.** Rules and classical machine learning still run in banks and apps, often as the checks around a language model.
