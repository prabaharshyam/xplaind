## What it is

Artificial intelligence is older than most people think. The phrase was coined in 1956 at a summer workshop in the United States, where researchers believed machines could soon be made to reason like people. Since then, AI has gone through waves of excitement and disappointment, and three broad approaches:

- **Rules written by people** (1950s to 1980s): programs that followed hand-written logic, such as chess programs and "expert systems" for medical diagnosis.
- **Learning from data** (1990s to 2010s): machine learning, where programs find patterns in examples instead of following rules.
- **Large models trained on huge data** (2017 onwards): deep neural networks, and especially language models, that learn general skills from enormous amounts of text and images.

## How it works

**The rules era.** Early AI tried to write down knowledge as rules: "if the patient has fever and a rash, consider measles". This worked in narrow areas but broke easily: the world has too many exceptions to write down. Funding fell twice, in periods called "AI winters".

**The learning era.** Instead of rules, give the computer many examples with answers and let it work out the pattern. Spam filters, product recommendations, credit scores and fraud detection all use this. In 1997 a computer beat the world chess champion. In the 2010s, **deep learning**, using large neural networks, made huge gains in recognising images and speech, helped by more data and faster chips called GPUs.

**The language model era.** In 2017, researchers published a new design called the transformer, which handles long passages of text well. Companies trained ever larger transformer models on huge amounts of text to predict the next word. At a large enough size, these models could write, summarise, translate, answer questions and write code. When chat assistants built on them were released to the public in late 2022, hundreds of millions of people started using AI directly within months.

**Why it sped up.** Three things grew together: data (the internet), computing power (GPUs and large data centres) and better model designs. Investment then poured in, which sped things up further.

**What has not changed.** Every era over-promised. Today's models are far more capable, but they still make confident mistakes, which later lessons explain.

## A worked example

Think about how a bank in India has checked loan applications over the years.

- In the 1990s, an officer used a rulebook: income above a certain level, a guarantor, a set ratio of loan to income. Rules were clear but rigid.
- From the 2000s, credit bureaus and scoring models learned from millions of past loans which patterns predicted repayment. Decisions got faster and more consistent.
- Today, a language model can read a customer's bank statements and salary slips and write a summary for the officer in seconds.

Each step built on the previous one. The officer still makes the decision; the tools changed how much reading and checking they do by hand.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking AI began with chat assistants.** It is a 70-year-old field; chat assistants are its latest, most visible form.
- **Assuming progress is steady.** It came in bursts, with long slow periods between.
- **Believing every prediction.** Experts have often been wrong about timelines, in both directions.
- **Mixing up the terms.** AI is the broad field; machine learning is one approach; language models are one kind of machine learning.
