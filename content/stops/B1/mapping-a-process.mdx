## What it is

Before you automate anything, with AI or without, you need to know how the work actually happens. A **process map** is a diagram of how work flows from a starting event to an outcome. For each step it shows:

- **what** happens;
- **who** does it;
- **which system** or tool is used: email, Tally, a portal, WhatsApp, Excel;
- **how long** it takes, including waiting;
- **where** errors, rework or delays happen;
- **decision points**, such as "is the amount above ₹50,000?".

```text
  Start event                                              Outcome
 +-----------+   +-----------+   +-----------+   +-----------+
 | Invoice   |-->| Typed     |-->| Approved? |-->| Paid      |
 | arrives   |   | into Excel|   |  yes / no |   |           |
 +-----------+   +-----------+   +-----------+   +-----------+
  who: mail room   who: accounts   who: manager    who: accounts
  system: email    system: Excel   wait: 2 days    system: Tally
                   time: 4 min     <- bottleneck   time: 3 min
```

You can draw it on paper, a whiteboard or a free diagram tool. Boxes and arrows are enough. The value is not the drawing; it is the conversation and the numbers that go into it.

This lesson follows the accounts team at a textile exporter in Ludhiana as they map how supplier invoices get paid. By the end you will be able to map a real process, measure it roughly and decide what to change first.

## How it works

### 1. Choose one process with clear edges

Pick a flow with a clear start and end: "from supplier invoice received to payment made", not "accounts". Big, vague processes produce big, vague maps that nobody uses.

### 2. Map the real process, not the official one

The manager's description is usually the official version. The people who do the work know the real one, with its workarounds and waiting. Sit with them and ask them to walk through **the last real case**: "Show me what you did with yesterday's invoices." Write each step on a sticky note or box as they go.

Useful questions:

- "What do you do next?"
- "Where do you get that information from?"
- "How long does that take? How long do you wait?"
- "What happens when it goes wrong?"

### 3. Add the details, especially exceptions

For each step, note who, what system, how long and how often it goes wrong. Then ask about exceptions: "What happens when the GSTIN is missing? When the amount does not match the PO?" Exceptions are rare but slow, and they often take most of the total time.

### 4. Measure roughly

You do not need perfect data. Rough numbers are enough to prioritise and to prove a gain later:

| Measure | How to get it | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Volume | Count last month's cases | 600 invoices a month |
| Hands-on time per item | Time a few real cases | 4 minutes to type one |
| Waiting time | Compare dates in emails or the system | 2 days for approval |
| Exception rate | Count cases that needed extra work | 15% missing a PO number |
| Error or rework rate | Count corrections | 3% typed wrongly |

These numbers become your **baseline**.

### 5. Find the waste

Typical signs:

- the same data typed in two or more places;
- waiting for approvals or replies;
- searching for information in emails or folders;
- checking the same thing twice;
- copying by hand between systems that could talk to each other.

### 6. Decide, step by step

For each wasteful step, choose one of five options:

```text
   Is the step needed at all?
        |
   no --+--> Remove it
        |
  yes --+--> Can it be simpler?           yes --> Simplify it
                  | no
                  v
         Are the rules clear and fixed?   yes --> Automate with rules
                  | no
                  v
         Is it reading or writing text?   yes --> Automate with an AI step
                  | no                            plus a human check
                  v
              Leave it, for now
```

Remove waste before you automate. Automating an unnecessary step just makes the waste faster.

## A worked example

A textile exporter in Ludhiana has an accounts team of two assistants and a manager. They process about 600 supplier invoices a month. The manager thinks the process takes "a few minutes per invoice".

The team maps the real process together, using last Tuesday's invoices:

| Step | Who | System | Time | Problems |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Invoice arrives by email, WhatsApp or courier; paper ones are scanned | Assistant | Email, phone, scanner | 5 min for each paper invoice | Three channels to check |
| 2. Details typed into Excel | Assistant | Excel | 4 min | Typing errors about 3% |
| 3. Purchase order checked | Assistant | Tally | 3 min | 15% have no PO number: phone call to purchase team, about 20 min |
| 4. Manager approves | Manager | Email | Wait: 2 days on average | Biggest delay |
| 5. Details typed again for payment | Assistant | Tally | 3 min | Double data entry |
| 6. Payment scheduled weekly | Assistant | Bank portal | 1 min | Can wait up to a week more |

**Findings.** About 15 minutes of hands-on work per invoice on average once exceptions and paper invoices are counted, which is roughly 150 hours a month. Data is typed twice. And the biggest delay is not typing at all: it is waiting two days for approval.

**Decisions.**

| Waste | Decision |
| --- | --- |
| Three arrival channels | Simplify: ask suppliers to email invoices to one address |
| Typing invoice details | Automate with an AI step that reads the fields, plus an assistant's check |
| Typing again into Tally | Remove: push the checked data into Tally once |
| Waiting for approval | Simplify: one-tap approval on WhatsApp for amounts under ₹1 lakh |
| Missing PO numbers | Remove the exception: require PO numbers from major suppliers |

The map makes the case to the owner, and the baseline numbers will prove the gain in three months.

## Common mistakes

- **Mapping from the manager's description.** Talk to the people who do the work, using a real recent case.
- **Ignoring exceptions.** They are where most of the time hides.
- **No numbers.** Without volumes and times you cannot prioritise or prove improvement.
- **Automating the waste.** Remove and simplify before you automate.
- **Mapping too big a process.** Pick one flow with a clear start and end.
- **Forgetting waiting time.** Delays between steps often matter more than the steps themselves.

## Practise

Read Atlassian's guide to process mapping, linked below, and GOV.UK's guide to the discovery phase.

1. **Interview.** Choose one process you know, such as fee collection at a coaching centre, leave approvals or stock reordering. Interview one person who does it about the last real case.
2. **Draw the map.** Draw it with who, system, time and failure points for every step, and rough numbers for volume and exceptions.
3. **Decide.** Mark the three biggest sources of waste and decide for each: remove, simplify, automate with rules, automate with AI plus a check, or leave it.

**You are done when you can** show your map with numbers and explain which change you would make first and why.
