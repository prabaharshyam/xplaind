## What it is

A **process map** is a diagram of how work flows from a starting event to an outcome. For each step it shows:

- **what** happens,
- **who** does it,
- **which system** or tool is used (email, Tally, a portal, WhatsApp, Excel),
- **how long** it takes, including waiting,
- **where** errors, rework or delays happen,
- **decision points** ("is the amount above ₹50,000?").

You can draw it on paper, a whiteboard or a free diagram tool. Simple boxes and arrows are enough.

## How it works

**Choose one process with clear edges.** For example: "from supplier invoice received to payment made", not "accounts".

**Map the real process, not the official one.** Sit with the people who do the work and ask them to walk through the last real case: "Show me what you did with yesterday's invoices." Write each step on a sticky note or box.

**Add the details.** For each step: who, what system, how long it takes, how often it goes wrong. Ask about exceptions: "What happens when the GSTIN is missing?" Exceptions often take most of the time.

**Measure roughly.** Volume per week or month, time per item, error and rework rates. These become your baseline.

**Find the waste.** Typical signs:

- the same data typed in two or more places;
- waiting for approvals or replies;
- searching for information in emails or folders;
- checking the same thing twice;
- manual copying between systems that could talk to each other.

**Decide.** For each wasteful step, choose: remove it, simplify it, automate it with rules, automate it with an AI step plus a check, or leave it.

## A worked example

A Ludhiana textile exporter's accounts team processes about 600 supplier invoices a month. The manager thinks the process takes "a few minutes per invoice".

Mapping with the two accounts assistants shows:

1. Invoices arrive by email, WhatsApp and courier (paper). Paper ones are scanned. (5 minutes each for paper.)
2. An assistant types invoice details into Excel. (4 minutes.)
3. She checks the purchase order in Tally. (3 minutes; 15% of invoices have no PO number and need a phone call to the purchase team, about 20 minutes each.)
4. The manager approves by email. (Waiting: average 2 days.)
5. Details are typed again into Tally for payment. (3 minutes.)
6. Payment is scheduled weekly.

Findings: about 18 minutes of hands-on work per invoice on average, double data entry, and the biggest delay is waiting for approval, not data entry.

Decisions: ask suppliers to email invoices to one address; automate extraction of invoice fields with an AI step and a check; push data into Tally once; route approval requests to the manager on WhatsApp with one-tap approval for amounts under ₹1 lakh; require PO numbers on invoices from major suppliers. The map makes the case, and the baseline numbers will prove the gain.

## Common mistakes

- **Mapping from the manager's description.** Talk to the people doing the work.
- **Ignoring exceptions.** They often take most of the time.
- **No numbers.** Without volumes and times you cannot prioritise or prove improvement.
- **Automating the waste.** Remove unnecessary steps before automating.
- **Mapping too big a process.** Pick one flow with a clear start and end.

## Practise

Read Atlassian's guide to process mapping and GOV.UK's guide to the discovery phase.

1. Choose one process you know (fee collection at a coaching centre, leave approvals, stock reordering). Interview one person who does it about the last real case.
2. Draw the map with who, system, time and failure points for every step.
3. Mark the three biggest sources of waste and decide for each: remove, simplify, automate with rules, automate with AI plus a check, or leave.

**You are done when you can** show your map with numbers and explain which change you would make first and why.
