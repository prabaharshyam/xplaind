## What it is

"AI job" is not one job. Companies hire for a family of roles, and the titles change from company to company. A "GenAI developer" at one firm does what an "AI engineer" does at another, and a post titled "AI engineer" may really be data analysis. So the first rule of an AI career is: **read the duties in the job description, not the title.**

The most useful way to sort the roles is by how much code you write in a normal week:

```flow
dir: LR
title: How much code the role needs
No code | AI product manager, AI governance, AI in your own field -> Some code | AI automation specialist, AI security engineer, AI evaluation
Some code -> Code every day | AI, GenAI and agentic AI engineers, FDE, ML engineer, MLOps, data engineer
```

### Roles that write code every day

- **AI engineer** builds AI systems end to end. In India the title usually means applications on language models, but many posts also expect classical machine learning basics.
- **Generative AI and LLM engineer** goes deep on language models: prompts, structured output, RAG, evals and serving costs.
- **Agentic AI engineer** builds agents that plan, call tools and take actions, and makes them safe and observable.
- **Forward deployed engineer (FDE)** builds these systems inside a customer's business. Half the job is engineering; the other half is finding the right problem and getting people to use the tool.
- **ML engineer** trains and deploys the company's own prediction models: fraud, credit risk, ranking, forecasting, vision. The signal is labelled data, features and retraining.
- **MLOps engineer** runs models in production: serving, monitoring drift and cost, safe releases and the shared platform.
- **Data engineer** builds the pipelines that move and clean the data every model depends on. No AI system works without this, so it is a common and steady way into AI teams.
- **Data scientist and applied scientist** analyse data, run experiments and design new models. Applied scientist roles at big tech and research labs often expect a master's or PhD and published work.

### Roles that need some code

- **AI automation specialist** connects business apps with tools such as n8n, Make or Power Automate and adds AI steps. You will read APIs and JSON and write small scripts.
- **AI security engineer** defends AI systems and uses AI to defend. A normal week includes threat-modelling a chatbot, testing it for prompt injection and data leaks, and reviewing what an agent is allowed to touch. It builds on security basics plus light Python, and few people have both skill sets.
- **AI evaluation and data quality roles** write test questions, grading rubrics and labelling guidelines, and judge model answers. Many begin as annotation or rating work. The steady entry point is domain expertise: a lawyer who rates legal answers, or a Tamil speaker who judges Tamil output. Rating work alone rarely grows into a senior role; building evals as an engineer does.

### Roles that need no code

- **AI product manager** decides which AI features to build, how to measure quality and what to do when the model is wrong.
- **AI governance and compliance specialist** checks that AI is safe, fair and lawful: risk assessments, policies, audits and incident handling, under laws such as India's Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act and, for EU customers, the EU AI Act.
- **Your current field, plus AI.** AI-literate marketers, HR staff, teachers, lawyers and doctors are valued without changing title at all.

Xplaind has a roadmap for each of the ten named roles: AI Engineer, Generative AI and LLM Engineer, Agentic AI Engineer, Forward Deployed Engineer, ML Engineer, MLOps Engineer, AI Automation Specialist, AI Security Engineer, AI Product Manager, and AI Governance and Compliance Specialist.

## How it works

### Where the jobs are

| Employer type | What they build | What they look for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IT services firms | AI solutions for global clients | Delivery, client skills, sometimes cloud certifications; large fresher intakes |
| Product companies and startups | AI features in their own products | Strong builders, speed, deployed projects |
| Global capability centres (GCCs) | AI for a multinational's own business | Engineering quality, domain knowledge, security awareness |
| Consulting firms | Strategy and implementation | Communication, problem framing, delivery |
| Overseas companies, remote | Product work | An excellent portfolio and clear writing; very competitive |

GCCs deserve special attention. The Nasscom-Zinnov India GCC Landscape report (May 2026) counted 2,117 GCCs employing about 2.36 million people in FY26, with Bengaluru the largest hub. A bank's or retailer's GCC hires engineers to build AI for its own business, so domain knowledge counts more than at an IT services firm.

How big is AI hiring overall? foundit's job-posting tracker counted 2,90,256 AI-linked job posts in India in 2025, led by IT-software and banking and finance, with Bengaluru the top hub. It forecast 32% growth in 2026. Read such numbers carefully: a post is not a hire, one opening can be posted several times, and "AI-linked" may include jobs that only mention AI.

### How hiring works

**Where to look.** Start with Naukri and LinkedIn. Instahyre and foundit are also widely used, and Wellfound lists many startups. GCCs often hire through their own careers pages. A referral from someone inside is often the fastest route to a human reader, so alumni and seniors matter more than a hundred cold applications.

**Typical rounds for an engineering role.** The steps vary by company, but the common pattern is:

1. An online coding test or a take-home task.
2. A technical round on Python, APIs and AI basics, such as "how would you stop this RAG app making up answers?"
3. A project deep dive. They pick one project from your resume and ask why you made each choice, how you measured it and what failed. Fake or copied projects fall apart here.
4. For experienced hires, a design round: "design a support assistant for 10,000 users a day".
5. A manager or HR round on teamwork, salary and notice period.

Product, governance and security roles swap the coding test for case studies: write a spec, assess the risk of a feature, or find the weakness in a chatbot.

### What employers look for

- **Engineering roles:** Python, APIs, Git and one cloud; language-model skills such as prompting, RAG, agents and evals; projects that show a deployed, measured system, not a notebook.
- **Product, governance and automation roles:** knowing what AI can and cannot do, framing problems, defining metrics, mapping processes and writing clearly.
- **Everyone:** evidence. A project with a test set and a number, or a story of something you delivered at work, beats a list of certificates. Certificates often help most at IT services firms and for cloud-heavy roles, as a tiebreaker, not as the main proof.

Three gotchas when you read this table:

- **The high early bands are rarely fresher jobs.** Most FDE and AI product manager posts ask for prior engineering or product experience, so the "0 to 2 years" figure applies to the few who get in early.
- **CTC is not take-home pay.** It often includes variable pay, joining bonuses and stock that may never pay out in full.
- **Specialist pay follows the person, not the title.** An ML engineer who ships fraud models at a bank can out-earn a "GenAI engineer" who has only built demos.

### Timelines and the fresher market

As a rough guide, Xplaind's roadmaps take about three months of steady study for the product, automation and governance paths, four for agentic AI, six for FDE, MLOps and AI security, and nine for AI engineer, GenAI engineer and ML engineer. That is learning time. Plan for the job search to take a few months more.

Freshers have the hardest time. Many posts ask for two or more years of experience, and AI now does much of the routine work juniors used to learn on (see the lesson "AI and jobs"). Freshers get in through IT services hiring, internships that convert, GCC graduate programmes and small startups that value projects over degrees. Experienced people usually switch fastest **inside their own company**, by joining an AI pilot.

### How to read a job description

Look at the verbs and nouns in the duties, not the title. These signals tell you which role a post really is:

| If the duties say | It is really |
| --- | --- |
| LLM APIs, RAG, vector database, agents, evals | AI or GenAI engineer |
| Train models, features, labelled data, precision and recall, retrain | ML engineer |
| Kubernetes, model serving, monitoring drift, CI/CD, GPU cost | MLOps engineer |
| Pipelines, Spark, data warehouse, data quality | Data engineer |
| Work at client sites, discovery, demos, integration | Forward deployed engineer |
| Prompt injection, red teaming, threat model, OWASP | AI security engineer |
| Write rubrics, rate answers, labelling guidelines | AI evaluation or annotation |
| Dashboards, Excel, Power BI, "exposure to ChatGPT a plus" | Data analyst with an AI label |

## A worked example

### Decoding one job post

Suppose Sneha, a final-year B.Tech student in Chennai, finds this post (an invented example, but typical):

> **GenAI Engineer, fintech, Chennai, 0 to 2 years.** Build features from UPI transaction data. Train and tune gradient-boosted models to flag fraud. Track precision and recall weekly and retrain monthly. Python, SQL, scikit-learn. Exposure to LLMs is a plus.

The title says GenAI. The duties say features, training, precision, recall and retraining, and LLMs are only "a plus". Using the table, this is an **ML engineer** job. It is a good job, but if Sneha applies with three RAG projects she will look like the wrong fit. She marks it "ML, apply only if I add one tabular project" and moves on.

She reads twenty posts this way. Fourteen are real AI engineer work, four are ML engineer work and two are analyst jobs with an AI label. The skills that repeat in the fourteen are Python, RAG, evaluation and deployment, so that is her plan.

### Sneha's switch, month by month

The numbers below are an illustration of a realistic path, not a survey.

- **Months 1 to 3:** Git, APIs and her first language-model calls on the AI Engineer roadmap. She already knows Python basics, so she skips part of the first stage and finishes the learning in seven months instead of nine.
- **Months 4 to 7:** three projects, each deployed and each with a short evaluation report.
  1. **A question-answering assistant over her university's 200-page academic regulations.** She writes 60 test questions with correct answers. Answer accuracy is 68% at first, mostly because rules split across two pages were lost. Changing how she splits the text lifts it to 85%. She also measures how often the cited page really supports the answer.
  2. **An invoice extractor** that turns scanned GST invoices into JSON: GSTIN, date, total and tax. On 100 invoices she measures accuracy field by field. Adding a check that every GSTIN is exactly 15 characters catches many reading errors.
  3. **A support-ticket agent** that reads a complaint, looks up the order with a tool call and drafts a reply. She measures how often it picks the right tool and its cost per ticket.
- **Months 8 to 10:** applications. Her first 30 get almost no replies. The cause is her resume: it lists tools ("LangChain, FAISS, FastAPI") instead of results. She rewrites each bullet as a result: "raised answer accuracy from 68% to 85% on a 60-question test set". Over the next 50 applications, plus five alumni referrals, she gets eight first rounds.
- **What went wrong.** In one project deep dive, the interviewer asks why her assistant failed on the remaining 15% of questions. She has not looked, and she is rejected. From then on she keeps a written list of failure cases for every project.
- **Result:** an AI engineer offer at a mid-size product company in Chennai at ₹9 lakh CTC, inside the 6 to 12 band for 0 to 2 years.

### Two shorter cases

**Rahul** has five years as a Java backend developer at an IT services firm and enjoys client calls. He follows the FDE roadmap in the evenings and volunteers for his company's first AI pilot, a contract-search tool for a client, where his integration skills matter most. The friction: his first demo impresses, but the client's staff ignore the tool for weeks, until he sits with them and learns they need answers inside the system they already use. That lesson, told well, gets him moved into the AI practice about seven months later.

**Kavitha** is an operations manager at a Chennai hospital chain and has never coded. She takes the AI Product Manager roadmap and the automation lessons. She leads a pilot that drafts discharge summaries for doctors to review, and measures minutes saved per summary and errors the doctors catch. The friction: the first version saves time but invents a medicine dose twice in the first week, so she adds a rule that every dose must be checked against the prescription before a doctor signs. The pilot, with that safeguard, becomes her new role as AI product lead.

| Person | Background | Role chosen | Main evidence | Time taken |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sneha | Student, Python | AI engineer | Three deployed projects with test sets | About 10 months |
| Rahul | Java developer, likes clients | Forward deployed engineer | An internal AI pilot that people use | About 7 months |
| Kavitha | Hospital operations, no code | AI product lead | A measured pilot with a safety rule | About 6 months |

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing by title, hype or the highest salary.** Read twenty real job descriptions and talk to someone in the role before you commit.
- **Applying to every post with "AI" in the title.** An ML or analyst job labelled GenAI wastes your application and the recruiter's time.
- **Collecting certificates instead of projects.** Certificates show you studied. Employers want proof you can build or deliver.
- **Projects with no numbers.** A deployed demo without a test set looks like every other resume. Measure it and know where it fails.
- **Ignoring your existing strengths.** A security analyst, a banker or a nurse who adds AI skills is rarer than one more fresh GenAI engineer.
- **Trying to learn everything.** Pick one roadmap and go deep; you can widen later.
- **Expecting overnight results.** Plan for months of learning and months of searching.
- **Applying only to the biggest names.** GCCs, mid-size product firms and IT services AI practices often hire more people between them.

## Practice

Watch the video lessons in the Watch & learn panel for a wider view of the skills.

1. **Decode ten job descriptions.** Find ten AI posts in India on Naukri or LinkedIn. For each, write the title, the three most-mentioned duties, which role it really is (use the table above) and whether it needs coding.
2. **Match yourself.** Pick the two roles that best fit your background. For each, list what you already have, what you lack and the salary band on the Jobs page.
3. **Plan one piece of evidence.** Choose one project or work pilot you could finish in eight weeks, and write how you will measure it.
4. **Talk to a person.** Message one person working in one of those roles, such as a senior, an alumnus or a LinkedIn contact, and ask what a normal week looks like.

**You are done when you can** name the AI role that suits you best, give three reasons based on job posts rather than hype, and describe the first piece of evidence you will build.
