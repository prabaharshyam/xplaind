## What it is

Writing is where AI assistants save the most time for most people. A language model is, at its core, a machine for producing text, so it is very good at four everyday writing jobs:

| Job | What you give it | What you get |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Drafting** | Rough notes, the reader, the goal | A first version of an email, report section or post |
| **Rewriting** | Your text and how to change it | Shorter, clearer, kinder, more formal, or simpler |
| **Translating** | Text and a target language | A version in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali and others, or plain English for non-experts |
| **Summarising** | A long document, thread or transcript | Key points, decisions and actions |

In all four, **you stay the author**. You decide what is said, you check it, and you own it. The assistant is a fast junior writer, not the person who signs the email.

## How it works

### Drafting from your notes

A good drafting prompt has three parts: **your points, the reader, and what you want the reader to do.** Add a length limit if it matters.

> "Here are my notes from the vendor meeting. Draft an email to my manager summarising the three options and recommending option 2. She is busy, so keep it under 150 words and put the recommendation first."

Without the reader and the goal, you get generic text that you then rewrite from scratch.

### Keeping your voice

Readers notice text that sounds machine-made: "I hope this email finds you well", "delve into", long lists of three adjectives. To avoid it:

1. Paste one or two of your own past messages and ask the assistant to match the style.
2. Ask for plain, short sentences.
3. Delete phrases you would never say out loud.

### Rewriting

Rewriting is often more useful than drafting, because the facts are already yours:

- "Make this kinder but still firm."
- "Explain this for a customer with no technical background."
- "Cut this to half the length; keep every number."

### Translating

Good assistants handle Hindi, Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, Telugu and other Indian languages reasonably well, but quality varies, especially for formal writing, legal terms and regional usage. For anything important, such as official letters, health or legal information, or messages to many customers, have a fluent speaker check it.

### Summarising

Say exactly what kind of summary you need. "Summarise this" gives you a vague paragraph. This gives you something you can act on:

> "From this transcript, list: decisions made, open questions, and actions with owner and deadline. Quote the exact words wherever a deadline was agreed."

Summaries can drop important details, soften deadlines or invent points that were never made. Check anything you will act on against the source.

### Responsibility and privacy

If you send it, you said it. Before sending, check:

| Check | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Names and titles | Models swap or misspell names |
| Numbers and dates | The most common silent error |
| Promises and commitments | A draft may promise something you cannot deliver |
| Tone | Cheerful text about bad news reads badly |
| Confidential content | Only use tools your organisation approves for that data |

## A worked example

Fatima is an operations lead at a logistics company in Kolkata. A shipment for an important client is delayed. She has a one-hour meeting transcript and 40 emails about it, and the client wants an update today.

**Summarise.** In the company's approved assistant she pastes the transcript and asks: "Summarise into decisions made, open issues, and actions with owner and deadline. Quote the exact words where deadlines were agreed." In two minutes she has a one-page summary.

She checks the three deadlines against the quotes. One summary line says "Friday", but the quoted words are "end of next week". She corrects it. That single check is the most valuable minute of the morning.

**Draft.** Next: "Draft an update email to the client in a calm, professional tone. Apologise for the delay without blaming our partner, give the new delivery date of 14 November, and offer a call this week. Under 150 words." She pastes one of her old emails as a style example.

**Edit.** The draft is good. She cuts "We sincerely regret any inconvenience this may have caused", which sounds like a form letter, adds a personal line about the client's festival-season stock, and checks the date once more.

| Step | Time with AI | Usual time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Read transcript and emails, find decisions | 10 minutes (summary plus checks) | 60 minutes |
| Write the client email | 15 minutes (draft plus edits) | 45 minutes |
| Total | 25 minutes | about 1 hour 45 minutes |

She saved time and still checked everything that mattered: the deadline, the date and the tone.

## Common mistakes

- **Sending without reading.** Errors and odd phrases go out under your name.
- **Bland, machine-sounding text.** Give a style example and edit until it sounds like you.
- **Trusting summaries for decisions.** Check details you will act on against the source, especially deadlines and numbers.
- **Unchecked translations for important messages.** Get a fluent speaker to read them.
- **Pasting confidential documents into unapproved tools.** Follow your organisation's rules.
- **Over-long output.** Ask for a word limit. Shorter is usually better, especially for busy readers.

## Practise

Use any assistant your school or workplace allows, and the AI Fluency course linked below for good habits.

1. **Draft and edit.** Draft a real email from your rough notes, then edit it until it sounds like you. Count how many changes you made, and which kind: facts, tone or style.
2. **Summarise and check.** Summarise a long article or report into decisions, numbers and open questions. Check three points against the original.
3. **Translate and mark.** Translate a short notice into an Indian language you know well, and mark every error or awkward phrase.

**You are done when you can** show a draft, your edits and a summary you checked, and say which things you would never send unchecked.
