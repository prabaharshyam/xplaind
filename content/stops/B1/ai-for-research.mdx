## What it is

AI assistants can save hours of research, but they can also hand you a confident wrong answer that you repeat in a meeting. The skill is not "using AI for research". It is **using AI for research and checking it fast**.

Assistants help in three ways:

- **Exploring a topic:** an overview, key terms, the main arguments and what to look up next.
- **Asking questions of your own material:** uploading a report, policy or spreadsheet and asking about it.
- **Comparing and thinking:** weighing options, listing pros and cons, finding holes in a plan.

How much you can trust the answer depends on **where it came from**:

```text
 Where the answer comes from          How checkable it is
+-------------------------------+   +--------------------------+
| Model memory only             |-->| Hard: no source to open  |
| Web search with links         |-->| Good: open the links     |
| Your uploaded document        |-->| Best: check the page     |
| Spreadsheet + calculation     |-->| Good: recompute a sample |
+-------------------------------+   +--------------------------+
```

This lesson follows Meena, who runs a small shop in Madurai, as she uses an assistant to understand a tax decision. By the end you will know how to explore, ground and check research with AI.

## How it works

### Exploring a topic

Start broad, then narrow. "Explain how GST input tax credit works for a small trader, in simple terms, with an example" gives you a map. Then ask, "What are the key terms I should search on the official GST portal?" so you can go to the source. Treat the overview as a map, not the territory: it tells you where to look, not what is true.

### Answers with sources

When the assistant searches the web, it shows links. Open them. For each one, check three things:

1. **Does it exist?** Links can be broken or invented.
2. **Is it reliable?** Official sites (government portals, regulators like RBI or SEBI, NPCI for UPI) and reputable publishers beat anonymous blogs.
3. **Does it say what the answer claims?** Models sometimes cite a real page that does not support the claim.

Fact-checkers call the habit of leaving a page to see what other sources say **lateral reading**. It is faster and more reliable than staring at one page and judging it by its design.

### Grounding in your documents

Uploading a document **grounds** the answer in it. Say exactly what you want: "Answer only from this document and give the page number for each point." Then check the cited pages. If the document does not contain the answer, a good assistant says so. If it answers anyway, it may be filling the gap from memory.

### Spreadsheets

Many assistants analyse spreadsheets by writing and running code behind the scenes: totals, averages, charts and trends. This is powerful, and it fails in boring ways:

| Problem | What goes wrong | How to catch it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Misread column | Adds "invoice number" instead of "amount" | Ask which column it used |
| Merged cells or extra headers | Skips or double-counts rows | Check the row count |
| Indian number formats | Reads 1,00,000 wrongly | Spot-check large numbers |
| Returns or refunds as positives | Inflates totals | Recompute one month by hand |
| Text in number columns | Silently drops rows | Ask how many rows were used |

Always ask, "Show how you calculated each number," and recompute two or three yourself.

### A thinking partner

Some uses do not need perfect facts. "Here is my plan to open a second shop; what could go wrong?" or "Argue the opposite view" help you think more clearly, even if one point is weak.

## A worked example

Meena owns a garments shop in Madurai. She wants to know whether to register under the GST composition scheme, which offers simpler filing for small businesses.

**Step 1: explore.** She asks for a simple explanation of the scheme, who can use it, and its main pros and cons. The answer is clear and well organised. She knows tax rules change, so she asks for official sources. With web search on, the assistant links to the GST portal and a government FAQ. She opens both. One turnover limit in the answer is out of date; the official page shows the current figure. She notes the correct one.

**Step 2: ground in her data.** She uploads last year's sales spreadsheet and asks for monthly turnover and the yearly total, with the calculation shown. She checks two months by hand. One total is too high: a row of customer returns was entered as a positive number. She fixes the sheet and runs the analysis again.

**Step 3: think.** She asks, "Given these numbers, what questions should I ask my accountant about switching?" She gets six useful questions, including what happens to input tax credit and whether she can sell to other states.

| Step | What the AI did | What Meena checked | What she found |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Explore | Explained the scheme | Official GST pages | One limit was out of date |
| Ground | Totalled her sales | Two months by hand | Returns counted as sales |
| Think | Listed questions | Nothing needed | Six good questions |

AI saved her hours of reading and gave her a much better conversation with her accountant. The accountant, not the assistant, made the final call.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting answers without sources.** Ask for sources, and open them.
- **Believing citations without reading them.** A real link can still fail to support the claim.
- **Accepting unsourced statistics.** Precise-sounding numbers with no source are a classic hallucination.
- **Not checking spreadsheet maths.** Spot-check totals by hand, and ask which columns were used.
- **Using AI as the final authority on tax, law or health.** Use it to prepare, then confirm with official sources or professionals.
- **Uploading private documents to unapproved tools.** Follow your organisation's rules about which tools may see which data.

## Practise

Browse OpenAI Academy's sessions on research, linked below, and try one lesson on lateral reading from Civic Online Reasoning.

1. **Research with sources.** Research a topic you need for work or study. Ask for sources, open each one, and mark which claims held up, which were outdated and which had no support.
2. **Ground in a document.** Upload a public report and ask five questions with page references. Check every reference.
3. **Check a spreadsheet.** Analyse a small spreadsheet with an assistant and verify two of its numbers by hand.

**You are done when you can** show one claim you caught as wrong or outdated, and explain exactly how you found out.
