## What it is

AI assistants can help you research and analyse in three ways:

- **Exploring a topic**: getting an overview, key terms, the main arguments and what to look up next.
- **Asking questions of your own material**: uploading a report, policy or spreadsheet and asking about it.
- **Comparing and thinking**: weighing options, listing pros and cons, finding gaps in a plan.

Many assistants can also search the web and show links. That changes the reliability: an answer with links you can open is checkable; an answer from the model's memory alone is not.

## How it works

**Exploring.** Start broad ("Explain how GST input tax credit works for a small trader, in simple terms, with an example") and then narrow. Ask for the key terms so you can search official sources. Treat the overview as a map, not the territory.

**With sources.** When the assistant searches the web, open the links it cites. Check that the source exists, is reliable (official sites, reputable publishers) and actually says what the answer claims. Fact-checkers call this lateral reading: leave the page and see what other sources say.

**With your documents.** Uploading a document grounds the answer in it. Ask for page or section references: "Answer only from this document and cite the page." Then check the cited parts. If the document does not contain the answer, a good answer says so.

**With spreadsheets.** Many assistants can analyse a spreadsheet by writing and running code behind the scenes: totals, averages, charts, trends. Ask them to show how they calculated each number, and check a couple by hand. Watch for misread columns, merged cells and Indian number formats.

**Thinking partner.** "Here is my plan to open a second shop; what could go wrong?" "Argue the opposite view." These uses do not need facts to be perfect; they help you think.

## A worked example

Meena, a small-business owner in Madurai, wants to understand whether to register under the GST composition scheme.

1. She asks for a simple explanation of the scheme, who can use it and its main pros and cons. The answer is clear but she knows tax rules change, so she asks for official sources. The assistant, with web search on, links to the GST portal and a government FAQ. She opens both and finds that one turnover limit in the answer was out of date; the official page has the current figure.
2. She uploads last year's sales spreadsheet and asks for monthly turnover totals and the year's total, with the calculation shown. She checks two months by hand: one total was off because a row of returns was entered as a positive number. She fixes the sheet and reruns.
3. She asks: "Given these numbers, what questions should I ask my accountant about switching?" She gets six good questions and takes them to her accountant, who makes the final call.

AI saved her hours of reading and gave her a better conversation with a professional. It did not make the decision.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting answers without sources.** Ask for sources and open them.
- **Believing citations without reading them.** Links can be wrong or not say what is claimed.
- **Not checking spreadsheet maths.** Spot-check totals by hand.
- **Using AI as the final authority on tax, law or health.** Use it to prepare, then confirm with official sources or professionals.
- **Uploading private documents to unapproved tools.**

## Practise

Browse OpenAI Academy's sessions on research, and try one Civic Online Reasoning lesson on lateral reading.

1. Research a topic you need for work or study with an assistant. Ask for sources, open each one, and mark which claims held up.
2. Upload a document (a public report works) and ask five questions with page references. Check each reference.
3. Analyse a small spreadsheet with the assistant and verify two of its numbers by hand.

**You are done when you can** show one claim you caught as wrong or outdated and how you found out.
