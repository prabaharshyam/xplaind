## What it is

Research means turning a question into a decision you can defend. An AI assistant can do the slow parts fast: finding sources, reading long documents, summarising, comparing and calculating. You still own three things: **the question, the sources and the conclusion.**

This lesson is about the research workflow: how to frame a question, which assistant mode to use, how to make it show its evidence, and how to write up what you found. The lesson "Checking an AI answer" covers the general routine for checking any single answer (stakes, checkable claims, reliable sources). Here we build on it for longer research jobs.

The first decision is **which mode to use**, because each mode fails in its own way:

```flow
title: Pick the mode that fits the question
Your research question -[a broad first map]-> Plain chat
Your research question -[current facts and rules]-> Web search with links
Your research question -[a wide survey of many sources]-> Deep research report
Your research question -[answers from your own files]-> Document notebook
Your research question -[numbers in a sheet]-> Spreadsheet analysis
```

| Mode | Good for | Typical failure | Your check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plain chat (memory only) | Key terms, an overview, what to search next | Old or invented facts, no source to open | Treat every fact as a lead, not a finding |
| Web search with links | Current rules, figures and news | A real link that does not support the claim; a blog chosen over the official page | Find the exact sentence on the page |
| Deep research report | A landscape: many sources, options, arguments | Long reports with many weakly supporting citations | Trace only the claims your decision rests on |
| Document notebook or upload | Questions about your reports, policies, papers | Filling gaps from memory; misread tables in scanned PDFs | Ask a question the document cannot answer |
| Spreadsheet analysis | Totals, trends, charts | Wrong column, dropped rows, bad data | Check row count, recompute one sample |

We will follow Meena, who runs a garments shop in Madurai, as she uses an assistant to research one tax decision from start to finish.

## How it works

### Frame the question before you ask

The wording of a question shapes the answer. Assistants tend to agree with the person asking, a habit researchers call **sycophancy**, so a leading question can get a one-sided answer.

- **Leading:** "Why is the GST composition scheme better for small shops?"
- **Neutral:** "For a garments shop in Tamil Nadu, what are the advantages and disadvantages of the GST composition scheme, and who is not allowed to use it?"

A good research question names the **place** (rules differ by state and country), the **time** ("as of this year"), the **scope** (who, what size of business) and the **output** you want (a table, a list of pros and cons, questions for an expert).

Two prompts reduce bias further:

- "What is the strongest case against this?"
- "What evidence would change your answer?"

### Explore, and watch the date

Start broad: "Explain this topic simply, list the key terms, and tell me which official sources cover it." Treat the overview as a **map, not the territory**. It tells you where to look, not what is true.

Every model is trained on data up to a **knowledge cutoff**, and it does not know what changed after that. The most common research error is a rule, rate, limit or price that was true a few years ago, stated as current. So follow one rule: **for anything that changes, find the date of the source and confirm it on the official site.** If the answer came from memory alone, assume it may be stale.

### Make web answers show their evidence

When an assistant searches the web, it shows links. Opening them is the start, not the end. The key test is **support**: does the page actually say what the answer claims? A real, official page can be cited for a claim it never makes.

Make the assistant do part of this work:

- "For each claim, quote the exact sentence from the source that supports it, and give the date of the page."
- "Which of these sources is the original, and which are reporting someone else's figure?"

Then go **upstream**. If a blog quotes an NPCI figure, open NPCI. If a news story cites a government circular, open the circular. Secondary sources copy each other, so five pages repeating one number are still one source.

This is not a rare problem. In a March 2025 test by the Tow Center for Digital Journalism, eight AI search tools were asked to identify the source of short news excerpts, and they gave wrong answers to more than 60% of the queries.

### Deep research modes

Several assistants now offer a **deep research** mode (OpenAI launched one in ChatGPT in February 2025, and other assistants have their own). Instead of one quick search, an agent searches and reads many pages over several minutes, then writes a long report with dozens of citations. OpenAI itself warned at launch that it can still state wrong facts and draw wrong conclusions.

Use deep research for a **wide first survey**: "What options exist, what do sources say about each, and where do they disagree?" Do not use it as the final word. The gotcha is that **more citations mean more checking, not more trust.** A 20-page report looks thorough, but it can mix old and new sources, count copied pages as agreement, and attach a citation to a sentence the source only loosely supports.

A practical rule: before you read the report, write down the **three to five claims your decision depends on**. Ask the assistant for a table of those claims with the source, the date and the supporting quote for each. Check those against the original sources, and treat the rest of the report as background.

### Ground answers in your own documents

**Grounding** means making the model answer from material you give it, instead of from memory. You can upload a file to a chat, or use a notebook tool built for this, such as Google's Gemini Notebook (called NotebookLM until July 2026). Google says its chat answers from the sources you add and gives citations you can click to see the quoted passage in the source.

Many tools do this with **retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)**: they search your documents for the passages that match your question, then write the answer from those passages. If the search picks the wrong passage, the answer is wrong, even though it cites your file.

A strong grounding prompt:

> Answer only from the attached documents. For each point, quote the sentence and give the page number. If the documents do not answer the question, reply "Not in the documents".

Then run a **canary test**: ask one question you know the documents do not answer. If the assistant answers it anyway, it is filling gaps from memory, and you should check its other answers more strictly. Also check numbers taken from tables in scanned PDFs, which can be misread.

### Academic sources

Students and analysts often ask for research papers. Models can invent citations that look real, a form of **hallucination**: a real journal name, plausible authors and a title that does not exist. In a 2023 study published in Scientific Reports, 55% of the citations produced by GPT-3.5 and 18% of those produced by GPT-4 were fabricated. Whatever tool you use, check every paper before you rely on it:

1. Search the exact title in Google Scholar or on the publisher's site. If you cannot find it, do not cite it.
2. Read the abstract. Check that the paper studied what the claim says (the same people, the same outcome).
3. Cite the paper you read, not the assistant's summary of it.

### Spreadsheets

Many assistants analyse a spreadsheet by writing and running code behind the scenes. The maths is usually right; the **data and the column choice** are where it fails:

| Problem | What goes wrong | How to catch it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wrong column | Totals "invoice number" instead of "amount" | Ask which column it used |
| Merged cells or extra header rows | Skips or double-counts rows | Compare its row count with yours |
| Numbers stored as text | Values like 1,00,000 typed with lakh commas in a CSV are read as text or dropped | Ask how many rows were used in each total |
| Returns stored as positive numbers | Refunds are added to sales | Recompute one month by hand |
| Blank or "NA" cells | Rows silently left out of averages | Ask how missing values were handled |

Always ask: "Show the calculation and the code, and tell me how many rows you used." Then recompute one or two numbers yourself.

### Write up what you found

End with a short **research note**, not a pasted AI answer. For each claim, mark how strong the evidence is:

- **Verified:** you read it in an original or official source, and you note the date.
- **Likely:** a reputable secondary source says it, but you have not seen the original.
- **Unverified:** only the assistant says it. Do not rely on it.

This note is what you share with your manager, teacher or accountant. It shows what you know, how you know it, and what is still open.

## A worked example

Meena owns a garments shop in Madurai. She wants to know whether to join the **GST composition scheme**, which lets small businesses pay tax at a fixed rate on turnover with simpler filing. Her shop's numbers below are made up for this example; the GST rules are real and were checked in November 2026.

**Step 1: frame and explore.** Her first draft was "Why is the composition scheme good for my shop?". She rewrites it to be neutral:

> I run a garments shop in Tamil Nadu. As of 2026, what are the advantages and disadvantages of the GST composition scheme, what is the turnover limit, and who cannot use it? Give the date and source for each rule.

With web search on, the assistant replies that shops with turnover **up to ₹1 crore** can join, and cites two pages: an official government page and a tax blog.

**Step 2: check support and the date.** She asks, "Quote the sentence that gives the ₹1 crore limit, with the date of the page." The quote comes from the blog, written in 2018. The official page says something different. She confirms on official GST and government sites that the limit for goods suppliers in most states, including Tamil Nadu, is **₹1.5 crore**, raised from ₹1 crore with effect from 1 April 2019. (The limit is ₹75 lakh in some special category states.) The assistant had used an old figure from a secondary source.

**Step 3: ground in her data.** She uploads last year's sales sheet:

> Using only this sheet, give monthly sales and the yearly total. Show the calculation and tell me how many rows you used.

The assistant reports a yearly total of **₹1,41,10,000**. She checks October, her busiest month because of Deepavali. The assistant shows ₹19,02,000. Her own sum gives ₹17,78,000: sales of ₹18,40,000 minus returns of ₹62,000. The returns were stored as positive numbers, so they were **added** instead of subtracted. That error doubles the effect: the gap is 2 × ₹62,000 = ₹1,24,000.

She fixes the returns column and runs it again. Across the year, sales were ₹1,38,00,000 and returns ₹3,10,000, so her net sales are **₹1,34,90,000**, not ₹1,41,10,000.

Now the Step 2 error shows its cost. With the old ₹1 crore limit, she would have wrongly ruled herself out. If net sales are the turnover that counts (a question for her accountant), she is ₹15,10,000 below the ₹1.5 crore limit, and about 11% growth next year would take her over it.

**Step 4: ask for the case against.** She asks, "What are the strongest reasons a shop like mine should not join?" Two points stand out. Composition taxpayers cannot claim input tax credit on their purchases, and they cannot make inter-state supplies of goods. Meena had planned to start sending orders to customers in Kerala. She confirms both rules on an official source.

**Step 5: write the note.**

| Claim | Evidence | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Limit for goods is ₹1.5 crore in Tamil Nadu | Official GST pages, in force since 1 April 2019 | Verified (checked November 2026) |
| Her net sales were ₹1,34,90,000 | Her own sheet, corrected and recomputed | Verified |
| No input tax credit; no inter-state supply of goods | Official source | Verified |
| Whether net sales are the turnover that counts, and so her headroom | Not found yet | Open: ask the accountant |

She takes the note and four questions to her accountant, including what happens if she crosses the limit mid-year and how the Kerala plan changes the choice. The assistant saved her hours of reading; the accountant made the final call.

## Common mistakes

- **Asking a leading question.** "Why is X better?" gets a case for X. Ask for both sides and the case against.
- **Using memory-only answers for current rules.** Limits, rates and prices change. Find the date and the official page.
- **Stopping at a secondary source.** A blog or news story repeating a figure is not the original. Go upstream.
- **Trusting a report because it is long.** Dozens of citations mean more to check. Trace the few claims your decision rests on.
- **Skipping the canary test.** If you never ask a question the document cannot answer, you do not know whether it is grounded.
- **Citing papers you have not opened.** Find each title in Google Scholar and read the abstract.
- **Accepting a spreadsheet total without the row count.** Dropped or double-counted rows are invisible unless you ask.
- **Pasting the AI's answer as your finding.** Write a short note that says what is verified and what is still open.
- **Uploading private documents to an unapproved tool.** Follow your organisation's rules about which tools may see which data.

## Practice

Browse OpenAI Academy's sessions on research, linked below, and try one lateral reading lesson from Civic Online Reasoning.

1. **Frame, then search.** Pick a rule or figure you need for work or study. Write a leading version and a neutral version of the question, run both, and note how the answers differ. For the neutral one, ask for the supporting quote and date for each claim, and go upstream to the original source.
2. **Ground and test.** Upload a public report and ask five questions with page references and quotes. Add one canary question the report cannot answer. Check every reference and record how the assistant handled the canary.
3. **Check a sheet.** Analyse a small spreadsheet with an assistant. Compare its row count with yours and recompute two numbers by hand.

**You are done when you can** show a short research note with at least one claim you caught as wrong, outdated or unsupported, and the original source that settled it.
