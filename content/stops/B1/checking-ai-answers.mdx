## What it is

Checking an AI answer means **confirming the parts you will rely on before you act**. It does not mean doubting everything equally. A birthday message needs a quick read. A medicine dose, a legal deadline or a customer's refund amount needs proof.

Here is a five-step routine you can run in your head:

```text
  1. Stakes        What happens if this is wrong?
        |
  2. Claims        Underline names, numbers, dates, rules, quotes
        |
  3. Verify        Check those claims against reliable sources
        |
  4. Reasoning     Does it make sense? Does it hold if I ask again?
        |
  5. Escalate      High stakes or sources disagree? Ask a person.
```

The point of the routine is speed. Most answers need only steps 1 and 2; you check hard only when it matters.

This lesson follows Suresh, who looks after his diabetic father in Coimbatore, through two very different questions to an assistant. By the end you will know how much to check, what to check and where.

## How it works

### Stakes first

Match your effort to what could go wrong:

| Stakes | Examples | How much to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Low** | Ideas, names, first drafts, brainstorming | Skim and edit; no checking needed |
| **Medium** | Work emails, reports, customer replies, assignments | Check every fact and figure you include |
| **High** | Health, money, law, safety, anything public or irreversible | Verify every claim against official sources or a qualified person |

### Find the checkable claims

Underline every **number, date, name, rule and quotation**. These are where errors hide, and they are also the easiest things to check. A sentence like "The scheme supports students with family income below ₹2.5 lakh, and the deadline is 30 November" contains two claims you can verify in a minute.

### Use reliable sources

Good sources, in rough order of trust:

1. **The original:** the law, the circular, the paper, the product label.
2. **Official sites:** government departments, regulators such as RBI or SEBI, the company's own pages.
3. **Reputable publishers:** established news organisations and reference works.

Use **lateral reading**: open new tabs and see what independent sources say about a claim or a website, instead of judging a page by how professional it looks.

### Check the AI's own sources

If the answer cites links, open them. Confirm three things: the page exists, the source is trustworthy, and the page actually says what the answer claims. Invented or mismatched citations are common.

### Test the reasoning

- Ask: "How did you get this number?"
- Ask: "What would make this answer wrong?"
- Ask the same question in different words. If small changes flip the answer, trust it less.

### Watch for warning signs

```text
  Red flags in an AI answer
  [!] Very precise numbers with no source   ("exactly 73.4%")
  [!] Quotes from real people you can't find anywhere else
  [!] Rules that sound too convenient       ("no documents needed")
  [!] It agrees with whatever you suggest   ("Yes, you're right")
  [!] Old information on fast-changing topics (prices, rules, dates)
```

### A two-minute check for common claims

Most everyday claims can be checked quickly if you know where to look:

| Claim type | Where to check in India | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Government scheme rules and deadlines | The scheme's official portal or the department's site (often ending in gov.in or nic.in) | 2 minutes |
| Bank, loan or UPI rules | The RBI site, NPCI for UPI, or your bank's own website | 2 minutes |
| Train or travel rules | IRCTC or Indian Railways official pages | 1 minute |
| A medicine | The label and leaflet, then a pharmacist or doctor | 5 minutes |
| A quote from a famous person | Search the exact words in quotes; look for a reputable original | 2 minutes |
| A viral news claim | Reputable news sites, PIB Fact Check for claims about the government | 3 minutes |
| A number in a document or paper | The original document: search for the figure | 1 minute |

If you cannot find the claim in a reliable place within a few minutes, treat it as unverified. Do not repeat it, and do not act on it when the stakes are medium or high.

### Checking answers in Indian languages

Assistants are usually most accurate in English, because they saw the most English text in training. If you ask in Hindi, Tamil or Bengali, the facts can be weaker and the wording less precise. For anything important, ask the same question in English too and compare, or check the facts in an official source in either language.

### Know when to ask a person

A doctor, pharmacist, lawyer, chartered accountant, your manager or the relevant government office. AI is excellent at helping you **prepare** the right questions for them.

## A worked example

**A high-stakes question.** Suresh's father has type 2 diabetes and has caught a cold. Suresh asks an assistant whether his father can take a common over-the-counter cold medicine with his diabetes tablets.

The answer says it is "generally safe", gives a dose, and adds that some cold syrups contain sugar.

Suresh runs the routine:

| Step | What he did |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Stakes | High: health, an older person, regular medicines |
| 2. Claims | "Generally safe", the dose, the sugar note |
| 3. Verify | Read the medicine's label and leaflet: one ingredient carries a caution for people with diabetes. The assistant had not mentioned it. |
| 4. Reasoning | "Generally safe" is vague; it did not ask about his father's other conditions |
| 5. Escalate | Called the family pharmacist, who recommended a different product and confirmed the dose |

The assistant helped him know what to ask. The pharmacist gave the answer.

**A low-stakes question.** The same week, Suresh asks the assistant for ten names for his new tea stall. Stakes: low. He picks "Saravanampatti Chai Corner" because he likes it, and checks only one thing: that no other shop or brand already uses the name.

Same tool, very different checking, because the stakes were different.

## Common mistakes

- **Checking everything equally.** You will give up. Scale effort to stakes.
- **Trusting the tone.** Confidence and detail are not evidence.
- **Not opening citations.** Links can be fake, broken or unrelated to the claim.
- **Asking the AI to check itself and stopping there.** Useful for catching slips, but not independent.
- **Skipping the expert when stakes are high.** AI helps you prepare questions; experts answer them.
- **Using old answers for fast-changing facts.** Prices, rules and deadlines change; check the date of your source.

## Practise

Do one lateral reading lesson from Civic Online Reasoning, linked below.

1. **Five questions, five stakes.** Ask an assistant five factual questions: a scheme's eligibility, a historical date, a train rule, a health fact and a price. Rate each by stakes and verify the claims that matter.
2. **Audit the citations.** Ask for sources and open every link. Record which were missing, wrong or did not support the claim.
3. **Write your rule.** Write your own one-line checking rule for low, medium and high stakes, and keep it near your desk.

**You are done when you can** show one AI answer you caught as wrong and the source that proved it.
