## What it is

Checking an AI answer means **confirming the parts you will rely on before you act**. It does not mean doubting everything equally. A birthday message needs a quick read. A medicine for a diabetic parent, a legal deadline or a customer's refund amount needs proof.

Here is a five-step routine you can run in your head:

```flow
1. Stakes | who relies on this, and what if it is wrong? -> 2. Claims | the names, numbers, dates, rules and quotes you will act on
2. Claims -> 3. Verify | a current, reliable source; recompute numbers
3. Verify -> 4. Reasoning | ask neutrally; does it hold if I ask again?
4. Reasoning -> 5. Escalate | high stakes, or sources disagree? Ask a person
```

The point of the routine is speed. Most answers need only steps 1 and 2. You check hard only when it matters.

This lesson is the general routine for any single answer. "AI for research and analysis" builds on it for longer research jobs, such as tracing a claim through many sources. Here we follow Suresh, who looks after his diabetic father in Coimbatore, through two very different questions to an assistant.

## How it works

### Why AI answers go wrong

You check better when you know where errors come from. "What AI cannot do" explains the limits in full. Four causes matter most for checking:

| Cause | What it looks like | The check that catches it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **It predicts likely text.** It writes what usually follows, not what it has looked up. | A smooth, confident answer with an invented detail, a **hallucination** | Find each key claim in a real source |
| **Guessing is rewarded.** In a September 2025 paper, OpenAI researchers argued that common training and scoring methods reward a guess over "I don't know". | It rarely says "I'm not sure", even when it should | Ask "How confident are you, and what would you need to know?", then check anyway |
| **Its knowledge stops at a date.** A model learns from data up to a **knowledge cutoff**. | An old rule, rate or deadline stated as current | Find the date on the source |
| **It leans towards agreeing with you.** This is called **sycophancy**. | "Yes, you're right" to a wrong idea | Ask neutrally, and ask for the case against |

On sycophancy: in a 2023 study, Anthropic researchers found that five leading AI assistants regularly told users what they seemed to want to hear. They linked this partly to training on human ratings: people tend to rate answers that agree with them more highly. So the way you ask shapes what you get.

### Step 1: Decide the stakes

Ask three quick questions. Who will rely on this answer? What happens if it is wrong? Can the action be undone? A wrong slogan costs nothing. A missed scholarship deadline cannot be fixed later.

| Stakes | Examples | How much to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Low** | Ideas, names, first drafts, brainstorming | Skim and edit; you are the judge |
| **Medium** | Work emails, reports, customer replies, assignments | Check every fact and figure you include |
| **High** | Health, money, law, safety, anything public or irreversible | Verify every claim against official sources or a qualified person |

A useful rule: if anyone other than you will act on the answer, it is at least medium.

### Step 2: Pick out the claims you will act on

Underline every **number, date, name, rule and quotation**. These are where errors hide, and they are also the easiest things to check. A sentence like "The scheme supports students with family income below ₹2.5 lakh, and the deadline is 30 November" contains two claims you can verify in a minute.

Then ask which claims are **load-bearing**: if this one were wrong, would you do something different? Check those first. A wrong date in the background paragraph matters less than a wrong deadline.

Finally, look for what is **missing**. An answer can be accurate in every sentence and still leave out the warning that matters to you. You cannot fact-check a sentence that is not there. So ask directly, for example "What warnings or exceptions apply to someone with diabetes?", and read the warnings section of the original yourself.

### Step 3: Verify against a current, reliable source

Good sources, in rough order of trust:

1. **The original:** the law, the circular, the paper, the product label.
2. **Official sites:** government departments, regulators such as RBI or SEBI, the company's own pages.
3. **Reputable publishers:** established news organisations and reference works.

Use **lateral reading**, the habit of professional fact-checkers: leave the page, open new tabs and see what independent sources say about the claim or the website. Do not judge a page by how professional it looks. Remember that five pages repeating one number are still one source.

**If the answer gives links, open them**, and ask four questions:

- Does the page exist?
- Is it a source you would trust on its own?
- Does it say exactly what the answer claims? A real, official page can be cited for a sentence it never contains.
- Is it current? Official sites keep old notices online. Last year's scholarship notice is real, official and wrong for you.

"AI for research and analysis" shows how to make the assistant quote the supporting sentence and how to trace a claim back to its original.

### A two-minute check for common claims

Most everyday claims can be checked quickly if you know where to look:

| Claim type | Where to check in India | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Government scheme rules and deadlines | The scheme's official portal or the department's site (often ending in gov.in or nic.in) | 2 minutes |
| Bank, loan or UPI rules | The RBI site, NPCI for UPI, or your bank's own website | 2 minutes |
| Train or travel rules | IRCTC or Indian Railways official pages | 1 minute |
| A medicine | The label and any leaflet, then a pharmacist or doctor | 5 minutes |
| A quote from a famous person | Search the exact words in quotes; look for a reputable original | 2 minutes |
| A viral news claim | Reputable news sites, and PIB Fact Check for claims about the government | 3 minutes |
| A number in a document or paper | The original document: search it for the figure | 1 minute |

If you cannot find the claim in a reliable place within a few minutes, treat it as unverified. Do not repeat it, and do not act on it when the stakes are medium or high.

### Check numbers and code by running them

Never take a total, a percentage or a loan instalment on trust. Put the inputs into a calculator or a spreadsheet and work it out yourself. If the assistant used a list you gave it, count the items: a dropped row changes every total.

Asking "How did you get this number?" is useful, but the explanation is not evidence. It is more generated text. In March 2025, Anthropic researchers looked inside one of their models while it added 36 and 59. It used its own mix of rough estimates and last-digit tricks. Yet when asked how it did the sum, it described the school method of carrying the 1. A neat, step-by-step explanation can sit on top of a wrong number, so recompute it or find it in a source.

The same goes for code. If an assistant writes a formula or a script, run it on a small example where you already know the answer. "I have tested this" from an assistant means nothing until you have seen it work.

### Step 4: Test the reasoning

- **Ask neutrally.** "Why is the new tax regime better for me?" invites agreement. "Compare the old and new tax regimes for someone earning ₹9 lakh a year, with the case for each" does not.
- **Ask for the case against.** "What is the strongest reason this answer could be wrong?"
- **Ask again differently.** Rephrase the question in a new chat. If small changes flip the answer, trust it less.
- **Do not stop at a self-check.** Asking it to "double-check" can catch slips, but it is the same system checking itself.
- **A second assistant is not independent either.** If two assistants disagree on a fact, you know you must check it. If they agree, that is not proof: both may have learned the same out-of-date page.

### Watch for warning signs

| Red flag in an AI answer | Example |
|---|---|
| Very precise numbers with no source | "exactly 73.4% of applicants" |
| Quotes from real people you cannot find anywhere else | A line credited to a minister that no news site carries |
| Rules that sound too convenient | "No documents are needed" |
| It agrees with whatever you suggest | "Yes, you're right" after you push back |
| A firm answer on a fast-changing topic, with no date | This year's limit, rate or price |

### Images, charts and screenshots

Answers increasingly come with pictures, and pictures feel like proof. They are not.

- **A chart is only as good as its numbers.** If an assistant draws a chart, check two or three of the values behind it against the data.
- **A screenshot can be edited or invented.** Before you trust a screenshot of a notice or a message, find the original on the official site. A reverse image search can show where an image first appeared.
- **Assistants misread photos.** When you share a photo of a bill or a form, check the figures it read against the photo itself.

"Deepfakes and misinformation" covers fake images and notices in more depth.

### Checking answers in Indian languages

Answer quality can vary from one language to another, and names and numbers are easy to garble in translation. Before you rely on an assistant in Hindi, Tamil or Bengali, test it on a question you already know the answer to. For anything important, check the facts in an official source, which is often available in both English and your language.

### Step 5: Know when to ask a person

Go to a person when the stakes are high or when reliable sources disagree: a doctor, pharmacist, lawyer, chartered accountant, your manager or the relevant government office. AI is excellent at helping you **prepare** for them. Ask it for the questions to take along, then bring the answer you got, the sources you checked and what is still unclear.

## A worked example

**A high-stakes question.** Suresh's father, aged 68, has type 2 diabetes and has caught a cold. Suresh has a box of an over-the-counter cold tablet at home. It contains paracetamol and a decongestant, a medicine that eases a blocked nose.

His first question is a leading one: "This cold tablet is fine to take with diabetes tablets, right?" The assistant agrees that it is "generally safe" and gives a dose. Then he remembers the warning about leading questions. He opens a new chat and asks neutrally: "My father is 68, has type 2 diabetes and takes tablets for it. What should I check before he takes a cold tablet that contains paracetamol and a decongestant?" This answer is more careful. It mentions that cold syrups can contain sugar and suggests asking a pharmacist, but it still says nothing specific about the decongestant.

Suresh runs the routine:

| Step | What he did |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Stakes | High: health, an older person, regular medicines, and he cannot undo a bad reaction |
| 2. Claims | "Generally safe", the dose, the sugar note. Missing: any warning about the decongestant |
| 3. Verify | He read the box and the printed information that came with it. For the decongestant, it said to ask a doctor before use if you have diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease or thyroid problems. Neither answer had said this. |
| 4. Reasoning | The answer shifted with his wording: "generally safe" when he led, "ask a pharmacist" when he asked neutrally. It never asked about his father's other conditions. |
| 5. Escalate | He took the box to the family pharmacist, who asked about his father's conditions and other medicines, suggested a product without a decongestant for him and confirmed the dose |

Notice two lessons. The neutral question got a better answer than the leading one, but still not a complete one. And the key fact was an omission: it was not a wrong sentence he could check, but a missing one. Only the original, the label, showed it. The assistant helped him know what to ask. The pharmacist gave the answer, for his father in particular: this is an example of checking, not medical advice, and anyone else should read their own label and ask a doctor or pharmacist.

**A low-stakes question.** The same week, Suresh asks the assistant for ten names for his new tea stall. Stakes: low, because he is the judge and he can change his mind. He picks "Saravanampatti Chai Corner" because he likes it, and checks only one thing: that no other shop nearby already uses the name.

Same tool, very different checking, because the stakes were different.

## Common mistakes

- **Checking everything equally.** You will give up. Scale effort to stakes.
- **Trusting the tone.** Confidence and detail are not evidence.
- **Asking a leading question.** "Why is X true?" gets a case for X. Ask neutrally and ask for the case against.
- **Checking only what is there.** The missing warning can matter most. Ask about exceptions and read the original.
- **Treating an official link as current.** Old notices stay online. Check the year, round or version.
- **Accepting the AI's working as proof.** Recompute numbers yourself and run code on an example you know.
- **Counting a self-check or a second AI as independent.** Disagreement tells you to check; agreement proves nothing.
- **Skipping the expert when stakes are high.** AI helps you prepare questions; experts answer them.

## Practice

Do one lateral reading lesson from Civic Online Reasoning, linked below.

1. **Five questions, five stakes.** Ask an assistant five factual questions: a scheme's eligibility, a historical date, a train rule, a health fact and a price. Rate each by stakes, then verify the load-bearing claims and note the date of each source.
2. **Lead, then ask neutrally.** Ask one question twice in separate chats: once as "Why is X true?" and once as "Is X true, and what is the case against it?" Write down how the answers differ.
3. **Recompute one number.** Ask for a calculation from your own life, such as splitting a bill or a monthly loan instalment. Ask for the working, then redo it in a calculator or spreadsheet.

**You are done when you can** show one AI answer you caught as wrong, outdated or missing something important, and the source that proved it.
