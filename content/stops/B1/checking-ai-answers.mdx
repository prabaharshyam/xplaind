## What it is

Checking an AI answer means confirming the parts you will rely on before you act. It does not mean doubting everything equally. A birthday message needs a quick read; a medicine dose, a legal deadline or a customer's refund amount needs proof.

A simple routine:

1. **Decide the stakes.** What happens if this is wrong?
2. **Find the claims that matter.** Names, numbers, dates, rules, quotes, sources.
3. **Verify** those claims against reliable sources.
4. **Test the reasoning.** Does it make sense? Does it fit what you know?
5. **Escalate** to a person when the stakes are high or sources disagree.

## How it works

**Stakes first.** Low (ideas, drafts, brainstorming): skim and edit. Medium (work emails, reports, customer replies): check facts and figures. High (health, money, law, safety, anything public or irreversible): verify every claim against official sources or a qualified person.

**Spot checkable claims.** Underline every number, date, name, rule and quotation. These are where errors hide and where checking is easiest.

**Use reliable sources.** Official websites (government departments, regulators, the company's own pages), reputable news organisations, the original document. Use lateral reading: open new tabs and see what independent sources say, rather than judging a page by how it looks.

**Check the AI's own sources.** If it cites links, open them. Confirm the page exists, is trustworthy and says what the answer claims. Invented or mismatched citations are common.

**Test the reasoning.** Ask: "How did you get this number?" "What would make this wrong?" Ask the same question differently and see if the answer changes. If small changes flip the answer, trust it less.

**Watch for warning signs.** Very precise numbers with no source; quotes from real people you cannot find elsewhere; rules that sound too convenient; answers that agree with whatever you suggest.

**Know when to ask a person.** A doctor, pharmacist, lawyer, chartered accountant, your manager or the relevant government office.

## A worked example

Suresh's father is diabetic. Suresh asks an assistant whether his father can take a common over-the-counter cold medicine alongside his diabetes tablets.

Stakes: high, health. The answer says it is "generally safe" and gives a dose.

Suresh underlines the claims: "generally safe", the dose, and a note that some cold medicines contain sugar. He checks the medicine's label and the official leaflet, which mention a caution for people with diabetes for one ingredient. The assistant had not mentioned that. He calls the family pharmacist, who recommends a different product and confirms the dose.

The AI helped him know what to ask. The pharmacist gave the answer.

Another day, Suresh asks the assistant to suggest ten names for his new tea stall. Stakes: low. He picks one he likes without checking anything, except that no other shop nearby uses it.

## Common mistakes

- **Checking everything equally.** You will give up. Scale effort to stakes.
- **Trusting the tone.** Confidence and detail are not evidence.
- **Not opening citations.** Links can be fake or unrelated.
- **Asking the AI to check itself and stopping there.** Useful, but not independent.
- **Skipping the expert when stakes are high.** AI helps you prepare questions; experts answer them.

## Practise

Do one lateral reading lesson from Civic Online Reasoning.

1. Ask an assistant five questions with factual answers (a scheme's eligibility, a historical date, a train rule, a health fact, a price). Rate each by stakes and verify the claims that matter.
2. Ask for sources and open every link; record which were missing, wrong or mismatched.
3. Write your own one-line checking rule for low, medium and high stakes, and stick it near your desk.

**You are done when you can** show one AI answer you caught as wrong and the source that proved it.
