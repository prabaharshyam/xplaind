## What it is

The fastest way to learn AI is to use it for something real. A chat assistant is most useful when you treat it like a **capable new colleague**: it knows a lot in general but nothing about your situation, your customers or your company. You brief it clearly, review what it gives you, and check anything that matters before you use it.

```text
       Brief                 Review                 Check                Use
 +---------------+     +----------------+     +----------------+     +----------+
 | who, task,    | --> | Is it what I   | --> | Are the facts  | --> | Send it, |
 | context,      |     | asked for?     |     | that matter    |     | with my  |
 | format        |     | Give feedback  |     | right?         |     | name on  |
 +---------------+     +----------------+     +----------------+     +----------+
        ^                      |
        +----- iterate --------+
```

This lesson is about doing that once, properly, on a task from your own work or studies: drafting, summarising, explaining, planning or brainstorming. Its check is a small project, so by the end you will have three prompts, three answers and a list of claims you checked.

You will follow Rohit, who works in customer support at a broadband company in Chennai, as he writes a help article with an assistant.

## How it works

### Pick a good task

| Good first tasks | Poor first tasks |
| --- | --- |
| An email or message you need to send | A legal or tax deadline |
| A summary of a long article or meeting | A medicine dose |
| An explanation of a topic you half-know | Anything you cannot judge yourself |
| A plan for an event or a week | A decision that cannot be undone |
| Interview questions to prepare for | Anything involving confidential data in an unapproved tool |

Good tasks are ones where a draft saves time and you can easily tell good from bad. You learn faster when you can judge the output.

### Write a full prompt

A full prompt has four parts:

| Part | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Who you are, who it is for** | "I'm a final-year B.Com student preparing for a campus interview at a bank." |
| **The task** | "List ten questions they are likely to ask." |
| **Useful context** | "It's a public sector bank, for a probationary officer role. I did an internship at a credit co-operative." |
| **The format** | "As a numbered list, with a one-line tip for each." |

### Iterate

The first answer is a draft. Ask for changes: shorter, more specific, a different tone, more examples, fewer clichés. Point out what is wrong. Two or three rounds usually beat trying to write one perfect prompt.

### Try different prompts for the same task

This is the heart of the lesson. Write three versions:

1. **A one-line prompt**, the way most people start.
2. **A detailed prompt** with all four parts.
3. **A detailed prompt that asks the assistant to ask you questions first:** "Before you write, ask me three questions."

The results are often very different. Comparing them teaches you more about prompting than any list of tips.

### Check the claims

Find the **factual claims** in the answer: numbers, dates, names, rules, product details. Check at least the important ones against a reliable source: an official website, the original document, a textbook or an expert.

```text
  Answer:  "Routers should be restarted every week."   <- a claim
              |
              v
  Source:  Ask the network team / read the router manual
              |
              v
  Result:  "Only restart when there's a problem."      -> fix the line
```

If you cannot check something important, do not rely on it.

### Mind what you share

Do not paste personal data, passwords or confidential work documents into tools your organisation has not approved. The lesson "Use AI safely at work" covers this in detail.

### Why answers change

If you ask the same question twice, you may get different answers. Assistants pick each next word with some randomness, and small changes in wording change the result. This is one more reason to check facts rather than trusting any single answer.

## A worked example

Rohit works in customer support at a broadband company in Chennai. His manager asks him to write a short help article: "Why is my Wi-Fi slow?"

**Prompt 1, one line:**

> "Write an article about slow Wi-Fi."

The result is 600 words, generic, and mentions products his company does not sell.

**Prompt 2, detailed:**

> "I work for a broadband provider in Chennai. Write a 250-word help article for home customers titled 'Why is my Wi-Fi slow?' Cover router placement, too many devices, and when to restart the router. Friendly, simple English, with numbered steps. Do not mention brands."

The result is close to usable.

**Prompt 3, detailed plus questions first:**

Same as prompt 2, plus: "Before writing, ask me three questions about our customers and router models." The assistant asks which router models customers have, what plan speeds are common, and whether customers can log in to the router's settings. Rohit answers. The article now mentions the dual-band router the company supplies and how to switch to the faster band.

**Checking claims.** The article says "routers should be restarted every week". Rohit asks the network team, who say restarting is only needed when problems occur, and that weekly restarts cause needless support calls. He fixes the line.

| Prompt | Length | Accuracy | Fit for our customers | Used? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. One line | 600 words | Mentions other brands | Poor | No |
| 2. Detailed | 250 words | One wrong claim | Good | Almost |
| 3. Detailed plus questions | 250 words | One wrong claim, then fixed | Very good | Yes, after review |

The final article goes to his manager for review and is published the next week.

## Common mistakes

- **One-line prompts.** Give who, task, context and format.
- **Accepting the first answer.** Iterate with specific feedback.
- **Checking nothing.** Find the claims that matter and verify them.
- **Pasting confidential or personal data into unapproved tools.**
- **Choosing a task you cannot judge.** Start where you can tell good from bad.
- **Trusting one answer.** If it matters, ask again differently or check a source.

This lesson's check is a small project:

1. **Pick a task** from your own work or studies.
2. **Write three prompts** for it: a one-line prompt, a detailed prompt, and a detailed prompt that asks you questions first.
3. **Check one claim** in each answer against a source you trust.
4. **Write it up:** which prompt worked best and why, what was right, what was wrong, and how you could tell.

**You are done when you can** show three prompts, their answers, the claims you checked and what you would change next time.
