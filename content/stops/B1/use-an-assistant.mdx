## What it is

The fastest way to learn AI is to use it for something real, every day. A chat assistant is most useful when you treat it like a **capable new colleague**: it knows a lot in general but nothing about your situation, your customers or your company. You brief it, review its work and check anything that matters.

Other lessons cover the parts of that in depth: "Prompting without jargon" covers writing the brief, "What AI cannot do" covers the limits, and "Checking an AI answer" covers verification. This lesson is about the **daily practice** that ties them together:

- choosing an assistant and learning its controls,
- picking the right feature for each task (a new chat, web search, an attached file, voice),
- keeping chats tidy so the assistant does not drift,
- knowing what your privacy settings do and do not protect.

```flow
title: A daily loop with an assistant
dir: LR
Choose | new chat, search on or off, attach a file? -> Brief | who, task, context, format
Brief -> Review | is it what I asked for?
Review -[specific feedback]-> Brief
Review -> Check | the claims someone will rely on
Check -> Save | keep the prompt that worked
```

The check for this lesson is a small project. You will run one task from your own work three ways, check one claim in each answer and write up what you learned. You will follow Rohit, who works in customer support at a broadband company in Chennai, as he does the same.

## How it works

### Pick one assistant and learn it well

You do not need to pay to do this lesson. The widely used general assistants all have free plans, as of November 2026:

| Assistant | Made by | Good to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ChatGPT | OpenAI | Free plan includes web search, with lower limits on file uploads and voice than paid plans |
| Gemini | Google | Signs in with your Google account; the app works in Hindi and several other Indian languages |
| Claude | Anthropic | Free plan includes web search, file uploads and voice mode |

Limits on free plans change often, so check the plan page inside the app rather than an old article. Pick one and use it for two weeks before you compare. The habits in this lesson transfer between assistants; the brand matters much less than how you use it.

Two cautions before you sign up:

- **Free trials renew.** In November 2025, for example, OpenAI offered users in India a free year of its paid Go plan. Offers like this, including ones bundled with phone plans, can renew at the normal price unless you cancel. Note the renewal date the day you sign up.
- **Work and personal accounts are different.** If your employer or college gives you an approved assistant, use that one for work. Its terms on storage and training are set by a contract, not by your own settings. "Use AI safely at work" explains why.

### Learn the controls that matter

Most of the skill in daily use is knowing which control to reach for. Names differ between assistants, but nearly all of them have these:

| Control | What it does | Use it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **New chat** | Starts with a clean slate | You start a different task, or the current chat has drifted |
| **Web search** | Looks up current pages and shows links | The answer depends on anything that changes: rates, prices, rules, dates, news |
| **Attach a file or photo** | Lets it read your document, spreadsheet or screenshot | The answer must follow a specific source, such as your company's guide |
| **Voice input** | Turns your speech into a prompt | Typing is slow, or you think better speaking, including in your own language |
| **Thinking or reasoning mode** | Spends longer working through a problem before answering | Multi-step problems, plans and calculations; not needed for a short email |
| **Saved instructions or projects** | Keep background it should always know | You keep explaining the same thing, such as your role or preferred style |
| **Temporary or incognito chat** | Not saved to your history, not used for training | A one-off question you do not want kept in your history |

A useful default: start every new task in a new chat, leave search on for anything factual, and attach the source whenever one exists. An answer built on your own source is easier to check than one built from the assistant's memory.

### Brief it, then iterate

A good **prompt** gives who you are and who it is for, the task, the context the assistant cannot know, and the format you want back. Then treat the first answer as a draft and give specific feedback, such as "cut it to 120 words and drop the second paragraph". Two or three rounds usually beat trying to write one perfect prompt. "Prompting without jargon" teaches this properly; here you only need to do it.

One move is worth using often in daily work: end your brief with "Before you start, ask me any questions you need answered." The assistant then asks for the facts it would otherwise guess.

### Keep chats tidy

Every assistant has a **context window**: the amount of text it can take in at once, including the whole conversation so far. In a very long chat, early instructions can get crowded out, so the assistant starts ignoring them. Three habits prevent this:

- **One task, one chat.** Your CV and your current-affairs revision do not belong in the same conversation.
- **Restart with a summary.** When a chat drifts, ask it to "summarise what we agreed in five bullets", then paste that into a new chat.
- **Save what you repeat.** Put your standing background, such as "I'm a final-year B.Com student; keep answers short", in saved instructions or a project, so you do not retype it.

Keep a simple note on your phone of prompts that worked. After a month, that note is your personal toolkit.

### Use files, photos and voice with care

**Files.** Attaching a document helps the answer follow your source. It does not guarantee it: the assistant can still add a plausible sentence that is not in the document. Ask it to "say which section each step comes from", then spot-check those sections yourself. Do not rely on the assistant to flag its own unsupported lines.

**Photos and screenshots.** Crop out names, phone numbers, account numbers and anything else it does not need. Assistants can misread figures in a photo, so check numbers against the original. "Images, voice and other AI tools" covers reading documents from photos in more depth.

**Voice and Indian languages.** Voice input is a quick way in, and many assistants accept Hindi and other Indian languages. For example, Google launched the Gemini app in India in June 2024 with nine Indian languages. Test it in your language on a task you can judge before you rely on it, and always read the transcript: names and numbers are where speech recognition slips.

### Know what your privacy settings do

Each assistant has a switch that controls whether your chats can be used to improve its models. As of November 2026, ChatGPT calls it "Improve the model for everyone", Claude has a model-improvement setting in its privacy settings, and Gemini is moving it from its Keep Activity setting to a separate training setting (Google announced the change in November 2026, so your account may show either). Find it and decide on purpose; do not leave it to the default.

Be clear about what these settings do:

| Setting | What it changes | What it does not change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Model-improvement switch off | Your new chats are not used to train future models | Chats can still be stored for a period, for example for safety checks |
| Temporary or incognito chat | Not saved to your history, not used for training | It is still kept for a short time, and it is still a personal account |

Neither one makes it acceptable to paste customer records, colleagues' personal details or confidential documents into a tool your organisation has not approved.

### Decide what to check

Assistants can produce a **hallucination**: a fluent, confident statement that is false. You cannot check everything, so check by stakes. Ask two questions about each claim: "Will anyone rely on this?" and "What happens if it is wrong?" Numbers, dates, names, rules, prices and steps specific to your organisation usually pass that test; general wording and ideas usually do not. "Checking an AI answer" gives a fuller method.

A second assistant is useful for ideas and for spotting disagreement. If two assistants disagree on a fact, you know you must check it. If they agree, that is not proof: both may have learned the same out-of-date page.

## A worked example

Rohit works in customer support at a broadband company in Chennai. His manager asks for a short help article: "Why is my Wi-Fi slow?" His company has not approved any AI tool for customer data, so he uses his personal free account and only public material: the router setup guide on the company's website. He leaves the model-improvement switch off and keeps customer tickets out of it.

He runs the task three ways, each in a new chat.

**Set-up 1: one line, no features.**

> "Write an article about slow Wi-Fi."

The result is about 600 words, generic, and recommends mesh Wi-Fi kits the company does not sell.

**Set-up 2: a full brief.**

> "I work for a broadband provider in Chennai. Write a 250-word help article for home customers titled 'Why is my Wi-Fi slow?' Cover router placement, too many devices, and when to restart the router. Friendly, simple English, numbered steps, no brand names."

The result is the right length and tone. One line says: "Restart your router once a week to keep it fast."

**Set-up 3: the same brief, plus the company's guide attached.** He adds:

> "Use only the attached setup guide for steps and settings, and say which section each step comes from."

This version uses the guide's own steps for switching between the router's two bands, and the weekly-restart line has gone. But it adds: "The 5 GHz band covers the whole house." Rohit checks the cited section. The guide says the opposite: 5 GHz is faster close to the router, and 2.4 GHz reaches further, so far rooms may do better on 2.4 GHz. The assistant had cited a real section for a claim that section does not make.

**Checking.** He asks the network team about the weekly restart. They say a restart is only needed when there is a problem, and that weekly restarts lead to needless support calls.

His write-up, which is the shape your project write-up should take:

| Set-up | Claim checked | Source | Verdict | Used? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. One line | Recommends mesh kits | Company product page | Wrong for us: we do not sell them | No |
| 2. Full brief | "Restart your router once a week" | Network team | Wrong | Almost |
| 3. Brief plus guide | "The 5 GHz band covers the whole house" | Setup guide, cited section | Wrong: the guide says 2.4 GHz reaches further | Yes, fixed after checking |

**What he concluded:** "For help articles I will always attach our own guide, because it removed the restart error and fitted our router. But I will still check every step against the section it cites, because attaching the guide did not stop it adding a wrong line." The article went to his manager for review and was published the next week.

Notice what made the difference. The better prompt fixed the fit; the attached source fixed most of the facts; only checking caught the rest.

## Common mistakes

- **Running everything in one long chat.** Start a new chat per task, and restart with a summary when it drifts.
- **Asking about current facts with search off.** Rates, rules and prices need search, and then you still open the link.
- **Treating a citation as proof.** A cited page or section may not say what the assistant claims. Read it.
- **Assuming an attached file makes the answer correct.** It helps; it does not stop added claims.
- **Trusting a voice transcript.** Read it and fix names and numbers before you build on it.
- **Thinking a temporary chat makes confidential data safe.** It changes history and training, not whether you may share the data.
- **Never changing the defaults.** Find the model-improvement switch and decide on purpose.
- **Forgetting a trial's renewal date.** Free offers often turn into paid plans.

## Practice

Take the AI Fluency course linked below. Its ideas of delegation, description, discernment and diligence match this lesson's daily loop.

Then do this lesson's project:

1. **Set up.** Choose one assistant. Find its new chat, web search, file upload, voice and model-improvement controls, and decide your privacy setting.
2. **Pick a task** from your own work or studies that has at least one checkable fact, uses no personal or confidential data, and that you can judge yourself.
3. **Run it three ways**, each in a new chat: a one-line prompt; a full brief; the full brief plus the feature the task needs (web search for current facts, your own document attached, or "ask me questions first").
4. **Check one claim in each answer** against a source you trust: an official website, the original document or a person who knows.
5. **Write it up** as a table like Rohit's, with a two- or three-sentence conclusion.

**You are done when** your write-up shows:

- all three prompts word for word, with their answers;
- one checked claim per answer, with the source named and a verdict: right, wrong, or could not confirm;
- a conclusion that says which set-up you will use next time for this kind of task, based on what your checks found, not on which answer sounded best.
