## What it is

A chat assistant is most useful when you treat it like a capable new colleague who knows a lot in general but nothing about your situation. You brief it clearly, review what it gives you, and check anything that matters before you use it.

This lesson is about doing that for a real task from your own work or studies: drafting, summarising, explaining, planning or brainstorming.

## How it works

**Pick a good task.** Strong first tasks are ones where a draft saves time and you can easily judge quality: an email, a meeting summary, an explanation of a topic you half-know, a plan for an event, interview questions to prepare. Weak first tasks are ones where you cannot check the answer, or where an error would be costly.

**Write a full prompt.** Include:

- *who you are and who it is for*: "I'm a final-year B.Com student preparing for a campus interview at a bank";
- *the task*: "list ten questions they are likely to ask";
- *useful context*: the role, the bank type, your background;
- *the format*: "as a numbered list, with a one-line tip for each".

**Iterate.** The first answer is a draft. Ask for changes: shorter, more specific, different tone, more examples, fewer clichés. Point out what is wrong. Two or three rounds usually beat one perfect prompt.

**Try different prompts for the same task.** A one-line prompt, a detailed prompt, and a prompt that asks the assistant to ask you questions first often produce very different results. Comparing them teaches you more about prompting than any list of tips.

**Check the claims.** Find the factual claims in the answer (numbers, dates, names, rules) and check at least the important ones against a reliable source: an official website, the original document, a textbook, an expert. If you cannot check something important, do not rely on it.

**Mind what you share.** Do not paste personal data, passwords or confidential work documents into tools your organisation has not approved.

## A worked example

Rohit works in customer support at a Chennai broadband company. His task: write a short help article on "Why is my Wi-Fi slow?" for customers.

- *Prompt 1*: "Write an article about slow Wi-Fi." Result: generic, 600 words, mentions products the company does not sell.
- *Prompt 2*: "I work for a broadband provider in Chennai. Write a 250-word help article for home customers titled 'Why is my Wi-Fi slow?' Cover router placement, too many devices, and when to restart the router. Friendly, simple English, with numbered steps. Do not mention brands." Result: close to usable.
- *Prompt 3*: Same, plus "Before writing, ask me three questions about our customers and router models." The assistant asks about router models and plan speeds; Rohit answers; the article now mentions the dual-band router the company supplies.

Checking claims: the article says "routers should be restarted every week". Rohit asks the network team, who say restarting is only needed when problems occur. He fixes the line. The final article goes to his manager for review.

## Common mistakes

- **One-line prompts.** Give context, task and format.
- **Accepting the first answer.** Iterate with specific feedback.
- **Checking nothing.** Find the claims that matter and verify them.
- **Pasting confidential or personal data into unapproved tools.**
- **Choosing a task you cannot judge.** Start where you can tell good from bad.
