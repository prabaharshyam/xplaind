## What it is

Three terms come up constantly, and they nest inside each other:

- **Artificial intelligence (AI)** is the broad goal: software that does tasks we used to think needed human judgement, such as recognising faces, translating languages or answering questions.
- **Machine learning (ML)** is the main way AI is built today: instead of writing rules by hand, you show a program many examples and it learns the pattern. A spam filter learns from millions of emails marked spam or not spam.
- A **large language model (LLM)** is one kind of machine learning model, trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next word. Chat assistants are built on LLMs.

So every LLM is machine learning, and all machine learning is AI, but not the other way round. A fraud alert on your UPI app is machine learning but not a language model.

## How it works

**Rules versus learning.** A rule-based program follows instructions a person wrote: "if the amount is above ₹50,000 and the device is new, ask for an OTP". Machine learning finds the rules itself from examples: thousands of past transactions labelled fraud or genuine. It can spot patterns too subtle to write down, but it is only as good as its examples.

**How a language model writes.** During training, an LLM reads an enormous amount of text and learns, word piece by word piece, what tends to come next. Ask it a question and it writes an answer one small piece (a token) at a time, each time picking a likely next piece given everything before. With enough data and size, this simple skill produces writing, summaries, translations and code.

**What it does not do.** It does not look facts up in a database unless a product connects it to one. It does not know things that happened after its training data was collected. It cannot check its own claims. That is why it can produce fluent, confident text that is wrong, called a hallucination.

**Why it feels intelligent.** Human writing contains a great deal of knowledge and reasoning. A model that has learned to continue such text well can explain, compare and plan in ways that look like understanding, and are often useful, while still being a prediction machine.

## A worked example

Asha runs HR for a 200-person company in Pune. In one morning she meets three kinds of AI:

- Her email's **spam filter** (machine learning, not a language model) moves a fake job offer to spam. It learned from millions of labelled emails.
- Her phone's **face unlock** (machine learning on images) recognises her even with glasses on.
- She asks a **chat assistant** (a large language model) to draft a policy announcement about the new work-from-home rules. The draft is good. It also states that "the Shops and Establishments Act requires 30 days' notice for policy changes", which sounds plausible. Asha checks with the company's lawyer: the claim is wrong for her state. She removes it.

The assistant saved her 40 minutes of drafting. Checking its facts took five. That split is typical.

## Common mistakes

- **Using AI, ML and LLM as the same thing.** They nest; knowing which is which helps you judge a product's claims.
- **Thinking a language model looks things up.** Unless the product says it searches or uses your documents, it is generating from patterns.
- **Trusting confidence.** Fluent tone tells you nothing about accuracy, especially for statistics, laws and recent events.
- **Assuming AI understands like a person.** It can be brilliant on one question and absurd on a similar one.
