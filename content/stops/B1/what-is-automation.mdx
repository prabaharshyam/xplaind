## What it is

Think of the most boring part of your working week: copying details from emails into a spreadsheet, sending the same reply twenty times, forwarding messages to the right person. **Workflow automation** is software doing that repeated sequence of steps for you, across the apps you already use.

Every automation has three parts:

```text
   TRIGGER              STEPS (nodes)                          RESULT
 +------------+    +------------+    +------------+    +------------+
 | New email  | -> | Read it,   | -> | Add a row  | -> | Message    |
 | arrives    |    | pull out   |    | to the     |    | the team   |
 |            |    | the fields |    | sheet      |    |            |
 +------------+    +------------+    +------------+    +------------+
        \_____________ data passes along each arrow ____________/
```

- A **trigger**: what starts it. A new email, a form submitted, a new row in a sheet, a time of day, or a message from another app through a **webhook**, a web address one app calls to send data to another the moment something happens.
- **Steps**, often called **nodes**, actions or modules: what happens next. Read the email, pull out the invoice number, look up the customer, add a row, send a message.
- **Data passed along**: each step's output becomes the next step's input.

Tools such as **n8n**, **Zapier**, **Make** and **Microsoft Power Automate** let you build these visually, by connecting boxes, with little or no code.

**AI steps** add something new: judgement-like abilities with messy text. An AI step can read an unstructured email and extract fields, classify a request, summarise a document or draft a reply. That extends automation from tidy, rule-based work into the messy, text-heavy work most offices are full of.

This lesson follows a travel agency in Kochi as it automates its booking enquiries. By the end you will be able to describe any automation as trigger, steps and checks, and pick good first candidates.

## How it works

### Rule-based automation

The classic kind: every input is predictable and every step is a fixed rule.

> "When a form is submitted, add a row to the sheet and email the team."

Rule-based automations are reliable, cheap to run and easy to test. Their weakness is that they break when input is messy: an email written differently, a missing field, a typo.

### AI-assisted automation

Here an AI step handles the variety, while rules and people handle the risk:

```text
  Email to accounts@
        |
        v
  [AI step] What is this?  ---- spam ----------------> archive
        |                 \---- payment query -------> forward to accounts team
        | invoice
        v
  [AI step] Extract vendor, amount, GSTIN, due date
        |
        v
  [Rule] All fields found and amount under Rs 1 lakh?
        |-- yes --> add row to sheet
        |-- no  --> ask a person to check
```

| | Rule-based steps | AI steps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Good at | Fixed, predictable input | Messy, varied text |
| Example | "If amount is above ₹50,000, ask for approval" | "Read this email and find the invoice amount" |
| Cost per run | Almost nothing | A small model charge |
| Can be wrong? | Only if the rule is wrong | Yes, sometimes; add checks |
| Best use | Routing, calculations, moving data | Reading, classifying, summarising, drafting |

Good automations combine both: AI reads, rules decide, people check the risky parts.

### What is worth automating

Look for work that is:

1. **Frequent**: daily or weekly, not twice a year.
2. **Repetitive**: the same steps each time.
3. **Rule-based or easy to check**: a person can quickly confirm the result.
4. **Digital**: the data already lives in apps, emails or files.
5. **Painful**: slow, error-prone or boring.

Rare, high-judgement or highly sensitive tasks are usually poor first candidates.

### What automation needs

- **Access to the apps**, usually through ready-made connectors that use each app's API.
- **Permissions** to read and write where needed, and no more.
- **A clear definition** of the steps, which is why mapping the process comes first.
- **An owner** who notices when it breaks and fixes it.

## A worked example

A small travel agency in Kochi receives about 40 booking enquiries a day by email and its website form. Today, staff copy each one into a spreadsheet, reply with a standard message, and forward it to the right agent: domestic, international or pilgrimage tours. On busy days, enquiries sit unanswered for hours and some get lost in the inbox.

They build this automation in n8n:

| # | Step | Type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A new enquiry arrives by email or website form | Trigger |
| 2 | Read the enquiry; extract name, phone, destination, dates, number of travellers and budget; classify as domestic, international or pilgrimage | AI step |
| 3 | Add a row to the bookings sheet | Rule |
| 4 | Send the customer a polite acknowledgement with the expected response time | Rule, with a fixed template |
| 5 | Notify the right agent on the team chat with the details | Rule, based on the class from step 2 |
| 6 | If the AI could not find a phone number or travel dates, send the enquiry to the front desk to handle by hand | Check |

**Results after a month:**

| Measure | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Staff time on enquiries | About 2.5 hours a day | About 30 minutes a day, mostly the flagged ones |
| Time to first reply | 2 to 6 hours | Under 5 minutes |
| Lost enquiries | A few each week | None found |
| Wrongly classified enquiries | Not tracked | About 1 in 30, caught by agents |

The owner of the automation is the front-desk lead. When a new website form added a "special requests" field, the extraction step started missing dates for a day; she noticed the rise in flagged enquiries and fixed the step.

## Common mistakes

- **Automating a broken process.** Fix and simplify the process first, then automate it.
- **Starting with the hardest, rarest task.** Begin with frequent, simple, checkable work.
- **No owner.** Every automation breaks eventually; someone must notice and fix it.
- **Letting AI steps act unchecked on risky actions.** Add a person for payments, deletions and sensitive messages.
- **Forgetting data rules.** Customer data in automations is still covered by privacy law.
- **Giving the automation more access than it needs.** Limit permissions to what each step uses.

## Practise

Browse Zapier's guides on automation basics, linked below, and read n8n's quickstart page.

1. **List.** Write down five repetitive tasks you or your team do every week. For each, note the trigger, the steps and where the data lives.
2. **Score.** Score each from 1 to 5 for frequency, repetitiveness, ease of checking and pain. Pick the highest-scoring one.
3. **Design.** For that task, mark which steps are simple rules, which would need an AI step, and where a person should check.

**You are done when you can** describe one automation from your own work as trigger, steps and checks, and say why it is a good first candidate.
