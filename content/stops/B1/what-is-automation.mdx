## What it is

**Workflow automation** is software doing a repeated sequence of steps for you, across the apps you already use. Every automation has three parts:

- A **trigger**: what starts it. A new email, a form submitted, a new row in a sheet, a time of day, a message from another app.
- **Steps** (often called nodes, actions or modules): what happens next. Read the email, pull out the invoice number, look up the customer, add a row, send a message.
- **Data passed along**: each step's output becomes the next step's input.

Tools such as n8n, Zapier, Make and Microsoft Power Automate let you build these visually, with little or no code.

**AI steps** add judgement-like abilities: read an unstructured email and extract fields, classify a request, summarise a document, draft a reply. That extends automation from tidy, rule-based work into messy, text-heavy work.

## How it works

**Rule-based automation.** "When a form is submitted, add a row to the sheet and email the team." Every input is predictable and every step is a fixed rule. Reliable and cheap.

**AI-assisted automation.** "When an email arrives at accounts@, have AI decide if it is an invoice, a payment query or spam; if an invoice, extract vendor, amount, GSTIN and due date; add it to the sheet; if anything is missing or unusual, ask a person." The AI step handles variety; rules and people handle risk.

**What is worth automating.** Look for work that is:

- **frequent** (daily or weekly),
- **repetitive** (the same steps each time),
- **rule-based or easy to check** (a person can quickly confirm the result),
- **digital** (data already lives in apps, emails or files),
- **painful** (slow, error-prone or boring).

Rare, high-judgement or highly sensitive tasks are usually poor first candidates.

**What automation needs.** Access to the apps (usually through connectors that use their APIs), permissions, a clear definition of the steps, and someone who owns the automation when it breaks.

## A worked example

A small travel agency in Kochi receives about 40 booking enquiries a day by email and website form. Staff copy each one into a spreadsheet, reply with a standard message, and forward it to the right agent (domestic, international or pilgrimage tours).

An automation:

1. **Trigger**: a new enquiry arrives by email or form.
2. **AI step**: read the enquiry and extract name, phone, destination, dates, number of travellers and budget; classify it as domestic, international or pilgrimage.
3. **Step**: add a row to the bookings sheet.
4. **Step**: send the customer a polite acknowledgement with the expected response time.
5. **Step**: notify the right agent on their team chat with the details.
6. **Check**: if the AI could not find a phone number or travel dates, send the enquiry to the front desk to handle by hand.

Staff save about two hours a day, enquiries are answered in minutes instead of hours, and none are lost in the inbox.

## Common mistakes

- **Automating a broken process.** Fix the process first, then automate it.
- **Starting with the hardest, rarest task.** Begin with frequent, simple, checkable work.
- **No owner.** Every automation breaks eventually; someone must notice and fix it.
- **Letting AI steps act unchecked on risky actions.** Add a person for payments, deletions and sensitive messages.
- **Forgetting data rules.** Customer data in automations is still covered by privacy law.

## Practise

Browse Zapier's guides on automation basics and read n8n's quickstart page.

1. List five repetitive tasks you or your team do every week. For each, write the trigger, the steps and where the data lives.
2. Score each for frequency, repetitiveness, ease of checking and pain, and pick the best candidate.
3. Mark which steps are simple rules and which would need an AI step, and where a person should check.

**You are done when you can** describe one automation from your own work as trigger, steps and checks, and say why it is a good first candidate.
