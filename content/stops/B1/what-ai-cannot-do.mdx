## What it is

AI assistants can draft, summarise, translate, explain and brainstorm remarkably well. They also have limits that are easy to forget because they write so fluently. The main ones:

- They can state **false facts** with full confidence.
- They may **not know recent events**, or your organisation's private information, unless it is given to them.
- They can be **weak with exact numbers**, calculations and dates unless they use a tool.
- They do not have **judgement or values** of their own; they produce likely text.
- They **cannot be held responsible**; you are.
- They **only know what you tell them** about your situation.

## How it works

**Why confident mistakes happen.** A language model writes the most likely next words based on patterns in its training. It does not look things up unless the product connects it to a search tool or your documents. When it lacks the facts, it can produce a plausible-sounding answer anyway. This is called a hallucination.

**Knowledge cut-off.** A model learns from data up to a certain date. Without a search tool, it may not know about a new government scheme, a recent price change or this year's tax rules.

**Private context.** It does not know your company's policies, your customer's history or your family's medical records unless you share them, and sharing them may not be appropriate (see the lesson on using AI safely).

**Numbers.** Many assistants now use a calculator or code tool for maths, but not always. Check important figures yourself.

**Judgement.** Deciding whether to approve a loan, hire a person, or what to tell a patient involves values, laws and context. AI can help prepare information; a person must decide and answer for the decision.

**Consistency.** Ask the same question twice and you may get different answers. That is fine for ideas, risky for facts.

## A worked example

Priya, an HR executive in Pune, asks an assistant: "What is the maximum gratuity amount that is tax-free in India?"

The assistant answers confidently with a figure. Priya remembers there was a change some years ago and is not sure the figure is current. She asks the assistant for its source; it gives a general description, not a link. She checks the Income Tax Department's website and a recent circular from her company's tax consultant, and finds the current limit.

Then she uses the assistant for what it does well: "Write a friendly two-paragraph email to an employee explaining how gratuity is calculated, leaving the limit as a placeholder." She fills in the verified figure herself.

Same tool, two tasks: she trusted it for drafting and checked it for facts.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating fluent writing as proof.** A well-written answer can still be wrong.
- **Asking about recent events without search.** The model may not know.
- **Letting AI make the decision.** It can prepare; you decide.
- **Assuming it knows your situation.** It knows only what you tell it.
- **Never using it because it makes mistakes.** People make mistakes too; use it where checking is easy.

## Practise

Read the chapter of Elements of AI on what AI is, and Microsoft's short introduction to generative AI.

1. Ask an assistant three factual questions about recent Indian news or rules, then check each answer against an official source. Note which were wrong or outdated.
2. Ask it the same opinion question twice and compare the answers.
3. Write down three tasks in your own work where AI could draft or summarise, and three where a person must decide.

**You are done when you can** name four limits of AI assistants and give an example of each from your own testing.
