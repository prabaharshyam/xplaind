## What it is

AI assistants can draft, summarise, translate, explain and brainstorm remarkably well. They also have limits that are easy to forget, precisely because they write so fluently. A confident, well-written answer feels true, even when it is not.

Here are the six limits that matter most in daily use:

| Limit | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **False facts** | It can state wrong information with full confidence | A made-up statistic or a law that does not exist |
| **Recent events** | It may not know what happened after its training data was collected | A new scheme announced last month |
| **Exact numbers** | It can be weak at calculations and dates unless it uses a tool | Adding up a long invoice wrongly |
| **Judgement** | It has no values or goals of its own; it produces likely text | Whether to give an employee a second chance |
| **Accountability** | It cannot be held responsible for a decision | Who answers when a loan is wrongly refused |
| **Your context** | It only knows what you tell it about your situation | Your company's leave policy |

Knowing these limits is not a reason to avoid AI. It is how you use it for the right things.

```text
        Where AI helps most            Where you must check or decide
 +-------------------------------+   +-------------------------------+
 | drafting, rewriting           |   | facts, figures, dates         |
 | summarising your own text     |   | recent rules and prices       |
 | explaining a topic            |   | decisions about people        |
 | brainstorming options         |   | anything you'll be blamed for |
 +-------------------------------+   +-------------------------------+
```

This lesson follows Priya, an HR executive in Pune, as she uses an assistant for one task it is good at and one where it fails quietly. By the end you will be able to name each limit and work around it.

## How it works

### Why confident mistakes happen

A language model writes the most likely next words based on patterns in its training data. It does not look things up unless the product connects it to a search tool or your documents. When it lacks the facts, it does not stop and say "I don't know" by default. It produces a plausible-sounding answer anyway. This is called a **hallucination**.

```text
  Question: "What is the tax-free gratuity limit?"
       |
       v
  Model: patterns from training text, some old, some new
       |
       +--> knows the current rule?      -> correct answer
       +--> learned an older figure?     -> confident, out of date
       +--> never saw it clearly?        -> confident, invented
```

All three paths produce equally confident writing. The tone tells you nothing about which path was taken.

### The knowledge cut-off

A model learns from data up to a certain date, its **knowledge cut-off**. Without a search tool, it may not know about a new government scheme, a recent price change or this year's tax rules. Some assistants can search the web, which helps, but only if the product uses search for your question and finds good sources.

### Private context

The model does not know your company's policies, your customer's history or your family's medical records unless you share them, and sharing them may not be appropriate. Its answer about "your leave policy" is a guess based on typical policies.

### Numbers

Many assistants now use a calculator or a code tool for maths, which is reliable. But not always: sometimes they do arithmetic "in their head" by predicting digits, which can go wrong on long sums. For important figures, ask to see the working and check it yourself.

### Judgement and accountability

Deciding whether to approve a loan, hire a person, discipline an employee or what to tell a patient involves values, laws and context. Someone must answer for the decision. AI can help prepare information, list options and point out risks. A person must decide. Keeping a person in that role is called **human-in-the-loop**.

### Consistency

Ask the same question twice and you may get different answers, because assistants choose words with some randomness. That is fine, even useful, for ideas. For facts, a changing answer is a warning sign.

## A worked example

Priya is an HR executive at a manufacturing company in Pune. An employee retiring after 22 years asks how much of her gratuity will be tax-free.

**Task 1: a fact.** Priya asks an assistant: "What is the maximum gratuity amount that is tax-free in India?"

The assistant answers confidently with a figure. Priya remembers the limit was raised some years ago and is not sure the figure is current. She asks the assistant for its source; it gives a general description, not a link.

She checks the Income Tax Department's website and a recent circular from her company's tax consultant, and finds the current limit. The assistant's figure was the older one.

**Task 2: a draft.** Now she uses the assistant for what it does well:

> "Write a warm, two-paragraph email to a retiring employee explaining how gratuity is calculated for 22 years of service, and that part of it is tax-free up to a limit. Leave the limit as [LIMIT] so I can fill it in."

The draft is clear and kind. She fills in the verified figure herself and adds a line congratulating the employee.

| Task | Limit involved | What Priya did |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tax-free gratuity limit | False facts, out of date | Checked the official source; the AI was wrong |
| Explanation email | None: drafting is a strength | Used the draft, filled in the verified figure |
| Whether to approve early payment | Judgement, accountability | Followed company policy and her manager's decision |

Same tool, three tasks: she trusted it for drafting, checked it for facts and kept the decision for people.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating fluent writing as proof.** A well-written answer can still be wrong.
- **Asking about recent events without search.** The model may simply not know.
- **Letting AI make the decision.** It can prepare information; you decide and answer for it.
- **Assuming it knows your situation.** It knows only what you tell it.
- **Trusting mental maths.** Ask for the working, or use a spreadsheet.
- **Never using it because it makes mistakes.** People make mistakes too. Use it where checking is easy.

Read the chapter of Elements of AI on what AI is, linked below, and Microsoft's short introduction to generative AI.

1. **Test recent facts.** Ask an assistant three factual questions about recent Indian news or rules, then check each answer against an official source. Note which were wrong or outdated.
2. **Test consistency.** Ask it the same opinion question twice and compare the answers.
3. **Sort your tasks.** Write down three tasks in your own work where AI could draft or summarise, and three where a person must decide.
