## What it is

Using AI safely at work comes down to three questions you ask every time:

```text
   BEFORE                       AFTER                       ALWAYS
 +----------------------+    +----------------------+    +----------------------+
 | What am I putting    |    | What am I getting    |    | Who is responsible?  |
 | in? Is this data     | -> | out? Is it accurate  | -> | You are, for what    |
 | allowed in this tool?|    | and appropriate?     |    | you send or decide.  |
 +----------------------+    +----------------------+    +----------------------+
```

Most AI incidents at workplaces are not dramatic hacks. They are ordinary people under time pressure doing ordinary things: pasting a client's contract into a personal chatbot account on a Friday evening, or forwarding an AI-written figure to a manager without checking it. This lesson is about building the habits that stop those mistakes.

You will follow Meena, an associate at a small consulting firm in Hyderabad, as she handles an urgent request without breaking her client's trust. By the end you will be able to decide what you may and may not paste into an AI tool at work, and explain why.

## How it works

### Know your tools

Many companies license an **enterprise** version of an AI assistant. These usually come with agreements on how data is stored, who can see it and whether it is used to train models. Free or personal accounts may have very different terms, such as keeping your conversations and using them to improve the service.

| | Personal free account | Company-approved enterprise tool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who agreed the terms | You, by clicking "accept" | Your company, in a contract |
| Is your data used for training? | Possibly, depending on settings | Usually not, by agreement |
| Can your company see or control it? | No | Yes: admin controls and logs |
| Fine for confidential work? | No | Yes, within the policy |

If your company has approved tools, use those for work. If it has approved none, treat every AI tool as unapproved for confidential information.

### Sort information before you paste it

| Category | Examples | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Usually fine** | Public articles, your company's public website text, your own generic writing, made-up examples | Any tool, still check the output |
| **Approved tools only** | Internal documents, unreleased plans, meeting notes | Only tools your company has approved |
| **Avoid entirely** unless a specific tool and use are approved | Customer or employee **personal data** (PII) such as names with phone numbers, Aadhaar, PAN, bank details, health information; passwords and keys; client documents under confidentiality agreements | Do not paste |

**PII** means personally identifiable information: any details that identify a person. India's **Digital Personal Data Protection Act** requires organisations to use personal data only for a lawful purpose, usually one the person consented to, and to protect it with reasonable security safeguards. Most of these duties apply from May 2027. Pasting customer details into an unapproved AI tool can break that law once it applies, and it can already break your company's contracts with clients.

### Strip what you do not need

Often you can get the help without the sensitive part:

```text
  Before:  "Draft a reply to Mr Suresh Iyer (98xxxxxx45, policy
            MH-2291-0087) about his delayed claim of Rs 1,84,000."

  After:   "Draft a polite reply to a customer whose health insurance
            claim is delayed by two weeks. Apologise, explain we need
            one more hospital document, and give a new date."
```

The second prompt gets an equally good draft. You add the real name and details yourself, in your own email system.

Other ways to strip data:

- Replace names with "Customer A" or "Supplier B".
- Remove or round account numbers, amounts and dates.
- Summarise the situation in your own words instead of pasting the whole document.
- Ask a general question: "What clauses usually carry risk in a vendor contract?"

### Check what comes out

AI can be wrong, out of date or wrong in tone. Before you use the output, check:

- facts and figures that matter;
- anything that could embarrass you or the company;
- that it reflects your company's actual policies, prices and promises.

### Own the result

If an AI-written report section has a wrong figure and you send it to your manager, it is your mistake. "The AI wrote it" is not an excuse at work, and it should not be.

### Follow the policy, or ask

Read your company's AI policy if there is one. If not, ask your manager, and follow the cautious rules above in the meantime.

## A worked example

Meena is an associate at a small consulting firm in Hyderabad. At 7 pm on Friday, a client emails a 40-page vendor contract and asks for a one-page summary of the risky clauses by Monday morning.

Her firm has not approved any AI tool yet. The quick option would be to paste the whole contract into her personal chatbot account and ask for a summary. She stops and runs the three questions:

| Question | Meena's answer |
| --- | --- |
| What am I putting in? | A confidential contract naming the client's vendor and pricing. The firm's agreement with the client forbids sharing documents with third parties. |
| What will I get out? | A summary she would have to check line by line anyway. |
| Who is responsible? | She is, and the firm is, to the client. |

So instead she:

1. Reads the contract herself and notes the clauses on liability, termination, payment and data protection.
2. Uses the chatbot only for structure, asking, "What clauses in a typical vendor services agreement usually carry the most risk for the buyer?" without pasting anything confidential. She gets a useful checklist of eight clause types.
3. Writes the summary herself using that checklist, which helps her spot an unusual auto-renewal clause.
4. On Monday, raises with her manager that the firm should approve a tool for this kind of work.

Her manager agrees. The firm later licenses an enterprise assistant for client work, and asks clients for consent before using it on their documents.

Meena's summary took longer than pasting would have, but she kept the client's trust and still got real help from AI.

## Common mistakes

- **Using personal AI accounts for work documents.** Even once, it puts the data outside your company's control.
- **Pasting personal data "just this once".** Remove or replace it; the draft will be just as good.
- **Forwarding AI output without reading it closely.** Check figures, names and promises.
- **Assuming no policy means no rules.** Laws and client contracts still apply.
- **Hiding AI use.** Being open with your manager protects you and helps the whole team learn.
- **Pasting screenshots without looking.** Screenshots often contain names, emails and numbers you did not mean to share.

## Practise

Work through the Ethics of AI course chapters on privacy and responsibility, linked below.

1. **Classify.** List five pieces of information you handle at work or college and classify each as fine to paste, approved tools only, or never.
2. **Strip and try.** Take one real task and do it with an AI tool after removing every name, number and confidential detail. Did you still get useful help?
3. **Find the policy.** Find your organisation's AI policy, or write the three questions you would ask your manager about it.

**You are done when you can** explain to a colleague what they may and may not paste into an AI tool at your workplace, and why they stay responsible for the result.
