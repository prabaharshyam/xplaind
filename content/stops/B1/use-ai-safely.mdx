## What it is

Using AI safely at work comes down to three questions you ask before and after every use:

1. **What am I putting in?** Is this information allowed to go into this tool?
2. **What am I getting out?** Is it accurate and appropriate enough to use?
3. **Who is responsible?** You are, for whatever you send, publish or decide.

Most AI incidents at workplaces are not dramatic hacks. They are ordinary people pasting a client's contract into a personal chatbot account, or forwarding an AI-written figure without checking it.

## How it works

**Know your tools.** Many companies license an enterprise version of an AI assistant with agreements on how data is stored and whether it is used for training. Free or personal accounts may have different terms, such as keeping conversations and using them to improve the service. If your company has approved tools, use those for work. If it has approved none, treat every AI tool as unapproved for confidential information.

**Sort information before you paste it.**

- *Usually fine*: public information (published articles, your company's public website text), your own generic writing, made-up examples.
- *Approved tools only*: internal documents, unreleased plans, meeting notes.
- *Avoid entirely unless a specific tool and use are approved*: customer or employee personal data (names with phone numbers, Aadhaar, PAN, bank details, health information), passwords and keys, client documents covered by confidentiality agreements.

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act requires organisations to use personal data only for the purposes people agreed to and to protect it. Pasting customer details into an unapproved AI tool can break that, and your company's contracts with clients.

**Strip what you do not need.** Often you can get the help without the sensitive part: replace names with "Customer A", remove account numbers, summarise rather than paste the full document.

**Check what comes out.** AI can be wrong, out of date or inappropriate in tone. Check facts and figures that matter, read for anything that would embarrass you or the company, and make sure it reflects your company's actual policies.

**Own the result.** If an AI-written report section contains a wrong figure and you send it to your manager, it is your mistake. "The AI wrote it" is not an excuse in a workplace, and it should not be.

**Follow and ask.** Read your company's AI policy if it has one. If not, ask your manager, and follow the cautious rules above meanwhile.

## A worked example

Meena is an associate at a small consulting firm in Hyderabad. On Friday evening a client emails a 40-page vendor contract and asks for a one-page summary of the risky clauses by Monday.

Her firm has not approved any AI tool yet. The quick option would be to paste the contract into her personal chatbot account. She stops and considers: the contract is confidential, it names the client's vendor and pricing, and her firm's agreement with the client forbids sharing documents with third parties.

Instead she:

- reads the contract herself and notes the clauses on liability, termination, payment and data;
- uses the chatbot only for help with structure, asking "What clauses in a typical vendor services agreement usually carry the most risk for the buyer?" without pasting anything confidential;
- writes the summary herself using that checklist;
- on Monday, raises with her manager that the firm should approve a tool for this kind of work.

Her manager agrees, and the firm later licenses an enterprise assistant for client work with the client's consent.

## Common mistakes

- **Using personal AI accounts for work documents.**
- **Pasting personal data "just this once".** Remove or replace it.
- **Forwarding AI output without reading it closely.**
- **Assuming no policy means no rules.** Laws and client contracts still apply.
- **Hiding AI use.** Being open with your manager protects you.

## Practise

Work through the Ethics of AI course chapters linked below on privacy and responsibility.

1. List five pieces of information you handle at work or college and classify each as fine to paste, approved tools only, or never.
2. Take one real task and do it with an AI tool after removing every name, number and confidential detail. Did you still get useful help?
3. Find your organisation's AI policy, or write the three questions you would ask your manager about it.

**You are done when you can** explain to a colleague what they may and may not paste into an AI tool at your workplace, and why they remain responsible for the result.
