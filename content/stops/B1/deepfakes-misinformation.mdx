## What it is

A **deepfake** is AI-made or AI-altered media that makes a real person appear to say or do something they did not. A video of a film star promoting a trading app. A politician's speech that never happened. A cloned voice of your son saying he is in trouble and needs money now.

**Misinformation** is false or misleading information, whether people share it knowingly or not. AI makes it cheap to produce convincing fakes at scale: fake notices, fake screenshots, fake news articles and fake voices.

```text
        What AI can fake today
 +---------------------------------------------+
 |  Face      generate a person who never      |
 |            existed, or swap a real face     |
 |  Lips      sync a real face to new words    |
 |  Voice     clone from a few seconds of audio|
 |  Documents fake circulars, letters, receipts|
 |  Text      fake news articles and posts     |
 +---------------------------------------------+
     Cheap, fast, and often good enough to fool
```

In India, deepfakes show up most often in investment scams using celebrity faces, voice-clone calls targeting parents and grandparents, and fake government notices shared on WhatsApp.

This lesson follows Lakshmi's father, a retired teacher in Thanjavur, as he faces a deepfake video and a voice-clone call in the same week. By the end you will know why spotting fakes by eye is not enough, what to check instead, and where to report in India.

## How it works

### How fakes are made

AI tools can now do things that needed a film studio a few years ago:

- **Generate faces** of people who do not exist.
- **Swap a face** onto another person's body in a video.
- **Sync lips** to new audio, so a real person seems to say new words.
- **Clone a voice** from a short recording, sometimes only a few seconds taken from a public video or a voice note.

### Why spotting by eye is getting harder

Older fakes had tell-tale signs: odd blinking, blurry teeth, mismatched lighting, strange hands, a robotic voice. These still appear in cheap fakes, but the best fakes now pass casual inspection, especially on a small phone screen. Research projects such as Detect Fakes show that people are often not much better than chance at spotting good ones.

So the useful question is not "does this look fake?" but "**does the context hold up?**"

### What works better: check the context

| Check | Ask yourself | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source** | Who posted it first? Is it on the person's official account or a reputable news site? | A "news clip" that only exists on a forwarded WhatsApp message |
| **Plausibility** | Would this person really say this? Is it pushing me to act fast, pay, invest or share? | A famous businessman promising 30% monthly returns |
| **Corroboration** | Do reliable outlets report it? What do fact-checkers say? | Searching the claim shows news reports calling it a scam |
| **Pause** | What does waiting an hour cost me? | Usually nothing, while acting fast can cost a lot |

For claims about the Government of India, **PIB Fact Check** verifies or debunks them. Independent fact-checkers also publish regularly.

### Voice-clone scams

These follow a script:

```text
  1. Urgent call or voice note from a "relative"
         "I've had an accident" / "I've been arrested"
  2. Secrecy              "Don't tell Amma"
  3. Pressure             "Send it now, there's no time"
  4. Payment              UPI ID, gift card or bank transfer
         |
         v
  Defence: hang up -> call back on the known number -> code word
```

Three habits protect your family:

1. **Hang up and call back** on the person's known number before paying anything.
2. **Agree a family code word** for real emergencies.
3. **Never pay under pressure.** Real emergencies survive a two-minute check.

### Report it

In India, report cyber fraud on the national cybercrime portal or by calling the **1930** helpline, and report the fake content to the platform. Report fast: banks can sometimes freeze money that was sent in the last few hours.

### The rules

India's IT Rules require platforms to act on unlawful content, including impersonation, when it is reported, and the government has proposed rules requiring AI-generated content to be labelled. Rules are changing, but the habits above work regardless of what the rules say.

## A worked example

Lakshmi's father, Ramanathan, is a retired teacher in Thanjavur who is active on several WhatsApp groups. A month ago, Lakshmi sat with him for twenty minutes and talked about scams. This week, that conversation pays off twice.

**Monday: the video.** A friend forwards a video of a well-known business leader recommending a "government-approved" investment app with "guaranteed 30% monthly returns". The video looks real: the voice, the face, the office background.

Ramanathan remembers the context checks. He searches the leader's name with the app's name. Several news sites report the video as a known deepfake scam, and the company has put out a statement denying it. He does not install the app, and he replies to the group with the news link.

**Wednesday: the call.** His phone rings. It sounds exactly like his grandson Karthik: "Thatha, I've had an accident on the bike, please send ₹50,000 to this UPI ID for the hospital, don't tell Amma."

His heart races. Then he notices the script: urgent, secret, pay now. He says he will call back, hangs up, and dials Karthik's number. Karthik answers from his college hostel in Chennai, completely fine.

| Message | Warning signs | What he checked | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Investment video | Guaranteed huge returns, "government-approved", forwarded only | Searched news for the leader and the app | Known deepfake scam; warned his group |
| Grandson's call | Urgent, secret, pay now, new UPI ID | Hung up and called Karthik directly | Karthik was safe; scam reported |

He reports the caller's number on the cybercrime portal and posts a warning on his friends' group. The family now uses a code word for emergencies.

## Common mistakes

- **Relying on spotting glitches.** Good fakes have none. Check the source and the context instead.
- **Acting under urgency.** Scammers rush you on purpose. Pause and verify.
- **Forwarding before checking.** Sharing spreads harm even with good intentions.
- **Trusting a familiar voice or face.** Both can be faked. Call back on a known number.
- **Not reporting.** Reports help stop the same scam reaching others, and fast reports can help recover money.
- **Assuming only older people are targeted.** Students and professionals fall for job, loan and investment fakes too.

## Practise

Try the Detect Fakes project, linked below, browse PIB Fact Check's recent posts, and do one lateral reading lesson.

1. **Test yourself.** Take the Detect Fakes test and record your score. Most people are surprised.
2. **Check a viral claim.** Take one viral claim from your family WhatsApp groups and check it with lateral reading or a fact-checker.
3. **Protect your family.** Agree an emergency code word with your family and teach one elderly relative the "hang up and call back" rule, in their language.

**You are done when you can** explain to an elderly relative, in their language, how to handle an urgent money request from a familiar voice.
