## What it is

A **deepfake** is AI-made or AI-altered media that makes a real person appear to say or do something they did not: a video of a film star promoting a trading app, a politician's speech that never happened, or a cloned voice of your son saying he is in trouble and needs money now.

**Misinformation** is false or misleading information, whether shared knowingly or not. AI makes it cheaper to produce convincing fakes at scale: fake notices, fake screenshots, fake news articles.

## How it works

**How fakes are made.** AI tools can generate faces, swap a face onto another body in a video, sync lips to new audio, and clone a voice from a short recording, sometimes a few seconds from a social media video.

**Why spotting by eye is getting harder.** Older fakes had tell-tale signs: odd blinking, blurry teeth, mismatched lighting, strange hands, robotic voices. These still appear, but the best fakes now pass casual inspection. Research projects show that people are often no better than chance at spotting good fakes.

**What works better: check the context.**

- **Source**: who posted it first? Is it on the person's official account or a reputable news site?
- **Plausibility**: would this person really say this? Is it pushing you to act fast, pay, invest or share?
- **Corroboration**: do reliable outlets report it? Search the claim; check fact-checkers. For claims about the Government of India, PIB Fact Check verifies or debunks.
- **Pause before sharing**: the cost of waiting an hour is low.

**Voice-clone scams.** A call or voice note from a "relative" in an emergency asking for money urgently. Protect yourself: hang up and call the person back on their known number; agree a family code word; never pay under pressure.

**Report it.** In India, report cyber fraud on the national cybercrime portal or by calling the 1930 helpline, and report fake content to the platform. Banks act faster when fraud is reported quickly.

**Rules.** India's IT Rules require platforms to act on unlawful content, including impersonation, when reported, and the government has proposed rules requiring AI-generated content to be labelled. Rules are changing; the habits above work regardless.

## A worked example

Lakshmi's father, retired in Thanjavur, receives a WhatsApp video of a well-known business leader recommending a "government-approved" investment app with guaranteed 30% monthly returns. The video looks real. A day later, he gets a call in what sounds like his grandson's voice: "Thatha, I've had an accident, please send ₹50,000 to this UPI ID, don't tell Amma."

Lakshmi had talked to him about scams the month before. He checks the video's claim by searching the leader's name and the app: news sites report it as a known deepfake scam. For the call, he hangs up and dials his grandson directly; the grandson is at college and fine. He reports the number on the cybercrime portal and warns his friends' WhatsApp group.

The family now uses a code word for emergencies.

## Common mistakes

- **Relying on spotting glitches.** Good fakes have none; check the source and context.
- **Acting under urgency.** Scammers rush you. Pause and verify.
- **Forwarding before checking.** Sharing spreads harm even with good intentions.
- **Trusting a familiar voice.** Voices can be cloned. Call back on a known number.
- **Not reporting.** Reports help stop the same scam reaching others.

## Practise

Try the Detect Fakes project, browse PIB Fact Check's recent posts, and do one lateral-reading lesson.

1. Try to spot real versus fake in the Detect Fakes test and record your score.
2. Take one viral claim from your family WhatsApp groups and check it with lateral reading or a fact-checker.
3. Agree an emergency code word with your family and teach one elderly relative the "hang up and call back" rule.

**You are done when you can** explain to an elderly relative, in their language, how to handle an urgent money request from a familiar voice.
