## What it is

Most people meet generative AI as a chat box. The same technology now works with pictures, sound and video, and much of it is already inside apps you use: your phone camera and keyboard, your office suite, your video-call app and your chat assistant.

| Kind | What it does | Everyday example | Where it still fails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Image creation** | Makes a picture from a description | A festival poster for a small business | Exact words, prices, fine details; check any Indian-language text |
| **Image editing** | Removes a background, changes light, extends a picture | A product photo on a clean background | Changes things you asked it to keep |
| **Image reading** | Describes a photo or reads the text in it | A bill, a medicine strip, a timetable | Handwriting, faded print, similar digits such as 1 and 7 |
| **Speech to text** | Turns audio into written text | Transcribing a voice note or lecture | Names, numbers, noise, mixed languages |
| **Text to speech** | Reads text aloud in a natural voice | A shop announcement in Hindi or Tamil | Odd stress on names and some words |
| **Meeting assistants** | Transcribe a call and summarise it with action items | Notes from a team call | Unsupported languages, invented or missed decisions |
| **Video and music** | Make short clips, often with sound | A few seconds of product teaser | Length, keeping a face or product the same across clips, cost |

Models that read images as well as text are called **vision-language models**. They are what lets you photograph a handwritten list and ask an assistant to turn it into a spreadsheet.

The one idea to carry through this lesson: **every one of these tools hands you something that looks finished.** A poster looks printed, a transcript looks typed, a summary looks like minutes. The skill is knowing where each tool fails and checking exactly there, then handling rights, privacy and labels before anything leaves your phone.

```flow
title: Before anything AI-made is shared
dir: LR
Make it | image, transcript, voice or notes -> Check the risky parts | words, names, numbers, prices
Check the risky parts -> Check rights | real people, brands, voices, consent
Check rights -> Check privacy | ID numbers, phone numbers, other people
Check privacy -> Label and share | declare AI content when the platform asks
```

This lesson follows Anitha, who runs a home bakery in Tiruchirappalli, as she uses these tools for a Diwali campaign.

## How it works

### Why these tools fail where they do

Image and speech models learn from huge collections of examples. When they make something, they produce what is most likely given what they saw. That is why the overall picture or sentence is usually good: common things are easy to predict.

Rare, exact details are different. An image model draws letters as shapes, so a word it has seen less often can come out with a missing or extra letter, and a script with joined letters and vowel signs, such as Hindi or Tamil, gives it more ways to go wrong. A speech model picks the most likely word for a sound, so "fifteen" and "fifty", or a family name it has never heard, can easily swap. A meeting summary picks the most likely shape of a decision, so it can state a decision nobody actually made.

The practical rule follows: **use AI for the draft, and check the exact details yourself every time**: words in images, names, numbers, prices, dates and who agreed to what.

### Creating and editing images

You describe the picture in a prompt: subject, setting, style, colours, and the shape it is for, such as square for Instagram. Leave empty space where text will go. The lesson "AI image generation and editing with Gemini (Nano Banana) and Midjourney" teaches prompting in depth.

**Text in images.** Newer models write short English text far better than earlier ones. Google, for example, says its Nano Banana Pro model renders legible text in several languages, but its own list of limits still includes small text and exact spelling. Test any Hindi, Tamil or other Indian-script text letter by letter, because a wrong vowel sign is easy to miss. Default rule: **generate the picture, type the words.** Put headlines, prices, dates and phone numbers on with a design app such as Canva, and have a fluent reader check any Hindi, Tamil or other Indian-language text.

**Editing.** Asking a tool to edit a real photo ("brighter light, plain white background, keep the product unchanged") is often better than generating a new image. For anything you sell, show the real product. A generated "photo" of a cake that does not look like your cake misleads customers.

### Reading photos of documents

Photograph a bill, a handwritten list or a timetable and ask an assistant to read it or turn it into a table. This saves a lot of typing. Small, faded or handwritten text is often misread, and digits such as 1 and 7, or 5 and 6, are easy to confuse.

Two habits make this safe:

- **Check with arithmetic.** Ask the assistant to multiply quantity by rate on every line and add up the amounts. A line that does not match, or a total that differs from the paper, points straight at a misread.
- **Crop before you upload.** A photo can carry far more than you meant to share: an Aadhaar number, a bank account, someone else's phone number, a medical detail. Crop or cover those parts first. When a form asks for Aadhaar, use a masked Aadhaar from UIDAI, which shows only the last four digits. At work, use only the tools your organisation approves for documents.

### Speech to text and translation

Transcription is very good for clear English and improving fast for Indian languages. It still makes errors with:

- strong accents and fast speech;
- background noise, such as traffic or a busy shop;
- Hinglish and other mixed-language speech;
- names, places, numbers and technical words.

You can reduce errors before you start: record close to the phone in a quiet room, choose the right language in the tool, say important numbers slowly (for example, "four hundred and fifty, four five zero"), and spell out unusual names. After transcribing, read the transcript against the audio for every name and number.

**Translation** has its own trap: it can be grammatically correct but sound stiff or wrong to local readers. A quick check is to translate it back into the original language and compare. For anything public, such as a notice or an advert, have a native speaker read it.

### Voice generation

Text-to-speech produces natural announcements in many Indian languages, which helps shops, schools and public notices. Use the tool's stock voices, or your own voice. **Cloning a real person's voice** without consent is a different matter. In 2024 the Bombay High Court, in an interim order in Arijit Singh's case, stopped several named AI platforms from offering tools that copy the singer's voice, treating his voice as part of his personality rights. Cloned voices are also a main tool of the scams covered in the lesson "Deepfakes and misinformation".

### Meeting assistants

These join a video call, transcribe it and write a summary with action items. Before one joins:

1. **Tell everyone and get agreement** that the call is being recorded and summarised.
2. **Follow your organisation's rules** and the client's rules on which tools may join which meetings, and know where the recording is stored.
3. **Check the language.** Many meeting note-takers support only a short list of languages and expect one language per meeting. Check the tool's supported languages before a Hindi or Hinglish call.
4. **Check the summary before sharing.** Compare decisions, numbers, dates and owners with what was said; a confident summary can miss a condition or invent an agreement.

### Video and music

Short video clips with sound are now available in mainstream assistants. Google's Gemini app, for example, makes short clips with its Veo models, mostly on paid plans and with a small daily limit. Expect clips a few seconds long, trouble keeping the same face or product across clips, and many attempts for one usable shot. Background music generators raise the same rights questions as images. The lesson "Create cinematic AI videos with Google Veo and Flow" covers video properly.

### Tools to try (as of November 2026)

Free tiers and limits change often, so check each tool's own page before you rely on it.

| Job | Options to try | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Make or edit images | Gemini app (free daily quota), ChatGPT (limited free images) | Commercial-use terms; text in images |
| Add text to images | Canva or any design app | Check Indian-script text with a fluent reader |
| Indian-language speech and translation | Bhashini app from the Government of India (free); Sarvam AI's speech models, through its developer platform | Test on your own accent and vocabulary first |
| Quick voice notes to text | Voice typing in your phone keyboard | Numbers and names |
| Meeting notes | Note-takers in Google Meet and Microsoft Teams (paid plans or add-on licences) | Supported languages; consent; storage |

### Rights, labels and ownership

**Labels.** India's IT Rules amendment, notified on 10 February 2026 and in force from 20 February 2026, covers "synthetically generated information": AI-made or AI-altered images, audio and video that appear real. Platforms must label such content, and large social media platforms must ask users to declare it when they upload. When a platform asks, declare honestly. Rules keep changing, so check the latest before a big campaign.

**Ownership.** Who owns a purely AI-made image is not settled in India. The Copyright Act's clause on computer-generated works dates from 1994, long before today's tools, and a committee set up by DPIIT published the first part of a working paper on generative AI and copyright in December 2025, covering AI training and leaving ownership of AI outputs for a later part. Until this is settled, do not use a purely AI-made image as your logo or trademark: you may not be able to stop others copying it. Use AI for ideas, then have a designer create or substantially rework the final logo, and take legal advice for anything you plan to register.

**Commercial use.** Each tool has its own terms on selling or advertising with what it makes. Read them before you use an image in an advert.

| Do | Do not |
| --- | --- |
| Type important text onto images yourself | Publish text the image tool wrote without checking every letter |
| Declare AI content when a platform asks | Pass off AI copies of a living artist's style as your own art |
| Get agreement before recording people | Record calls secretly |
| Use stock voices or your own voice | Clone a real person's voice or face without consent |
| Crop ID numbers and other people's details from photos | Upload other people's private photos or recordings |

## A worked example

Anitha runs a home bakery in Tiruchirappalli. She wants to promote Diwali sweet boxes on Instagram and WhatsApp, and she has one evening.

**The poster.** She photographs her real sweet box and asks the image tool to edit it: "square poster, keep the box unchanged, put it on a wooden table with diyas glowing behind, gold and maroon colours, and a Tamil Diwali greeting at the top". She asks for the greeting to test the tool, then picks one of four results. The greeting has a wrong vowel sign, so she removes it and types the greeting, the price and her phone number in Canva. Her sister, who reads Tamil well, checks the final poster. When Instagram asks whether the post contains AI-made content, she says yes.

**The caption.** She records a voice note in Tamil describing the box, saying the price in English as many people do, transcribes it with an AI tool, and asks the assistant for a short caption in Tamil and English. The transcript turned the sweet adhirasam into a different word, and "four hundred and fifty rupees" into ₹415. She catches both by reading the transcript against her price list.

**The supplier bill.** She photographs her supplier's handwritten invoice, after covering the supplier's personal mobile number, and asks the assistant to make a table. These are the figures on the paper bill, for example:

| Item | Quantity | Rate (₹) | Amount (₹) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cashews | 5 kg | 950 | 4,750 |
| Sugar | 75 kg | 44 | 3,300 |
| Ghee | 10 kg | 620 | 6,200 |
| **Total** | | | **14,250** |

The assistant read the sugar quantity as 15 kg but kept the amount at ₹3,300. She asks it to multiply quantity by rate on each line: 15 × 44 = 660, which does not match 3,300, so she checks the paper and corrects it to 75 kg. Now 75 × 44 = 3,300 and the three amounts add up to the bill's total of ₹14,250.

**A suggestion she refuses.** A friend suggests putting a well-known actor's face on the poster "to get more likes". Anitha says no: she has no permission, it would mislead customers, it could break the actor's personality rights, and the tool's rules forbid it.

| Task | What went wrong | How she caught it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Poster | Wrong Tamil vowel sign in AI text | Typed text herself; a fluent reader checked |
| Caption | One sweet name and the price misheard | Read the transcript against her price list |
| Supplier bill | 75 kg read as 15 kg | Quantity times rate did not match the amount |
| Actor's face | Suggested, not made | Consent and rights check before making it |

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a finished-looking draft as finished.** A neat poster, transcript or summary can still carry a wrong price or name. Check the exact details every time.
- **Asking for twenty more images to fix a spelling.** Regenerating rarely fixes text reliably. Type it yourself.
- **Uploading whole documents.** Photos of bills and forms often carry Aadhaar numbers, phone numbers or bank details. Crop first.
- **Using a meeting assistant in a language it does not support.** A Hinglish call in an English-only tool gives a confident but wrong summary.
- **Publishing a machine translation without a native reader.** It may be correct but sound wrong to customers.
- **Using a purely AI-made image as a logo.** You may not be able to protect it.
- **Skipping the AI-content declaration** when a platform asks.
- **Cloning a voice "just for fun".** A real person's voice or face needs their consent, even in a joke.

## Practice

Do Microsoft Learn's short module on generative AI fundamentals, linked below, and read Google's help page on making images in Gemini.

1. **Make a poster.** Create a poster background for a real or imaginary event, then add the words yourself, including one line in your own language. Ask a fluent reader to check it.
2. **Test transcription.** Record a one-minute voice note in your own language with three names and three numbers in it. Transcribe it with two different tools and count the errors in names and numbers for each.
3. **Read a bill.** Photograph a printed or handwritten bill, cover any personal details, and have an assistant turn it into a table. Use the quantity-times-rate check to find any misread line.

**You are done when you can** name three non-chat AI tools you used, the exact detail each got wrong, how you caught it, and one thing you would not do with them.
