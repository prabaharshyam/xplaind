## What it is

An AI system is **biased** when it performs worse for, or treats unfairly, some groups of people: by gender, caste, religion, region, language, age, disability, income or other traits. **Fairness** is the work of finding and reducing those differences where they are not justified.

Bias in AI is rarely intentional. Nobody writes a rule saying "reject applicants from this district". It usually comes from two places: **the data the system learned from**, and **the way the system is used**.

```text
  Past decisions          Model learns            Model repeats
 +----------------+     +----------------+     +--------------------+
 | Mostly men     |     | "Men = good    |     | Ranks women lower  |
 | hired for      | --> |  engineers"    | --> | for new engineering|
 | engineering    |     |  (a pattern,   |     | roles              |
 | roles          |     |   not a fact)  |     |                    |
 +----------------+     +----------------+     +--------------------+
          Unfair history in, unfair predictions out
```

This matters more as AI makes or shapes decisions about loans, jobs, school admissions, insurance and welfare. A biased spell-checker is annoying. A biased loan model changes lives.

This lesson follows a Bengaluru startup that discovers its resume-screening tool is unfair, and fixes it. By the end you will be able to explain where bias comes from, recognise it in Indian settings and say how to test for it.

## How it works

### Where bias comes from

| Source | What happens | Indian example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Historical data** | The model copies past unfair decisions | Past hiring favoured a few colleges and mostly men, so the model does too |
| **Missing data** | Groups with few examples get worse results | A speech system trained on urban English speakers struggles with rural accents |
| **Proxies** | Other features stand in for a sensitive trait | Surname, PIN code or college reveal caste, religion or income |
| **Language** | Models work best in the language they saw most | A chatbot answers well in English and poorly in Odia |
| **Use** | A tool is used for a job it was not designed for | An assistant meant to suggest becomes an automatic gatekeeper |

### Why removing a field is not enough

A common first idea is: "Just delete gender and caste from the data." It rarely works, because other features carry the same information. In India, a surname can indicate caste or religion; a PIN code can indicate income and community; a college can indicate both. The model finds these **proxies** and learns the old pattern anyway.

### How bias shows up

- A loan model approves fewer applicants from certain districts, even with similar repayment records.
- A resume screener ranks women lower for engineering roles.
- An image generator shows only men when asked for "a doctor" and only women for "a nurse".
- A chatbot gives shorter, poorer answers in Indian languages than in English.
- A face-recognition system makes more mistakes on darker skin tones.

### What can be done

1. **Test by group.** Measure accuracy and outcomes for each group, not just overall. Bias hides in averages.
2. **Fix the data.** Collect more representative examples, rebalance, and reduce harmful proxies.
3. **Keep people in charge.** Use human review for decisions that affect lives, with the power to override the model.
4. **Explain and allow appeal.** People affected should understand the main reasons and be able to challenge a decision.
5. **Monitor after launch.** Users and data change; repeat the group tests regularly.

```text
  Build --> Test by group --> Fix data or rules --> Test again
    ^                                                   |
    |            Monitor in use, every quarter          |
    +---------------------------------------------------+
```

### Fairness has trade-offs

There is more than one definition of fairness, and they can conflict:

| Definition | Means | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equal approval rates | Each group approved at the same rate | May approve people likely to struggle with repayment |
| Equal error rates | The model is wrong equally often for each group | Approval rates may still differ |
| Same treatment for similar people | Two people with the same record get the same result | Needs agreement on what "similar" means |

Choosing one is a value decision, made openly with the people affected, the law and the organisation's purpose in mind. It is not something to leave to whoever happens to train the model.

## A worked example

A Bengaluru startup builds a resume-screening tool for a large IT client. It trains the model on five years of the client's hiring decisions: which resumes were shortlisted and which were not.

**Testing by group.** Before launch, the team compares shortlist rates for candidates with similar skills and experience:

| Group (similar skills and experience) | Shortlisted before the fix | After the fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Graduates of five well-known colleges | 48% | 31% |
| Graduates of other colleges | 14% | 27% |
| Men, engineering roles | 33% | 29% |
| Women, engineering roles | 19% | 27% |

**Finding the cause.** Past hiring had favoured those five colleges and mostly men, and the model had learned the pattern. College names and gendered words in resumes, such as "women's hostel secretary", acted as proxies.

**The fix.** The team removes names, photos, college names and gendered terms from what the model scores, rebalances the training examples, and tests again by group. The gaps narrow sharply, though not to zero.

**Changing the product, not just the model.** The tool now suggests a shortlist with reasons instead of rejecting people automatically. Recruiters review every rejection. Candidates can ask for a human review. The team repeats the group tests every quarter and reports the results to the client.

The model got fairer, but the bigger change was in how it was used.

## Common mistakes

- **Looking only at overall accuracy.** A system can be accurate on average and unfair to a group. Always break results down.
- **Thinking removing a sensitive field removes bias.** Proxies like surname, PIN code and college remain.
- **Assuming AI is neutral because it is maths.** It reflects the data and decisions it learned from.
- **Testing only in English.** Indian users speak many languages; test in the ones your users speak.
- **Automating high-stakes decisions without human review or appeal.** Keep a person accountable.
- **Testing once and forgetting.** Bias can appear after launch as data and users change.

## Practise

Take the chapter on fairness in the free Ethics of AI course, linked below, and read the EU AI Act summary's section on high-risk uses.

1. **Test an image generator.** Ask for "a doctor", "a nurse", "an engineer" and "a farmer" ten times each. Note the genders and appearances you get.
2. **Compare languages.** Ask a chat assistant the same question in English and in an Indian language you know, and compare the quality and length of the answers.
3. **Plan a bias test.** For one AI use in hiring or lending, list three ways bias could enter and one test for each.

**You are done when you can** explain proxies with an Indian example and describe how you would test a system for bias by group.
