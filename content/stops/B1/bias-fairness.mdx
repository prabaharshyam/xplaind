## What it is

An AI system is **biased** when it performs worse for, or treats unfairly, some groups of people: by gender, caste, religion, region, language, age, disability, income or other traits. **Fairness** is the effort to find and reduce such differences where they are unjustified.

Bias in AI is rarely intentional. It usually comes from the data the system learned from and the way it is used.

## How it works

**Where bias comes from.**

- **Historical data**: if past hiring favoured certain colleges or men for technical roles, a model trained on those decisions learns the same pattern.
- **Missing data**: if a speech system was trained mostly on urban English speakers, it works worse for rural speakers or regional accents. If a health model learned mostly from city hospitals, it may misjudge village patients.
- **Proxies**: removing "gender" or "caste" from data does not remove bias if other features (name, surname, address, PIN code, college) stand in for them.
- **Language**: models trained mostly on English often work worse in Indian languages and may carry stereotypes about groups.
- **Use**: a model fine on average can be misused, for example a tool meant for assistance used as an automatic gatekeeper.

**How it shows up.** A loan model approves fewer applicants from certain districts with similar repayment ability. A resume screener ranks women lower for engineering roles. An image generator shows only men when asked for "a doctor" and only women for "a nurse". A chatbot gives poorer answers in Odia than in English.

**What can be done.**

- *Test by group*: measure accuracy and outcomes for different groups, not just overall.
- *Fix the data*: collect more representative examples; remove or reduce harmful proxies.
- *Keep people in charge*: human review for decisions that affect lives, with the power to override.
- *Explain and allow appeal*: people affected should understand and be able to challenge decisions.
- *Monitor*: bias can appear after launch as users and data change.

**Fairness has trade-offs.** Different definitions of fairness (equal approval rates, equal error rates) can conflict. Choosing one is a value decision, made openly with the people affected and the law in mind.

## A worked example

A Bengaluru startup builds a resume-screening tool trained on five years of a client's hiring decisions.

Testing by group shows that, for candidates with similar skills and experience, the tool shortlists graduates of a handful of well-known colleges far more often, and shortlists women for engineering roles less often. The cause: past hiring decisions favoured those colleges and mostly men, and the model learned it. College names and gendered words in resumes acted as proxies.

The team removes names, photos, college names and gendered terms from the scoring input, rebalances training examples, and tests again by group. The gaps narrow sharply. They also change the product: the tool suggests a shortlist with reasons, recruiters review every rejection, and candidates can request a human review. They repeat the group tests every quarter.

## Common mistakes

- **Looking only at overall accuracy.** Bias hides in averages.
- **Thinking removing a sensitive field removes bias.** Proxies remain.
- **Assuming AI is neutral because it is maths.** It reflects its data.
- **Testing only in English.** Indian users speak many languages.
- **Automating high-stakes decisions without human review or appeal.**

## Practise

Take the chapter on fairness in the free Ethics of AI course, and read the EU AI Act summary's section on high-risk uses.

1. Ask an image generator for "a doctor", "a nurse", "an engineer" and "a farmer" ten times each and note the genders and appearances you get.
2. Ask a chat assistant the same question in English and in an Indian language you know, and compare the quality.
3. For one AI use in hiring or lending, list three ways bias could enter and one test for each.

**You are done when you can** explain proxies with an Indian example and say how you would test a system for bias by group.
