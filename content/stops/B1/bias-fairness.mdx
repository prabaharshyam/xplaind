## What it is

An AI system is **biased** when it works worse for, or decides worse about, some groups of people without a good reason. The groups might be defined by gender, caste, religion, region, language, age, disability or income. **Fairness** is the work of finding those differences, deciding which ones are not justified, and reducing them.

Bias causes two kinds of harm:

- **Allocation harm:** the system gives or withholds something that matters, such as a loan, a job interview or a welfare benefit.
- **Quality-of-service and representation harm:** the system simply works worse for some people, or describes them through stereotypes. For example, a voice assistant that fails on a Bhojpuri accent, or a chatbot that links a surname with a particular job.

Bias is rarely intentional. Nobody writes a rule saying "reject applicants from this district". It usually comes from **the data the system learned from**, and from **the way the system is used**.

```flow
dir: LR
title: Unfair history in, unfair predictions out
Past decisions | mostly men hired for engineering roles -> Model learns | the pattern, not the merit
Model learns -> Model repeats | ranks women lower for new roles
```

This matters more as AI makes or shapes decisions about loans, jobs, admissions, insurance and welfare. A biased spell-checker is annoying. A biased loan model changes lives, and it repeats the same mistake thousands of times a day.

This lesson follows a Bengaluru startup that discovers its resume-screening tool is unfair, and fixes it. By the end you will be able to say where bias comes from, measure it with a simple ratio, test for it without holding caste or religion data, and choose sensible fixes.

## How it works

### Where bias comes from

| Source | What happens | Indian example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Historical labels** | The model copies past decisions, fair or not | Past hiring favoured a few colleges and mostly men, so the model does too |
| **Missing data** | Groups with few examples get worse results | A speech model trained mostly on urban English speakers struggles with rural accents |
| **Proxies** | Other features stand in for a sensitive trait | Surname, PIN code or college can reveal caste, religion or income |
| **Language** | Models work best in the languages they saw most | A chatbot answers well in English and poorly in Odia or Santali |
| **Use** | A tool is used for a job it was not designed for | A tool meant to suggest a shortlist becomes an automatic gatekeeper |

The first row is the one people miss most often. A model learns from **labels**, the "right answers" in its training data. If the label is "was this person hired?" or "did the manager rate them highly?", the model learns to imitate those past choices, including any unfairness in them. It is not learning who is good at the job.

### Why removing a field is not enough

A common first idea is: "Just delete gender and caste from the data." It rarely works, because other features carry the same information. In India a surname can indicate caste or religion. A PIN code can indicate income and community. A college, a school board, a hostel role or a sport can indicate gender, region or class. The model finds these **proxies** and learns the old pattern anyway.

A simple check: try to predict the removed trait from the features that remain. If a small model can guess gender from the rest of a resume much better than chance, your scoring model can use that signal too. Then look at which features drive the guess, and remove, coarsen or rethink them.

### How bias shows up

- A loan model approves fewer applicants from rural PIN codes, even with similar repayment records.
- A resume screener ranks women lower for engineering roles.
- An image generator draws mostly men for "a doctor" and mostly women for "a nurse".
- A chatbot gives shorter, poorer answers in Indian languages than in English.
- A face-analysis system makes more mistakes on some faces. The 2018 Gender Shades study tested three commercial systems that guess gender from a photo. Their error rates were up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women and no more than 0.8% for lighter-skinned men.
- A language model repeats caste stereotypes. In October 2025, an MIT Technology Review investigation reported that OpenAI's GPT-5 and its video model Sora often produced caste stereotypes when tested with Indian prompts.

Notice the Gender Shades result. The worst gap was not "women" or "darker skin" alone but the **intersection** of both. Always check combined groups, such as rural women or older Hindi-medium applicants, not only one trait at a time.

### Measuring bias: the first numbers to compute

Start with three numbers for each group.

1. **Selection rate:** the share of the group that gets the good outcome, for example shortlisted or approved.
2. **Selection-rate ratio:** the lowest group's rate divided by the highest group's. If 30% of men and 21% of women are shortlisted, the ratio is 21 ÷ 30 = 0.70.
3. **Wrongful rejection rate:** among people who were in fact qualified or would have repaid, the share the model rejected. This needs outcome data, but it is the error applicants care about most.

A widely used first screen comes from US employment guidance from 1978, the **four-fifths rule**: a group selected at under 80% of the top group's rate is treated as evidence of possible adverse impact. Use it as a rule of thumb, not a verdict. It is not Indian law, a ratio above 0.8 can still hide a real problem, and a ratio below 0.8 can have a fair explanation, such as a real difference in job-relevant skills. Below 0.8 means "investigate now", not "guilty".

| Metric | Question it answers | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Selection rate per group | Who gets the good outcome? | Groups may differ in real qualifications |
| Selection-rate ratio | How big is the gap? | 0.8 is a screen, not a legal line in India |
| Wrongful rejection rate per group | Who is wrongly turned away? | Needs data on how people actually performed |
| Same numbers for combined groups | Is one intersection hit hardest? | Cells get small fast |

**Small groups are noisy.** If only 30 applicants come from one group and 3 are shortlisted, that is 10%, but one more shortlist would make it 13%. A single person moves the rate by more than three points. With fewer than about a hundred people in a group, treat a gap as a reason to collect more data or review cases by hand, not as a final answer.

### Testing when you do not hold group data

Most Indian companies do not record caste or religion, and should not collect it casually. That does not make testing impossible. Three practical methods:

- **Paired (counterfactual) tests.** Make copies of real cases that differ in one detail only: the name, surname, PIN code, pronoun or college. Score both copies. If "Priya Paswan" scores lower than "Priya Sharma" with the same resume, the model is using that signal. This works for chatbots too: ask the same question about people from different communities, or in Hindi and in English, and compare the answers.
- **A consented audit sample.** Invite a sample of applicants or users to share sensitive traits on a voluntary basis, for the stated purpose of fairness testing. Store this data separately, limit who can see it, and never feed it to the model. Under the DPDP Act, whose main duties apply from May 2027, personal data needs a clear purpose and, in most cases, consent.
- **Outcome review by region and language.** Even without personal traits, you usually know state, PIN code area and interface language. Splitting results on these often reveals the same gaps.

Be careful with one tempting shortcut: guessing each person's caste or religion from their surname to label your data. The guesses are often wrong, and you have now created sensitive data about people who never agreed to it.

### What can be done

1. **Test by group before launch**, using the numbers above, including combined groups.
2. **Fix the data.** Add examples from under-represented groups, rebalance, and question the labels. "Was hired" is not the same as "would do the job well".
3. **Remove or coarsen proxies**, then re-test, because new proxies can take their place.
4. **Change the product.** Make the model suggest rather than decide. A trained person reviews rejections and can overturn the model.
5. **Explain and allow appeal.** People affected should get the main reasons and a clear way to challenge a decision.
6. **Monitor after launch.** Repeat the group tests on a schedule and after any model, data or policy change.

```flow
dir: LR
title: The fairness loop
Build -> Test by group -> Find the cause -> Fix data, features or process -> Test again
Test again -[repeat after launch and every change]-> Test by group
```

### Fairness has trade-offs

There is more than one definition of fairness, and they can conflict:

| Definition | Means | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equal approval rates | Each group approved at the same rate | May approve people likely to struggle with repayment |
| Equal error rates | The model wrongly rejects good applicants equally often in each group | Approval rates may still differ |
| Same treatment for similar people | Two people with the same record get the same result | Needs agreement on what "similar" means |

Here is why the first two clash. Suppose that out of 100 applicants in Group A, 80 would repay, and in Group B, 60 would. (These numbers are made up to show the idea.) A perfect model approves exactly the 80 and the 60. It makes no errors in either group, so error rates are equal, but approval rates are 80% and 60%. Now force both groups to a 70% approval rate. In Group A you must reject 10 people who would have repaid. In Group B you must approve 10 people who will not. Equal approval rates have created unequal errors. Researchers have proved related results: when base rates differ, a risk score cannot both mean the same repayment chance in each group and have equal error rates in each group, unless it predicts perfectly.

So choosing a definition is a value decision. Make it openly, with the people affected, the law and the purpose of the system in mind, and write down why. Do not leave it to whoever trains the model. A different base rate can itself be a product of past unfairness, which is one more reason to question the labels.

### What the law says

As of November 2026, India has no law written specifically about algorithmic bias, and no general legal duty to test models for it. Existing laws still apply to what a model does. For example, the Code on Wages, 2019 bars employers from discriminating on gender in recruitment for the same or similar work. Two guidance documents name fairness as a core principle: India's AI Governance Guidelines from November 2025, and the Reserve Bank of India's FREE-AI committee report from August 2025 for the financial sector. Both are guidance, not binding rules with penalties.

The EU AI Act treats AI used in hiring and in credit scoring of people as **high-risk**. That means duties on data quality, human oversight, records and transparency. In May 2026, EU lawmakers agreed to move these high-risk duties to December 2027, and the change was formally adopted in June 2026. They will apply to Indian firms whose tools are used in the EU.

## A worked example

A Bengaluru startup builds a resume-screening tool for a large IT client. It trains the model on five years of the client's hiring decisions: which resumes were shortlisted and which were not. The label is "was shortlisted", so the model learns to copy past recruiters.

**Testing by group.** Before launch, the team runs the tool on 2,400 past applicants for engineering roles who all met the job's minimum requirements. The tool shortlists 480 of them (20%). The table cuts the **same 2,400 people** two ways, by college and by gender, plus one combined group. The figures are illustrative, made up for this lesson.

| Group | Applicants | Shortlisted before | Shortlisted after |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Five well-known colleges | 400 | 180 (45%) | 92 (23%) |
| Other colleges | 2,000 | 300 (15%) | 388 (19.4%) |
| Men | 1,600 | 384 (24%) | 328 (20.5%) |
| Women | 800 | 96 (12%) | 152 (19%) |
| Women from other colleges | 680 | 56 (8.2%) | 120 (17.6%) |
| Men from five colleges | 280 | 140 (50%) | 60 (21.4%) |

**Computing the ratios before the fix.** Other colleges against the five: 15 ÷ 45 = 0.33. Women against men: 12 ÷ 24 = 0.50. Women from other colleges against men from the five colleges: 8.2 ÷ 50 = 0.16. All are far below 0.8, and the combined group is hit hardest.

**Finding the cause.** The team runs a paired test. It takes 200 real resumes and makes two copies of each that differ only in gender signals: a man's or a woman's name, and one line, "Captain, college cricket team" in one copy and "Captain, women's cricket team" in the other. The women's versions score lower in most pairs. Separately, a small model predicts gender from the rest of the resume far better than chance. College names, gendered words and some hobbies were acting as proxies.

**The fix.** The team removes names, photos, college names and gendered terms from what the model scores. It rebalances the training examples and adds a skills test score as an input. Then it tests again by group, with the shortlist size held at 480 so the comparison is fair.

**Computing the ratios after the fix.** Other colleges against the five: 19.4 ÷ 23 = 0.84. Women against men: 19 ÷ 20.5 = 0.93. Across all combined groups, the highest rate is now women from the five colleges (32 of 120, 26.7%), so lowest against highest is 17.6 ÷ 26.7 = 0.66. The single gaps narrowed sharply, but the combined view is still below 0.8, so the team checks whether that gap matches skills test scores and records the judgement.

**Changing the product, not just the model.** The tool now suggests a shortlist with reasons instead of rejecting people automatically. Recruiters review a sample of rejections each week and can overturn any of them. Candidates can ask for a human review. The team repeats the group and paired tests every quarter, and after every model update, and reports the results to the client.

The model got fairer, but the bigger change was in how it was used.

## Common mistakes

- **Looking only at overall accuracy.** A system can be accurate on average and unfair to a group. Always break results down, including combined groups.
- **Thinking removing a sensitive field removes bias.** Proxies like surname, PIN code, college and wording remain. Test whether they predict the trait.
- **Treating the four-fifths rule as a pass mark.** A ratio of 0.85 does not prove fairness, and 0.75 does not prove discrimination. It tells you where to look.
- **Trusting tiny groups.** A rate based on 20 people can swing by five points per person. Collect more data or review cases by hand.
- **Guessing caste or religion from surnames to label data.** It is often wrong and creates sensitive data without consent. Use paired tests or a consented audit sample instead.
- **Assuming AI is neutral because it is maths.** It reflects the labels and decisions it learned from.
- **Testing only in English.** Indian users speak many languages; test in the ones your users actually use.
- **Human review that is only a rubber stamp.** If reviewers approve almost everything the model says, people are not really in charge. Give them time, reasons and the power to overrule.
- **Testing once and forgetting.** Bias can appear after launch as data, users and policies change.

## Practice

Read Chapter 6 (Fairness) of the free Ethics of AI course, linked below, and the EU AI Act summary's section on high-risk uses.

1. **Run a paired test on a chatbot.** Ask the same question, for example "Suggest three careers for Arjun, a 17-year-old from Patna", five times, changing only the surname to ones linked with different communities. Then ask it once in Hindi or another Indian language you know. Note differences in the careers suggested, tone and length.
2. **Test an image generator.** Ask for "a doctor", "a nurse", "an engineer" and "a farmer" ten times each. Count the genders and appearances you get, and work out the share for each.
3. **Compute a ratio.** A college placement cell's AI tool shortlists 40% of 500 students from one campus and 26% of 300 from another, with similar marks. Work out the selection-rate ratio, say whether it passes the 0.8 screen, and name two things you would check next.
4. **Plan a bias test.** For one AI use in hiring or lending, list three ways bias could enter, one test for each, and how you would test it if you held no caste or religion data.

**You are done when you can** explain proxies with an Indian example, compute and interpret a selection-rate ratio, and describe a paired test for a system where you hold no sensitive data.
