## What it is

A **workplace AI policy** tells employees how they may use AI tools at work. It exists because AI assistants are easy to use and easy to misuse: one pasted spreadsheet of customer phone numbers can break the law, a client contract and your company's trust in one move.

Good policies are short and practical. They usually cover seven things:

| Section | What it says | Example rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Approved tools** | Which assistants and features are allowed | "Use the company-licensed assistant; personal accounts are not allowed for work" |
| **Data rules** | What may and may not go into AI tools | "No customer personal data outside the CRM's built-in AI" |
| **Use cases** | Where AI is encouraged and where it is restricted | "Encouraged for drafting and summarising; not for final hiring decisions" |
| **Review and accuracy** | Who checks AI output, and who is responsible | "You check every figure; you own what you send" |
| **Disclosure** | When to tell people AI was used | "Tell clients when a report is substantially AI-drafted" |
| **Intellectual property** | Who owns AI-assisted work, and avoiding copying | "Do not paste others' copyrighted work to rewrite it" |
| **Reporting** | How to report a mistake or a problem | "Email the AI committee within 24 hours" |

This lesson follows Karthik, who works in sales at a manufacturer in Chennai, as he uses AI to prepare a proposal within his company's rules. By the end you will know what a good policy covers, how to sort your data, and what to do when your company has no policy at all.

## How it works

### Why companies care

When you type into a consumer AI tool, the text may be stored and, depending on the settings and terms, used to improve the service. That creates three risks:

1. **Legal.** Putting customer personal data into an unapproved tool may break India's DPDP Act, which limits how personal data is used and shared.
2. **Contractual.** Client contracts often forbid sharing their information with third parties.
3. **Confidentiality.** Strategy, unreleased financial results and source code can leak.

Company-licensed enterprise versions usually come with commitments: your data is not used for training, it is stored in a known place, and access is controlled.

```text
   Personal free account               Company-licensed tool
 +-----------------------------+     +-----------------------------+
 | Terms you clicked through   |     | Contract signed by company  |
 | Data may help train models  |     | Data not used for training  |
 | No admin control            |     | Admin controls, audit logs  |
 | Company cannot help if      |     | Security team knows where   |
 | something leaks             |     | data goes                   |
 +-----------------------------+     +-----------------------------+
          not for work data                  for approved work
```

### Sort your data into four categories

A simple way to decide what can go where:

| Category | Examples | Rule of thumb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Public** | Published brochures, the company website, public reports | Usually fine in any tool |
| **Internal** | Everyday internal documents, process notes, templates | Approved tools only |
| **Confidential** | Client contracts, unreleased results, strategy, pricing | Approved tools only, sometimes not at all |
| **Personal and sensitive** | Customer or employee names with contact details, health, salaries, Aadhaar or PAN numbers, bank details | Strictest rules; often only in specific approved systems |

When in doubt, move a document one category stricter.

### Remove what you do not need

Often you can do the task without the sensitive part. Replace names with "Customer A", remove phone numbers and account numbers, round financial figures, and keep only the structure you need. The assistant writes just as well about "a mid-size client in Coimbatore" as about the real company name.

### Accountability

Your name is on your work. If a client report has a wrong figure, "the AI wrote it" is not an excuse. Policies usually say this plainly, and managers and clients assume it anyway.

### When there is no policy

Many smaller companies have no AI policy yet. That does not mean there are no rules: laws and contracts still apply. Until there is a policy:

1. Ask your manager or IT team what is allowed.
2. Use only public material or your own non-confidential notes.
3. Remove names, numbers and identifiers.
4. Never paste personal data.
5. Check everything before you use it.
6. Tell your manager how you are using AI.

## A worked example

Karthik works in sales at a mid-sized machine-tools manufacturer in Chennai. He wants to use an AI assistant to prepare a proposal for a large automotive client.

His company's one-page policy says:

- The company-licensed assistant is approved for internal and confidential information. Personal free accounts are not allowed for work.
- Customer personal data, such as names and phone numbers, must not be entered except in the CRM's built-in AI features.
- Pricing must be checked by the finance team before sending.
- AI-assisted client documents must be reviewed by the account manager.

Here is what Karthik does:

| Step | What he did | Which rule it follows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gather material | The client's published requirements and the company's standard proposal sections | Internal and public data only |
| Clean his notes | Removed the client contact's name and phone number before pasting | No personal data outside the CRM |
| Draft | Used the licensed assistant to draft the proposal and a pricing table | Approved tool |
| Check pricing | Sent the table to finance, who corrected a discount calculated on the wrong base price | Finance reviews pricing |
| Review | His manager reviewed the final proposal | Account manager review |

The wrong discount would have cost the company about ₹4 lakh. The review step, not the AI, caught it.

A colleague suggests using a free personal account "because it's faster". Karthik explains the policy and shows him how to open the licensed tool. Policies only work when colleagues remind each other.

## Common mistakes

- **Using personal accounts for work data.** Even if it feels harmless, it puts the data outside your company's control.
- **Pasting customer personal data into general assistants.** Use the specific approved system, or remove the personal details.
- **Assuming "no policy" means "no rules".** Data protection law, contracts and confidentiality still apply.
- **Hiding AI use from your manager.** Transparency protects you if something goes wrong.
- **Skipping review because the AI is "usually right".** Usually right is not good enough for pricing, contracts or customer data.
- **Treating the policy as a one-time read.** Policies change as tools change; check for updates.

## Practise

Take the AI Fluency course's section on responsible use, linked below, and read PRS's summary of India's DPDP law.

1. **Find your policy.** Find your organisation's or college's AI policy. If none exists, write down five questions you would ask your manager.
2. **Classify ten documents.** List ten documents you use often and classify each as public, internal, confidential or personal. Note which tools you may use for each.
3. **Clean and do.** Take one real task, remove all personal and confidential details, and do it with an approved tool.

**You are done when you can** explain to a colleague which tools and which data are allowed for AI at your workplace, and what to do when you are unsure.
