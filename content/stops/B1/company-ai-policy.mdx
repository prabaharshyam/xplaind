## What it is

A **workplace AI policy** tells employees how they may use AI tools at work. It exists because AI assistants are easy to use and easy to misuse: one pasted spreadsheet of customer phone numbers can break a client contract, your company's confidentiality duties and, as India's data law comes fully into force, the law.

Good policies are short and practical. They usually cover seven things:

| Section | What it says | Example rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Approved tools** | Which assistants, features, extensions and notetakers are allowed | "Use the company-licensed assistant; personal accounts are not allowed for work" |
| **Data rules** | What may and may not go into each tool | "No customer personal data outside the CRM's built-in AI" |
| **Use cases** | Where AI is encouraged and where it is restricted | "Encouraged for drafting and summarising; not for hiring decisions" |
| **Review and accuracy** | Who checks AI output, and who is responsible | "You check every figure; you own what you send" |
| **Disclosure** | When to tell people AI was used | "Tell clients when a report is substantially AI-drafted" |
| **Intellectual property** | Who owns AI-assisted work, and avoiding copying | "Do not paste others' copyrighted work to rewrite it" |
| **Reporting** | How to report a mistake or a problem, and how fast | "Tell IT security within 1 hour; honest reports are not punished" |

As an employee, you do not need to memorise the policy. You need to answer three questions before each task, and know where in the policy each answer lives.

```flow
title: Three questions before you use AI on a work task
A work task -> Is this tool and this feature approved?
Is this tool and this feature approved? -[no]-> Stop and ask IT | or use public material only
Is this tool and this feature approved? -[yes]-> Is this data allowed in it?
Is this data allowed in it? -[no]-> Remove it, or use the named system
Is this data allowed in it? -[yes]-> Does the output need review or disclosure?
Remove it, or use the named system -> Does the output need review or disclosure?
Does the output need review or disclosure? -> Check, get it reviewed, then send
```

This lesson follows Karthik, who works in sales at a manufacturer in Chennai, as he prepares a proposal within his company's rules. By the end you will know what a good policy covers, how to sort your data, how to handle AI that is built into your everyday tools, what to do after a mistake, and what to do when your company has no policy at all.

## How it works

### Why companies care

When you type into a consumer AI tool, the text may be stored and, depending on the settings and the vendor's terms, used to improve the service. That creates three kinds of risk:

1. **Legal.** India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) limits how personal data is used and shared. Its Rules were notified in November 2025, and most duties for businesses apply from May 2027. Until then, the IT Act's 2011 rules on sensitive personal data, such as financial details, passwords and health records, still apply.
2. **Contractual.** Client contracts often forbid sharing their information with third parties, or allow it only in named countries. This applies today.
3. **Confidentiality.** Strategy, unreleased financial results and source code can leak. In 2023, Samsung restricted staff use of public chatbots after engineers pasted internal source code into one.

If you work in a bank, NBFC, insurer or hospital, your regulator adds rules on top, and your compliance team knows them. For example, the RBI's FREE-AI committee report (August 2025) recommends that regulated firms have a board-approved AI policy. It is advice rather than a binding rule, but it shows where the sector is heading.

A company licence changes who sets the rules:

| | Personal free account | Company-licensed tool |
|---|---|---|
| Terms | You clicked through them | A contract signed by the company |
| Training on your data | Depends on your settings and the vendor's terms | Set by the company's contract; ask IT what it says |
| Control | No admin control | Single sign-on, admin settings and audit logs |
| If something leaks | The company cannot see or fix it | The security team knows where the data went |
| Use it for | Public material at most | Approved work, within the data rules |

A licence is not a blank cheque. It covers the tool and settings the company approved, for the data the policy names.

### Sort your data into four categories

| Category | Examples | Rule of thumb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Public** | Published brochures, the company website, public reports | Usually fine in any tool |
| **Internal** | Process notes, templates, internal announcements | Approved tools only |
| **Confidential** | Client contracts, unreleased results, strategy, draft prices | Approved tools only, sometimes not at all |
| **Personal and sensitive** | Names with phone numbers or emails, salaries, health, Aadhaar or PAN numbers, bank details | Strictest; often only in one named system |

Three items that people often sort wrongly:

- **A draft price list for a client** is confidential, not public. It becomes public only if and when it is published, and a client quote usually never is.
- **A screenshot of your CRM or inbox** is personal data if names, numbers or emails are visible, whatever the screenshot was meant to show.
- **Your own meeting notes** take the category of the most sensitive thing in them. Notes from a client call are usually confidential, and personal if they hold someone's contact details.

When in doubt, move a document one category stricter.

### AI you did not open on purpose

Many leaks come not from opening a chatbot but from AI built into tools people already use. Check your policy for each of these; if it is silent, ask IT before switching one on.

| Feature | What it can see | Sensible default |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **AI in office suites and email**, such as "summarise this thread" or "draft a reply" | The documents, sheets and mail it is pointed at, sometimes your whole drive | Use only the version your company licensed and switched on, and only within the data rules |
| **Browser extensions** that rewrite, summarise or translate | Often every page you open, including the CRM, HR portal and webmail | Install only extensions IT has approved; remove the rest |
| **AI meeting notetakers** | Everything said on the call, by everyone, plus names and screens shared | Tell everyone at the start and let anyone object; for external calls, ask the other side first |
| **Coding assistants** | Open files, often the whole repository, and sometimes configuration files with keys | Only the approved assistant, only on code your client allows; keep passwords and keys out of files it reads |

Notetakers need special care because they record other people, not just you. Recording rules differ by country. In California, for example, recording a confidential conversation needs the consent of everyone in it, so a client call with a US team can raise a legal question, not just a polite one. Many client contracts also forbid recording. Treat a transcript like the meeting itself: it has the same category and the same rules.

### Remove what you do not need

Often you can do the task without the sensitive part. Replace names with roles ("the client's purchase head"), remove phone numbers and account numbers, round figures that do not matter, and keep only what the task needs. The assistant writes just as well about "a large automotive client" as about the real company. The worked example shows a full before and after.

### Review, disclosure and accountability

Your name is on your work. If a client report has a wrong figure, "the AI wrote it" is not an excuse. Policies usually say this plainly, and managers and clients assume it anyway. Two habits follow:

- **Check what matters most first:** prices, dates, names, legal terms and anything you are promising.
- **Disclose when the policy or the reader expects it.** Some clients ask to be told when a deliverable is largely AI-drafted. Telling your manager how you use AI also protects you if something goes wrong.

### If you paste something by mistake

Everyone makes this mistake once: the wrong file, the wrong tab, a personal account still signed in. What matters is the next hour.

1. **Stop.** Do not send more, and do not ask the chatbot to "forget" it. It cannot undo what is already stored.
2. **Note the facts:** which tool and account, the time, and exactly what went in.
3. **Report it at once** to IT or security, through the channel in your policy. Your company may have to report some incidents to CERT-In, India's cyber security agency, within 6 hours of noticing, so a delay of a day can turn a small problem into a breach of that duty.
4. **Delete the chat** after you have reported it, or as security tells you. Deletion helps, but the service may still keep a copy for a period under its terms, so deleting is not the same as fixing.
5. **Change any password, key or access token** you pasted, the same day.

Good policies say that honest, quick reports are not punished. Hiding a mistake is what turns it into a disciplinary matter.

### When there is no policy

Many smaller companies have no AI policy yet. That does not mean there are no rules: laws and contracts still apply. Until there is one:

1. Ask your manager or IT team which tools are allowed.
2. Use only public material or your own non-confidential notes.
3. Remove names, numbers and identifiers.
4. Never paste personal data, passwords or keys.
5. Check everything before you use it.
6. Tell your manager how you are using AI.

Better still, offer a one-page draft. Here is a template to adapt. It is a starting point, not legal advice; leave the final rules to whoever owns IT and legal.

```text
[Company] AI use policy (draft, version 1, [date])

1. Approved tools: [licensed assistant], with [settings]. No personal
   AI accounts for work. Ask IT before switching on any other AI
   feature, browser extension or notetaker.
2. Data: public and internal data in approved tools. Confidential
   client data only where the client contract allows it. Personal
   data only in [named systems]. Never passwords or keys.
3. Encouraged: drafting, summarising, research, formatting.
4. Needs approval: anything customer-facing, decisions about people
   (hiring, appraisal, credit), recording external calls.
5. Review: you check every fact and figure; [named role] reviews
   prices and anything sent to a client.
6. Disclosure: tell clients when a deliverable is largely AI-drafted,
   or when their contract asks.
7. Ownership: do not paste others' copyrighted work to rewrite it.
8. Mistakes: report to [contact] within 1 hour. Honest reports are
   not punished.
9. Owner: [name]. Reviewed every 6 months.
```

## A worked example

This is an illustrative case. The company, the names and the numbers are made up to show the method.

Karthik works in sales at a mid-sized machine-tools manufacturer in Chennai. He wants to use AI to prepare a proposal for a large automotive client that needs five CNC machines for a new plant.

His company's one-page policy says:

- The company-licensed assistant is approved for internal and confidential information. Personal free accounts are not allowed for work.
- Customer personal data, such as names and phone numbers, goes only into the CRM and its built-in AI features.
- The video-call tool's AI notetaker is approved for internal calls. For external calls, ask the other side first.
- Pricing must be checked by the finance team, and client documents reviewed by the account manager, before sending.

**Step 1: Clean the notes.** His notes from the first call read:

```text
Before: Spoke to Ramesh Natarajan (purchase head, 98765 43210) at
        Zentora Motors. Need 5 CNC machines for the new plant by March.
        Budget about Rs 2.6 crore. Unhappy with current vendor's
        service: engineers take 3 to 4 days to arrive.

After:  The client's purchase head needs 5 CNC machines for a new
        plant by March. Budget about Rs 2.6 crore. Unhappy with the
        current vendor's service: engineers take 3 to 4 days to arrive.
```

The name and phone number are personal data, so they stay in the CRM. The budget and the client's needs are confidential, which the licensed assistant is approved for. The client's name adds nothing to the draft, so it goes too.

**Step 2: Prompt with limits.** In the licensed assistant he writes:

```text
You are helping me draft a sales proposal for CNC machines. Using the
notes and our standard proposal sections below, draft three sections:
the client's needs, our proposed solution, and our service plan.
Formal Indian business English, under 600 words. Do not invent prices,
delivery dates or specifications. Write [CHECK] wherever a figure is
needed. Then build a pricing table from the price list below.
```

The "do not invent" line matters. Without it, assistants often fill gaps with plausible figures that nobody asked for.

**Step 3: Review catches the error.** The quote has two parts: five machines worth ₹2 crore, and ₹50 lakh of spares and installation, which the company never discounts. The assistant applied the standard 8% discount to the full ₹2.5 crore. Finance spotted it: 8% of ₹2 crore is ₹16 lakh, but the draft showed ₹20 lakh. The mistake would have given away ₹4 lakh. The review step, not the AI, caught it.

**Step 4: A notetaker question.** On the follow-up video call, the meeting tool offers to switch on its AI notetaker. It is an external call, so Karthik asks at the start. The purchase head says no: his own company forbids recording supplier calls. Karthik takes notes by hand and adds the contact details to the CRM afterwards.

A colleague later suggests a free personal account "because it's faster". Karthik shows him how to open the licensed tool, which takes less than a minute. Policies work when colleagues remind each other.

## Common mistakes

- **Using personal accounts for work data.** Even if it feels harmless, it puts the data outside your company's control.
- **Assuming an approved tool means every feature is approved.** Connectors, extensions, notetakers and agent modes can each send data somewhere new. Check each one.
- **Pasting customer personal data into general assistants.** Use the named system, or remove the personal details first.
- **Switching on a notetaker without asking.** It records everyone on the call, and the other side may not allow it.
- **Hiding a mistaken paste, or only deleting the chat.** Report it at once; deleting does not undo it, and delay can break a 6-hour reporting duty.
- **Assuming "no policy" means "no rules".** Data protection law, contracts and confidentiality still apply.
- **Skipping review because the AI is "usually right".** Usually right is not good enough for pricing, contracts or customer data.
- **Treating the policy as a one-time read.** Policies change as tools change; check for updates.

## Practice

Take the AI Fluency course's section on responsible use, linked below, and read PRS's summary of India's DPDP law. It covers the Act as passed in 2023; the Rules came in November 2025.

1. **Find your policy.** Find your organisation's or college's AI policy and answer the three questions from the top of this lesson for one task you did this week. If no policy exists, write down five questions you would ask your manager.
2. **Classify ten documents.** List ten documents you use often and classify each as public, internal, confidential or personal. Note which tools you may use for each.
3. **Audit your hidden AI.** List every AI feature, browser extension and notetaker on your work laptop and phone, and mark each as approved, unapproved or unknown.
4. **Clean and do.** Take one real task, remove all personal and confidential details as Karthik did, and do it with an approved tool.

**You are done when you can** explain to a colleague which tools, features and data are allowed for AI at your workplace, and what to do in the first hour after a mistaken paste.
