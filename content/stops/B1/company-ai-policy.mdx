## What it is

A **workplace AI policy** tells employees how they may use AI tools. Good ones are short and practical. They usually cover:

- **Approved tools**: which assistants and features are allowed, often a company-licensed version that does not use your data for training.
- **Data rules**: what you may and may not put into AI tools (customer data, personal data, confidential plans, source code, financials).
- **Use cases**: where AI is encouraged (drafting, summarising, research) and where it is restricted (hiring decisions, medical or legal advice, final decisions about customers).
- **Review and accuracy**: you check AI output before using it, and you are responsible for it.
- **Disclosure**: when to tell people AI was used (customers, clients, reports).
- **Intellectual property**: who owns AI-assisted work, and avoiding copying others' work.
- **Reporting**: how to report a problem or a mistake.

## How it works

**Why companies care.** Text typed into a consumer AI tool may be stored and, depending on settings and terms, used to improve the service. Putting customer details into such a tool may break India's DPDP Act, contracts with clients, or confidentiality rules. Approved enterprise versions usually come with commitments on storage, training and location.

**Data categories.** A simple way to think:

- *Public*: published information. Usually fine.
- *Internal*: everyday internal documents. Approved tools only.
- *Confidential*: client contracts, financial results before release, strategy. Approved tools only, sometimes not at all.
- *Personal and sensitive*: customer or employee personal data, health, financial details, IDs. Strictest rules; often not allowed in AI tools unless a specific system is approved.

**Accountability.** Your name is on your work. "The AI wrote it" does not excuse a wrong figure in a client report.

**When there is no policy.** Many smaller companies have none yet. Ask your manager or IT team. Meanwhile, act conservatively: use only public or your own non-confidential material, remove names and numbers, avoid personal data entirely, check everything, and tell your manager how you are using AI.

## A worked example

Karthik works in sales at a mid-sized Chennai manufacturer. He wants to use an AI assistant to prepare a proposal for a big client.

His company's policy, one page on the intranet, says:

- The company's licensed assistant is approved for internal and confidential information; personal free accounts are not.
- Customer personal data (names, phone numbers) must not be entered except in the CRM's built-in AI features.
- Pricing must be checked by the finance team before sending.
- AI-assisted client documents must be reviewed by the account manager.

Karthik uses the licensed assistant with the client's published requirements and the company's standard proposal sections. He removes the client contact's name and phone number from his notes before pasting them. He sends the draft pricing table to finance, who correct a discount that the AI had calculated on the wrong base. His manager reviews the final proposal.

A colleague suggests using a free personal account "because it's faster". Karthik explains the policy and points him to the licensed tool.

## Common mistakes

- **Using personal accounts for work data.**
- **Pasting customer personal data into general assistants.**
- **Assuming "no policy" means "no rules".** Laws and contracts still apply.
- **Hiding AI use from your manager.** Transparency protects you.
- **Skipping review because the AI "is usually right".**

## Practise

Take the AI Fluency course's section on responsible use, and read PRS's summary of India's DPDP law.

1. Find your organisation's AI policy (or your college's). If none exists, write down the questions you would ask your manager.
2. Classify ten documents you use often as public, internal, confidential or personal, and note which tools you may use for each.
3. Take one real task, remove all personal and confidential details, and do it with an approved tool.

**You are done when you can** explain to a colleague which tools and which data are allowed for AI at your workplace, and what to do when unsure.
