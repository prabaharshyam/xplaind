## What it is

AI is not a lawless zone. When an app uses AI to decide your loan, read your messages or generate your face, several sets of rules already apply. Three matter most for people in India:

| Rule | What it is | Who it affects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act)** | India's law on digital personal data, with rules notified in November 2025 and phased in over about eighteen months | Any organisation handling digital personal data of people in India, including in AI systems |
| **India's AI Governance Guidelines** | Principles for safe and trusted AI from the Ministry of Electronics and IT, released in November 2025 | Developers, deployers and regulators; mostly voluntary, relying on existing laws |
| **The EU AI Act** | The first broad AI law, sorting AI uses by risk | Companies whose AI is used in the European Union, including Indian firms |

Other existing laws still apply to AI: the Information Technology Act, consumer protection, criminal law for fraud and impersonation, and sector rules for banking, insurance and health. A bank's AI is watched by the RBI just as its other systems are.

```text
           Rules that touch an AI app in India
 +----------------------------------------------------+
 |  Sector rules (RBI for banks, IRDAI for insurance) |
 |  +----------------------------------------------+  |
 |  |  General laws (IT Act, consumer, criminal)   |  |
 |  |  +----------------------------------------+  |  |
 |  |  |  DPDP Act: your personal data          |  |  |
 |  |  +----------------------------------------+  |  |
 |  +----------------------------------------------+  |
 |  AI Governance Guidelines: principles on top       |
 +----------------------------------------------------+
   + EU AI Act if the product is used in Europe
```

This lesson follows Deepa, a teacher in Jaipur, as she applies for a loan through an app and uses her rights. By the end you will be able to explain, in plain words, what these rules mean for an ordinary user.

## How it works

### The DPDP Act in plain words

The DPDP Act calls the organisation that decides how your data is used a **data fiduciary**, and you, the person, a **data principal**. The key duties and rights:

- **Notice and consent.** Organisations must tell you clearly what personal data they collect and why, and generally get your consent for it.
- **Withdraw any time.** You can withdraw consent as easily as you gave it.
- **See, correct, erase.** You can ask for a summary of your data, correct it, and have it erased when it is no longer needed.
- **Security and breaches.** Organisations must protect your data, and tell you and the Data Protection Board of India about a breach.
- **Children.** Children's data needs verifiable consent from a parent, and children may not be tracked or targeted with advertising.
- **Penalties.** Large penalties apply, up to ₹250 crore for failing to take reasonable security safeguards.
- **Complaints.** You complain first to the organisation's grievance officer, then to the Data Protection Board.

```text
 You (data principal)            Organisation (data fiduciary)
 +------------------+   consent  +--------------------------+
 |  gives data      | ---------> |  uses it only as told    |
 |  can withdraw    | ---------> |  stops, erases           |
 |  can ask to see  | ---------> |  shows a summary         |
 |  can complain    | ---------> |  grievance officer       |
 +------------------+            +--------------------------+
          |  not resolved?
          v
   Data Protection Board of India
```

### India's AI Governance Guidelines

The guidelines put people first and promote trust, fairness, accountability, transparency and safety. They recommend new institutions to coordinate AI policy and voluntary measures for industry, such as transparency reports and ways for people to raise grievances. Importantly, they do **not** create a separate AI law. Instead, they rely on existing laws and on sector regulators to manage risk while encouraging innovation.

### The EU AI Act

The EU AI Act sorts AI uses into risk levels:

| Risk level | Examples | What is required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unacceptable | Social scoring by public authorities, manipulating people in harmful ways | Banned |
| High | AI in hiring, credit scoring, education, essential services, some medical uses | Risk management, good data, human oversight, records, transparency |
| Limited | Chatbots, AI-generated content | Tell people they are dealing with AI; label some generated content |
| Minimal | Spam filters, AI in games | No new duties |

Indian companies whose AI products are used in Europe must follow it, and its approach is shaping rules and customer expectations elsewhere.

### What it means for you

- Read what an app says it does with your data before you agree.
- Turn off permissions an app does not need.
- Use your rights to see, correct and delete your data.
- Expect to be told when you are dealing with AI in many situations.
- If an AI decision hurts you, such as a rejected loan or job application, ask how it was made and how to appeal.

## A worked example

Deepa, a school teacher in Jaipur, needs a ₹60,000 personal loan for her mother's surgery. She downloads a lending app.

**Permissions.** The app asks for access to her contacts, photos and SMS. She reads the notice: contacts are used "to improve services". That is vague and not needed for a loan, so she declines. The app still processes her application, which shows the permission was never essential.

**A fast rejection.** Two minutes later she is rejected, with no reason. She suspects an automated decision. She writes to the lender's grievance officer, listed in the app: "Please explain the main reasons for the decision on my application and arrange a review by a person." Within a week the lender replies with the main reasons, an old unpaid credit card dues entry, and a person reviews her file after she shows it was paid. The loan is approved.

**Leaving.** A month later she has repaid and stops using the app. She withdraws consent and asks for her data to be erased. The company confirms deletion by email, which she saves.

**Helping a friend.** When her friend's data appears in a leak from a different app, Deepa helps her check what the company has disclosed, change her passwords, and note how to complain to the Data Protection Board if the company does not respond.

| Situation | Right or rule Deepa used |
| --- | --- |
| Vague contacts permission | Notice and consent; refuse what is not needed |
| Instant rejection | Ask for reasons and human review through the grievance officer |
| Stopping use | Withdraw consent and request erasure |
| Friend's leak | Breach notice duties; complaint to the Data Protection Board |

## Common mistakes

- **Accepting every permission.** Many apps ask for more than they need. Refuse what is not essential.
- **Thinking AI is unregulated.** Data protection, consumer, criminal and sector laws already apply.
- **Not using your rights.** You can ask to see, correct and delete your data, and you can complain.
- **Assuming EU rules do not matter in India.** Indian products serving Europe must follow them, and they shape global practice.
- **Taking automated rejections as final.** Ask for reasons and a human review.
- **Treating guidelines as law.** India's AI Governance Guidelines are principles; the binding rules come from existing laws and regulators.

## Practise

Read PRS Legislative Research's summary of the DPDP law, linked below, and the EU AI Act's high-level summary.

1. **Read two privacy notices.** Pick two apps you use. Write down what data each collects, why, and how to delete it.
2. **Clean up permissions.** Check one app's permissions on your phone and turn off any it does not need.
3. **Write an erasure request.** In three sentences, write what you would send to a company to withdraw consent and ask for your data to be erased.

**You are done when you can** explain to a friend three rights they have over their personal data, where to complain, and one thing the EU AI Act bans.
