## What it is

AI is not a lawless zone. When an app uses AI to decide your loan, read your phone or generate a fake video of your face, several sets of rules already apply. The hard part is not finding a rule. It is knowing which rules are **binding law today**, which are **law that starts later**, and which are **only advice**. Mixing these up is the most common mistake.

India has no single AI law. AI is governed by laws written for data, the internet and finance, plus voluntary guidance. The European Union passed one broad AI law, which matters here because Indian companies sell AI products in Europe.

| Rule | Kind | Status on 28 November 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act)** and its Rules | Binding law on digital personal data | Rules notified on 13 November 2025; the Data Protection Board provisions apply now, but most duties and rights apply from May 2027 |
| **IT Act, 2000** and its rules: the 2011 rules on sensitive personal data, and the IT Rules amended in February 2026 | Binding law | In force. The 2011 rules apply until the DPDP Act fully replaces them in May 2027; the labelling rules for synthetic content apply since 20 February 2026 |
| **RBI rules for lenders**, such as the digital lending rules and fair practices code | Binding on banks, NBFCs and other RBI-regulated lenders | In force |
| **India's AI Governance Guidelines** (MeitY, 5 November 2025) | Voluntary guidance | Released; no new AI law |
| **RBI FREE-AI report** (13 August 2025) | Non-binding recommendations | Advice for regulated finance |
| **EU AI Act**, amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI | Binding law in the EU, with fines | In force since 1 August 2024, with duties switching on in stages until 2028 |

Consumer protection law and criminal law for fraud and impersonation also apply to AI, as they do to any product.

The rules do not form a ladder, with one on top of another. Each one reaches the same app from a different side:

```flow
title: Rules that reach one AI loan app used in India
DPDP Act -[personal data, from May 2027]-> AI loan app
IT Act rules -[sensitive data and content, today]-> AI loan app
RBI rules -[lending, today]-> AI loan app
AI Governance Guidelines -[advice only]-> AI loan app
EU AI Act -[only if used in the EU]-> AI loan app
```

This lesson follows Deepa, a teacher in Jaipur, as she applies for a loan through an app in November 2026 and uses the rules that really apply to her.

## How it works

### The DPDP Act in plain words

The DPDP Act calls the organisation that decides how your data is used a **data fiduciary**, and you, the person, a **data principal**. From May 2027, these duties and rights apply:

- **Notice and consent.** An organisation must tell you clearly what personal data it collects and why, and get your consent, unless a listed "legitimate use" applies.
- **Legitimate uses.** Section 7 lists a closed set of cases where no consent is needed. Examples are data you gave voluntarily for a stated purpose without objecting, a medical emergency, and some employment purposes. Unlike the EU's GDPR, there is no broad "legitimate interests" ground.
- **Withdraw any time.** You can withdraw consent as easily as you gave it.
- **See, correct, erase.** You can ask for a summary of your data, correct it, and have it erased when it is no longer needed. You can also name a nominee to act for you if you die or cannot act.
- **Security and breaches.** Organisations must protect your data and tell you and the Data Protection Board about a breach.
- **Children.** Anyone under 18 is a child. Their data needs verifiable consent from a parent, and children may not be tracked, monitored for behaviour or targeted with advertising.
- **Big players do more.** The government can name some organisations as **Significant Data Fiduciaries**. They need a data protection officer in India, a yearly impact assessment and audit, and checks that the algorithmic software they use does not put people's rights at risk.
- **Penalties.** Up to ₹250 crore for failing to take reasonable security safeguards, and up to ₹200 crore for failing to report a breach or for breaking the duties on children.
- **Your duties too.** Section 15 asks you not to impersonate anyone, not to hide material facts when giving identity documents, and not to file false or frivolous complaints. Breaking these can cost up to ₹10,000.

One thing the Act does **not** give you: a right to an explanation of an automated decision, or to have a person review it. The EU's GDPR has such a rule (Article 22). You can always ask, and a lender may agree, but asking is advice, not a right.

### What applies before May 2027

Until the DPDP Act's main parts start, the older rules stay in force. Section 43A of the IT Act and its 2011 rules on sensitive personal data cover things such as financial details, passwords and health records. They require a privacy policy, consent for collecting sensitive data, reasonable security and a grievance officer who must resolve complaints within one month. They do not cover ordinary data such as your contacts list, and they are weaker than the DPDP Act. They lapse when the DPDP Act fully applies in May 2027.

The rights flow below shows the DPDP Act route from May 2027:

```flow
title: Using your DPDP rights, from May 2027
dir: LR
You give data -[consent]-> Organisation uses it only as told
You withdraw consent -> Organisation stops and erases
You ask to see your data -> Organisation gives a summary
You complain -> Grievance officer
Grievance officer -[not resolved]-> Data Protection Board of India
```

### Publicly available data and AI training

Section 3(c)(ii) says the Act does not apply to personal data that the person has made, or caused to be made, public, or that someone was legally required to publish. AI companies read this as room to train models on public web pages. Lawyers disagree on how far it reaches. For example, it is unclear whether a photo you posted publicly, then deleted, still counts. Industry bodies have asked the government for a clearer exemption for AI training. Until that comes, treat "it was public" as a weak defence, and record how each data source became public.

### RBI rules for loan apps

For people in India, lending apps are where AI and personal data meet most often. The RBI's digital lending rules, rewritten in May and consolidated in November 2025, bind apps run by or for RBI-regulated lenders:

- **Phone access.** The app must not access your files and media, contacts list, call logs or telephony functions. It may take one-time access to the camera, microphone or location, with your consent, only for onboarding or KYC checks.
- **Consent and deletion.** You can refuse or revoke consent for data use and ask for data already collected to be deleted.
- **Check the app.** Since 1 July 2025 the RBI website lists the apps that regulated lenders report as theirs. An app not on the list, with no named bank or NBFC behind it, is outside these rules. Treat it as unsafe.
- **Reasons for rejection.** The fair practices code for banks requires them to convey the main reasons for rejecting any loan application in writing.
- **Complaints.** Complain to the lender first. If it does not reply within 30 days, or its reply does not resolve the problem, complain to the RBI Ombudsman at cms.rbi.org.in. A new Integrated Ombudsman Scheme applies from 1 July 2026.

The RBI's **FREE-AI** report (13 August 2025) made 26 recommendations on AI in finance, such as a board-approved AI policy. It is advice, not a rule, though it signals what the RBI may require later.

### India's AI Governance Guidelines and synthetic content

MeitY's guidelines, released on 5 November 2025, set seven principles ("sutras"), such as people first, fairness and equity, accountability, and being understandable by design. They recommend an AI Governance Group to coordinate policy, testing by the AI Safety Institute and a national database of AI incidents. They do **not** create an AI law. They rely on existing laws and sector regulators.

The binding part for AI content came separately. The IT Rules amendment notified on 10 February 2026 applies from 20 February 2026. It defines **synthetically generated information** as audio, images or video made or altered by computer so that they look real. Platforms must make sure such content carries a clear label, and must act on lawful takedown orders within three hours. Plain text is not covered. The duty falls on platforms and the tools that create such content, but anyone who shares a deepfake to cheat or defame someone can still be charged under criminal law.

### The EU AI Act

The EU AI Act sorts AI by what it is **used for**, not by which model it uses. The same model can be minimal risk drafting emails and high risk ranking job applicants.

| Risk level | Examples | What is required | Applies from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Banned | Social scoring that leads to unfair treatment; manipulation that exploits vulnerable people | Not allowed | 2 February 2025 |
| High risk | AI in hiring, credit scoring of people, access to education, essential services | Risk management, data governance, logging, documentation, human oversight, accuracy and security | 2 December 2027 (AI inside regulated products such as medical devices: 2 August 2028) |
| Transparency | Chatbots, deepfakes, AI-generated content | Tell people they are dealing with AI; mark generated content | 2 August 2026 |
| Minimal | Spam filters, AI in games | No new duties beyond basic AI literacy for staff | Literacy duty from 2 February 2025 |

Makers of **general-purpose models**, such as large language models, have had their own duties since 2 August 2025: technical documentation, a public summary of training content and a policy to respect EU copyright law.

The high-risk dates moved. The original Act set 2 August 2026 for hiring and credit. The **Digital Omnibus on AI**, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since 27 July 2026, moved it to 2 December 2027. The Omnibus also adds a ban on "nudifier" apps from 2 December 2026.

The Act reaches any company whose AI system is sold in the EU or whose output is used there, wherever the company is based. Fines go up to 35 million euros or 7% of worldwide turnover for banned practices. So an Indian start-up that sells a CV-screening tool to a firm in Germany must meet the high-risk duties by December 2027. Its customers will ask for evidence long before that.

### What it means for you

Decision rules that hold up:

- **Refuse phone permissions** that a service does not need. For a loan app, contacts, photos and call logs are never needed.
- **Check the lender**, not the app. Find the bank or NBFC named in the app and look it up on the RBI's list.
- **Ask in writing**, and keep copies. A written request with a date is what an ombudsman or board will want to see.
- **Know the date.** Before you quote a right, check whether it applies today or from May 2027.
- **Treat guidelines as signals.** They show where rules are heading, but you cannot enforce them.

## A worked example

It is November 2026. Deepa, a school teacher in Jaipur, needs a ₹60,000 personal loan for her mother's surgery. She downloads a loan app. The names and events are illustrative.

**Check the lender.** The app names a private bank as the lender. Deepa finds the app on the RBI's list of lending apps reported by regulated lenders, so the RBI's rules apply.

**Permissions.** The app asks for her contacts, photos and location. The notice says contacts are used "to improve services". Under the RBI's digital lending rules, the app should not read her contacts or photos at all, so she declines both. She allows location once for the address check. The app still processes her application, which shows the extra permissions were never needed.

**A fast rejection.** Two minutes later she is rejected, with no reason. She writes to the bank's grievance officer: "Please give me the main reasons for rejecting my loan application, as the fair practices code requires. If my credit record is the problem, I would also like a person to review my file." The written reasons are her legal due. The review is a request. The bank replies within a week. The main reason is an unpaid credit card entry on her credit report. She shows the card was paid off and asks the credit bureau to correct the entry. The bank chooses to review her file and approves the loan.

If the bank had ignored her for 30 days, her next step would be the RBI Ombudsman, not the Data Protection Board.

**Leaving.** After repaying, Deepa writes to withdraw consent and asks for her data to be deleted, except what the bank must keep by law. She saves the confirmation email. The DPDP erasure right is not in force yet, so her request rests on the RBI rules and the bank's own policy.

**Helping a friend.** Her friend's data appears in a leak from a shopping app. The DPDP duty to tell users about a breach is not yet in force, and a shopping app is not regulated by the RBI. So Deepa helps her change passwords, turn on two-factor login, watch for fake calls, and report any fraud at the cybercrime portal or on the 1930 helpline.

| Situation | Rule Deepa relied on | Status in November 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contacts and photos permission | RBI digital lending rules on phone access | In force for regulated lenders |
| Instant rejection | RBI fair practices code: main reasons in writing | In force for banks |
| Human review of her file | None; it was a request the bank chose to grant | Advice only |
| Stopping use and deletion | RBI lending rules on consent and deletion | In force; DPDP erasure right from May 2027 |
| Friend's leak | DPDP breach notice duty | From May 2027; until then, self-protection and the cybercrime route |

## Common mistakes

- **Quoting DPDP rights as if they apply today.** Most start in May 2027. Until then, the IT Act's older rules and sector rules carry the weight.
- **Believing you have a right to human review.** The DPDP Act and RBI rules give none. Ask anyway, but do not claim it as a right.
- **Treating guidelines as law.** MeitY's AI Governance Guidelines and the RBI's FREE-AI report are advice. The binding rules come from Acts, Rules and RBI directions.
- **Thinking AI is unregulated.** Data, IT, consumer, criminal and lending rules already apply to AI systems.
- **Trusting any loan app.** The RBI's rules bind regulated lenders and their apps. An app with no named, listed lender behind it is outside them.
- **Assuming "public" means free to use.** The DPDP exclusion for public data is narrower and less settled than many AI companies hope.
- **Ignoring the EU because you are in India.** If your product or its output is used in the EU, the EU AI Act applies.
- **Reading the EU delay as relief.** High-risk duties start on 2 December 2027, but customers ask for evidence now.
