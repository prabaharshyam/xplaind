## What it is

**Scope creep** is the gradual growth of what a project must deliver without matching changes to time, budget or other scope. In AI projects it often looks harmless:

- "Can it also handle Tamil documents?"
- "The collections team wants the same thing."
- "Could it make the credit decision, not just the summary?"
- "Let's add WhatsApp as well."

Each request may be reasonable. Together, accepted informally, they push the core deliverable past its date and blur what success means.

```text
   request arrives ("while you're at it...")
          |
          v
   "Let me size it and come back by Thursday"
          |
          v
   size: work | risk | time | cost | what it displaces
          |
          v
   options:  SWAP   add it, remove something similar
             EXTEND add it, move the date or add budget
             LATER  next phase, after the current gate
             NO     conflicts with purpose or rules
          |
          v
   business owner decides --> change request log (who, what, decision, date)
```

This lesson follows week seven of a credit-file pilot at a lender in Chennai, when three requests arrived in one week.

## How it works

**Spot it.** Watch for requests that start "while you're at it", requests from people outside the agreed users, and changes to what "done" means. Check each against the scope of work.

**Never say yes on the spot.** Say: "That's a good idea. Let me size it and come back by Thursday with what it would take and what it would move."

**Size it.** For each request: the work involved, risks (a new language needs new eval data and may need a different model), the effect on the timeline and cost, and what it would displace.

**Offer options, not refusals.** Typically:

1. *Swap*: add it and remove something of similar size.
2. *Extend*: add it and move the date or add budget.
3. *Later*: put it on the list for the next phase, after the current gate.
4. *No*: when it conflicts with the project's purpose or the client's own rules (for example, letting AI make credit decisions without regulatory review).

**Record decisions.** A change request log with the request, who asked, the sizing, the decision, who approved and when. The approver should be the business owner named in the SOW, not whoever asked.

**Keep the relationship.** People ask for more because the work is valuable. Thank them, show you take it seriously, and make the trade-off visible; most clients appreciate being given the choice.

**A change request log:**

| Request | From | Sizing | Recommendation | Decision | Date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Collections correspondence summaries | Collections head | About 4 weeks, new golden set | Later: phase 2 | Accepted by head of operations | Week 7 |
| Tamil salary slips | Regional credit head | About 8 days, Tamil OCR, 50 examples | Swap with PDF export | Accepted | Week 7 |
| Approve or reject recommendations | Senior officer | Regulatory and fairness review needed | No for this project | Accepted | Week 7 |

**Sizing AI work honestly.** For any new language, document type or task, include: collecting and labelling examples for a golden set, checking whether the current model and parsing handle it, running evals and fixing failures, updating guardrails and their tests, and training users. Teams that size only the code change routinely underestimate by half.

**Saying no well.** Thank the person, explain the reason in one sentence tied to the project's purpose or the client's rules, and offer a path: a separate assessment, a later phase, or the right team to ask. People usually accept a clear no with a path; what damages trust is a vague yes that never arrives.

## A worked example

Week 7 of the Chennai lender pilot. In one week, you receive three requests:

1. The collections head asks for summaries of borrower correspondence.
2. A regional credit head asks for Tamil-language salary slips to be supported.
3. A senior officer asks whether the system could recommend approve or reject.

Your change request note to the head of operations:

- *Collections summaries*: new document types, new users, new golden set. About four weeks. **Recommend: later**, as phase 2 after the production gate; it would otherwise move rollout by a month.
- *Tamil salary slips*: 6% of files in two regions; needs Tamil OCR and 50 labelled examples. About eight days. **Recommend: swap** with the planned PDF export feature, which officers rarely use.
- *Approve or reject recommendations*: changes the system from assistance to decision support for credit, with regulatory and fairness implications. **Recommend: no** for this project; if wanted, a separate assessment with the risk and compliance teams.

**What happened next.** The head of operations accepted all three recommendations in one meeting. The collections team got a date for phase 2, which became a second contract. The Tamil swap shipped in week 9 without moving the pilot's end date.

| Request | Recommendation | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Collections summaries | Later, as phase 2 | Dated phase 2, then a second contract |
| Tamil salary slips | Swap with PDF export | Shipped in week 9; end date unchanged |
| Approve or reject recommendations | No for this project | Separate assessment offered |

## Common mistakes

- **Saying yes in the meeting.** Size first, always.
- **Saying no without options.** It sounds unhelpful and pushes people to go around you.
- **Letting the requester approve.** Only the agreed business owner approves changes.
- **Not writing it down.** Unrecorded changes become arguments at acceptance.
- **Ignoring AI-specific costs.** New languages, document types or tasks need new eval data and testing, not just code.

## Practise

Read Atlassian's article on scope creep.

1. For your use case, invent three plausible change requests from different people.
2. Size each (work, risk, timeline and cost effect) and recommend swap, extend, later or no, with one line of reasoning.
3. Write the change request log entries and the short note you would send the business owner.

**You are done when you can** respond to a mid-meeting request with a calm "let me size that" and return with options the client can choose between.
