## What it is

Executives have little time, many priorities and responsibility for money and risk. They read the first lines of what you send and decide whether to read more. Communicating with them well means five habits:

- **Answer first.** The conclusion or the decision you need goes in the first sentence. The US Army's writing guidance asks for this, and it is often called BLUF: bottom line up front.
- **Their language.** Business results (time, cost, revenue, risk, customers), not model names or architectures.
- **Proof.** A number against an agreed baseline, a short demo, a user's words.
- **Trade-offs, honestly.** What it costs compared with what it is worth, what could go wrong, what you recommend.
- **A clear ask.** What you need from them, from whom, by when.

The shape is always the same. What changes is the content, and the content has to survive one test: a finance person will check every number.

This lesson follows the end-of-pilot meeting with the CEO and CFO of a fictional lender in Chennai, the same credit-file assistant used in the other lessons on this path.

## How it works

### The five-part structure

Use one pattern for any update, email or memo:

1. *Headline*: the main point in one sentence, usually a recommendation.
2. *Why it matters*: the business effect, in rupees, hours, customers or risk.
3. *Evidence*: two or three facts, each against a baseline.
4. *Risks or trade-offs*: what might change the picture, and what you will do about it.
5. *Ask*: the decision or help needed, who owns it, and a date.

Put every calculation in an appendix, one line each, so anyone can check it in a minute.

### Use their numbers, and compare value with cost

Use the KPI and baseline agreed in the scope of work. Then turn the result into money with a figure the client owns, such as their HR team's loaded cost of an hour (salary plus benefits and overheads). A figure you invent will be challenged; a figure from their own finance team will not.

Then do the CFO's arithmetic before the CFO does:

- **Same unit, same period.** If value is per year, cost must be per year. "₹50 lakh a year of value" next to "₹9 lakh a month to run" hides the fact that cost is ₹1.08 crore a year, more than double the value.
- **Net value.** Yearly value minus yearly running cost. This is the number that matters.
- **Payback.** If there is a one-off cost from here on (training, rollout visits), divide it by the monthly net value to get the months to pay it back. Money already spent on the pilot is gone either way, so it does not belong in a rollout decision.
- **Break-even.** The point where value equals cost, for example "the case holds as long as each file saves at least 13 minutes". It tells the executive what to watch.
- **Count each benefit once.** Freed officer hours and "no new hiring" are often the same benefit described twice. Pick one.
- **Hours only count if they are used.** Freed time is worth money only if someone has a plan for it. Name the plan and its owner.

A gotcha for India: salaries here are low compared with the dollar prices of many AI services. A case built only on hours saved is often much thinner in India than in the US case studies you read online. Check it early, and look for value in quality, speed and growth as well.

### Translate technical results

| Technical statement | Executive version |
| --- | --- |
| "96.4% accuracy on critical fields" | "About one figure in 28 needs a correction; low-confidence figures are flagged, and officers check every figure against its source before a decision" |
| "p95 latency 2 seconds" | "Nineteen answers in twenty appear within two seconds" |
| "Moved to a smaller model with caching" | "Running cost fell from ₹14 lakh to ₹9 lakh a month with no change in quality" |
| "Recall at 5 of 0.91" | "For nine questions in ten, the right document is among the five it reads" |

Two rules for translating. First, include the consequence and the safeguard, not just a friendlier number: an executive needs to know what a mistake costs and what catches it. Second, do not round a metric into a promise it does not make. "It finds the right document nine times out of ten" sounds like 90% correct answers; recall at 5 only says the right document was among five candidates.

### Be honest about uncertainty

Pilots usually run in the keenest branches with the best-prepared users. Give a range and plan for the cautious end: "The pilot reached 24 minutes per file, but the pilot branches were above average, so we expect 24 to 26 minutes at full rollout and plan for 26." Then show that the case still holds at the planning figure. One optimistic number invites the CFO to find the hole; a range with a break-even point shows you already looked.

### When the news is bad

Executives are told about successes all the time. How you deliver a miss is what they remember. Three rules:

- **First line, not last.** "The pilot missed its target" goes in the headline. Do not open with the good parts to soften it.
- **No surprises.** The executive should hear it from you, before the steering meeting, not from their own staff in it.
- **Options, then a recommendation.** Two or three choices, each with its cost and what it gets, and the one you recommend. A miss with no recommendation hands your problem to the busiest person in the room.

For example, suppose an insurer in Mumbai piloted automatic routing of claim emails with a target of 70% routed without a person:

> **Headline.** The pilot routed 52% of claim emails automatically, short of the 70% target. We recommend narrowing the scope rather than stopping.
>
> **Why.** English emails, about 55% of volume, reached 78%; emails mixing Hindi or Marathi with English reached 21%.
>
> **Options.** (1) Stop now: no further cost. (2) Go live for English emails only: above target for that group, live in four weeks. (3) Extend the pilot eight weeks for mixed-language emails: more cost, uncertain result.
>
> **Ask.** Approve option 2 this week; we will bring a mixed-language plan to the next quarterly review.

Recommending a stop when the numbers say stop is part of the job. It is also what makes your next recommendation believable.

### Demos for executives

Five minutes, one story: a real case, before and after, the user's view, and one moment showing a safeguard. Rehearse it. Have a recorded backup in case the network fails. Never show a demo case you have not tried that morning.

### Before the meeting

- **Send a pre-read.** Send the memo a day or two ahead, so the meeting is spent on the decision, not on reading.
- **Brief their staff.** Walk the CFO's finance analyst through the appendix, and the CEO's chief of staff through the ask, before the meeting. Their questions show you where the gaps are, and an executive trusts numbers their own team has already checked.
- **Prepare the obvious questions.** What does it cost at full scale? What happens when it is wrong? Is our data safe? What do our people think? How does this compare with competitors? Have a one-line answer to each, with detail on request.
- **Prepare the 60-second version.** Say the decision, the main result against the baseline, the net value, and the biggest risk with its mitigation, out loud, in under a minute. If you cannot, the memo is not clear yet. Executives often give you less time than promised.

### Handling tough questions

Answer the question asked in one sentence, then offer detail: "Yes, the data stays in our Azure region in India. I can show the architecture page if useful." If you do not know, say so and give a time when you will reply. Guessing in front of a CFO costs more trust than admitting a gap.

**When the baseline is challenged.** Say how it was measured, on what sample and over what period, and who agreed it in the scope of work. Then offer to re-measure: "If you think the pilot branches were unusual, we can time 50 files in two other branches this week and restate the value." A baseline you are willing to re-check is one they will accept.

**When asked about competitors.** Answer only with what you can source from public information, such as a published case study or annual report. Otherwise say you will find out. Never quote a competitor's results from memory or hearsay; one wrong claim in that room discredits every true one.

## A worked example

At the end of the pilot, the lender's CEO and CFO give you 15 minutes. The pilot facts: 20 officers in two branches for 10 weeks; median time per loan file fell from 41 to 24 minutes; files sent back by the credit committee fell from 7% to 5.1%. The lender handles 6,000 files a month across 180 officers, and running the system at full scale costs about ₹9 lakh a month, all in. The lender's HR team puts an officer hour at about ₹600, loaded. These are the fictional case's figures, used to show the method.

### The first draft, and why it fails

The first draft of the value lines said: "About 1,700 officer hours a month freed, roughly ₹50 lakh a year. Cost about ₹9 lakh a month." The finance analyst, briefed the day before, spotted two problems in a minute. ₹9 lakh a month is ₹1.08 crore a year, so the memo asked for more than twice what it promised to return. And the ₹50 lakh rested on an hourly cost nobody at the lender had agreed. That draft would have failed in the room.

### The revised memo

> **Recommendation: roll out the credit-file assistant to all 180 officers in three monthly waves.**
>
> **Result.** In a 10-week pilot with 20 officers, median time per loan file fell from 41 to 24 minutes, and files sent back by the credit committee fell from 7% to 5.1%. Officers check every figure against its source; credit decisions stay with them.
>
> **Value and cost.** At our planning figure of 26 minutes per file, the time saved is worth about ₹9 lakh a month, which covers the running cost. Fewer sent-back files add about ₹1.4 lakh a month. Net value: about ₹16 lakh a year at the planning figure, about ₹31 lakh if full rollout matches the pilot. The operations head will use the freed time for next year's growth in applications instead of new hiring.
>
> **Risks.** Branches with mostly photographed documents may see smaller gains; mitigated by an image clean-up step and branch visits. The case holds while each file saves at least 13 minutes. We report this monthly and will pause the next wave if it falls below.
>
> **Decision needed today:** approve the rollout budget and name an owner in operations.

### The appendix

| Line | Calculation |
| --- | --- |
| Time saved per file | 41 minus 26 = 15 minutes (planning figure; pilot gave 17) |
| Officer hours freed | 6,000 files × 15 min ÷ 60 = 1,500 hours a month |
| Value of hours | 1,500 × ₹600 = ₹9.0 lakh a month |
| Fewer sent-back files | 6,000 × (7% − 5.1%) = 114 a month × 2 hours each (lender's estimate) × ₹600 = about ₹1.4 lakh a month |
| Running cost | ₹9 lakh a month all in, about ₹150 per file |
| Net value | ₹10.4 lakh − ₹9 lakh = about ₹1.4 lakh a month, ₹16 lakh a year (₹2.6 lakh a month at the pilot's 17 minutes) |
| Break-even | (₹9 lakh − ₹1.4 lakh) ÷ ₹600 = about 1,270 hours, or about 13 minutes per file |

### In the room

The demo shows one real file, the officer's review screen and a flagged low-confidence figure being corrected. The CFO asks why the plan uses 26 minutes when the pilot reached 24; you explain that the pilot branches were above average. She asks for the break-even line to be added to the monthly report, and approves with that condition. The meeting takes ten minutes, because the arguments happened the day before with her analyst.

Notice what made it work. The case is honest that time saved only pays for the running cost, and that the gain comes from quality and growth. The executives approved a modest, well-checked case with a clear gate, which a stage-gate plan is designed for.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with the technology.** Lead with the result and the decision; architecture goes in an appendix, if anywhere.
- **Model metrics without translation.** Turn them into time, money, risk or customers, and include the safeguard.
- **Value and cost in different units.** "Per year" against "per month" hides a losing case. Always show net value.
- **Counting a benefit twice.** Freed hours and avoided hiring are usually the same rupees.
- **Overpromising.** A range, a planning figure at the cautious end and a break-even point beat a single best-case number.
- **Burying bad news.** A miss goes in the first line, with options and a recommendation.
- **Surprises in the room.** Send a pre-read and brief their staff first.
- **No ask.** If you do not request a decision, with an owner and a date, you rarely get one.

Take at least the first unit of Google's free technical writing course, which applies directly to executive writing.

1. Write a one-page recommendation memo for your use case using the five-part structure, with an appendix that has one line per number and a net value and break-even line.
2. Write the same memo as if the pilot had missed its target: headline, options with costs, and your recommendation.
3. Script a five-minute demo story: one real case, before and after, one safeguard.
4. List the five questions a CFO would ask, including a challenge to your baseline, and write a one-line answer to each.
