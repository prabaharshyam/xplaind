## What it is

Executives have little time, many priorities and responsibility for money and risk. Communicating with them well means:

- **Answer first.** Start with the conclusion or the decision needed, then the support.
- **Their language.** Business results (time, cost, revenue, risk, customers), not model names or architectures.
- **Proof.** A number against a baseline, a short demo, a user's words.
- **Trade-offs, honestly.** What it costs, what could go wrong, what you recommend.
- **A clear ask.** What you need from them, by when.

## How it works

**Structure.** One simple pattern for any update or email:

1. *Headline*: the main point in one sentence.
2. *Why it matters*: the business effect.
3. *Evidence*: two or three facts.
4. *Risks or trade-offs*: what might change the picture.
5. *Ask*: the decision or help needed, with a date.

**Numbers they recognise.** Use the KPI and baseline agreed in the scope of work, in rupees, hours or customers. "Saves 4,000 officer hours a month, about ₹1.2 crore a year at current salary costs" lands; "96.4% F1 on field extraction" does not, unless you translate it.

**Demos for executives.** Five minutes, one story: a real case, before and after, the user's view, and one moment showing a safeguard. Rehearse it. Have a recorded backup in case the network fails.

**Prepare for the obvious questions.** What does it cost at full scale? What happens when it is wrong? Is our data safe? What do our people think? How does this compare with what competitors are doing? Have one-line answers ready, with detail on request.

**Short documents.** A one-page memo beats a 30-slide deck for decisions. If slides are required, one idea per slide and the headline as the slide title.

**Be honest about uncertainty.** "We expect 20 to 25 minutes per file at full rollout; the pilot branches were above average, so we plan for 25" is more credible than a single optimistic number.

## A worked example

At the end of the pilot, the lender's CEO and CFO give you 15 minutes. Your one-page memo:

> **Recommendation: roll out the credit-file assistant to all 180 officers over three months.**
>
> **Result.** In a 10-week pilot with 20 officers, median time per loan file fell from 41 to 24 minutes, and files sent back by the credit committee fell from 7% to 5.1%.
>
> **Value at full scale.** About 4,000 officer hours a month freed, roughly ₹1.2 crore a year, which the operations head plans to use to handle 25% more applications without new hiring.
>
> **Cost.** About ₹9 lakh a month to run at full scale, including model costs and support.
>
> **Risks.** Adoption may be lower in branches with mostly photographed documents; mitigated by an image clean-up step and branch visits. All credit decisions remain with officers.
>
> **Decision needed today:** approve the rollout budget and name an owner in operations.

The demo shows one real file, the officer's review screen and a flagged low-confidence figure being corrected. The CFO asks how the ₹1.2 crore was calculated; you show the one-line formula in the appendix. Approval takes ten minutes.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with the technology.** Lead with the result and the decision.
- **Model metrics without translation.** Turn them into time, money, risk or customers.
- **Long decks for simple decisions.** One page is often enough.
- **Overpromising.** Ranges and plans for downside beat a single best-case number.
- **No ask.** If you do not request a decision, you rarely get one.

## Practise

Take at least the first unit of Google's free technical writing course, which applies directly to executive writing.

1. Write a one-page recommendation memo for your use case using the five-part structure, with every number traceable to a source.
2. Script a five-minute demo story: one real case, before and after, one safeguard.
3. List the five questions a CFO would ask and write a one-line answer to each.

**You are done when you can** give your recommendation and its main evidence in under 60 seconds, out loud, without slides.
