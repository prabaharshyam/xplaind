## What it is

A **scope of work (SOW)** is the document that turns discovery into a plan both sides sign. It says what you will deliver, what you will not, by when, how each deliverable will be accepted, what the client must provide, and how changes and money are handled. Some firms call it a statement of work. The two names are often used for the same document, though some firms treat the scope of work as one section of a statement of work.

An SOW usually sits under a **master services agreement (MSA)**. The MSA is signed once and holds the general legal terms: confidentiality, liability, data protection, how either side can end the contract. Each project then gets its own SOW with the specifics. If the SOW and the MSA disagree, the contract normally says which one wins, so check. If there is no MSA, those legal terms must go into the SOW itself or a separate contract, and the client's legal team will want to review them.

A good AI SOW has these parts:

- **Objective**: the problem, and the primary KPI with its baseline and target.
- **In scope** and **out of scope**: what you will build and do, and what you will not, stated equally clearly.
- **Deliverables**: each concrete output, such as a deployed service, an eval report, a runbook or training sessions.
- **Milestones**: dates, with the stage gates between proof of concept (POC), pilot and production.
- **Acceptance criteria**: the test each deliverable must pass.
- **Assumptions and dependencies**: what you rely on the client for, with dates.
- **Roles**: who decides, who accepts, who reviews, who supports.
- **Change process**: how new requests are sized and approved.
- **Risks**: the known ones, each with an owner.
- **Commercial terms**: price, payments, who pays model and cloud costs, who owns what.

Here is a one-page skeleton you can copy and fill in. Keep the headings even when a section is short; an empty heading is a question you have not answered yet.

```markdown
# Scope of work: [project name]
Under MSA [reference], dated [date]

1. Objective
   KPI: [measure], baseline [x], target [y], by [date]
   Guardrail: [measure] must not pass [z]
2. In scope: [features, users, documents, languages]
3. Out of scope: [named exclusions]
4. Deliverables: [service, eval report, runbook, training]
5. Milestones and gates
   Gate 1 (POC), week [n]: criteria in section 6
   Gate 2 (pilot), week [n]: criteria in section 6
6. Acceptance
   Test set: [size], held out from build work
   Ground truth checked by: [names or roles]
   Bar: [measure and threshold]
   Applies to model version: [provider, model, version]
   If a gate is missed: extend, narrow or stop
7. Dependencies (client): [item, owner, date, effect of delay]
8. Roles: decides [name]; accepts [name]; supports [name]
9. Change process: logged, sized in [n] days, approved by [name]
10. Risks: [risk, owner, mitigation]
11. Commercial
    Price model: [fixed / time and materials / mix]
    Payments: [share] on signing, [share] at each gate
    Model and cloud costs: [who pays, monthly cap]
    IP: [code, prompts, golden sets, eval harness]
    Data protection: [DPA reference]
12. Signatures: [client signatory], [acceptance authority], [your side]
```

## How it works

### Write acceptance as a test someone else could run

"The assistant answers accurately" cannot be accepted or rejected. A testable criterion has four parts:

1. **A named test set.** Which files or questions, how many, and who chose them.
2. **A bar.** For example, at least 95% field accuracy on the 10 critical fields.
3. **A judge.** Who decides what the right answer is, and how disagreements are settled.
4. **A version.** The model and configuration the result applies to.

Two traps sit inside the first and third parts. First, **the test set must be held out**. During a proof of concept the team studies failures and fixes them. If it is judged on the same files it studied, it can pass the gate by fitting those files, and then do worse on new ones. So split the data: one part to build and debug on, and a held-out part, locked away from the build team, that decides the gate. A useful rule is that the person who chooses the held-out set is on the client's side.

Second, **the old process is not automatically the right answer**. The ground truth is the set of correct answers you score against. If staff retyping figures cause the rework you are trying to reduce, their original entries contain those same errors. Scoring the AI against them punishes it when it is right and they were wrong. Have two experienced client staff check each test item, and agree in advance how a third person settles disagreements.

### Be honest about AI uncertainty

Model outputs vary, and nobody can promise 95% before seeing the client's real documents. Do not hide this; write it down. State the bar on the defined test set, the human review that stays in place even after acceptance, and what happens if a gate is missed. There are three options: a short extension with a fix plan, narrowing the scope to what works, or stopping with a written report. Agreeing this while everyone is optimistic makes a hard conversation later calm and quick.

### Pin the model version

Model providers update and retire models during an engagement. For example, as of November 2026 Anthropic's model deprecations page promises at least 60 days' notice before it retires a publicly released model, and says requests to a retired model fail. Other providers and cloud platforms set their own schedules. A system accepted on one model can behave differently on its replacement.

So the SOW should say: acceptance applies to a named model version. If the provider changes or retires it, you re-run the acceptance test on the new version. If the system falls below the bar, the SOW says who pays for the fixes. A common split is that fixes during your support period are yours, and after it they are change requests.

### Name what is out, and date what you depend on

"Out of scope: integration with the core banking system; Tamil-language documents; mobile app; customer-facing use." Most disputes come from things nobody wrote down.

Dependencies need an owner, a date and an effect: "Client provides read access to the document store by 10 July; each week of delay moves milestone 2 by one week." Without the effect, a client delay quietly becomes your missed deadline.

### Get the right person to accept

In many Indian enterprises the SOW is signed by procurement or IT, because they hold the vendor budget. But the person who can say "yes, this is done" is usually the business owner, such as the head of operations. If those are different people, name the business owner in the SOW as the **acceptance authority**. Then get their written agreement to the acceptance criteria before signing; an email reply is enough. Otherwise you can pass every test in the document and still hear "this is not what we needed".

### Settle the commercial terms

Clients push back on the commercial terms first, so draft them with the same care as the technical parts. You are not giving legal advice here. Your job is to raise each question, propose a sensible default, and let the client's legal and procurement teams, and your own, review the wording.

- **Pricing model.** A **fixed price** suits work you can size, such as a four-week POC with a defined test. **Time and materials** (paid by effort, often with a cap) suits work with real uncertainty, such as a pilot where users will ask for changes. Many AI projects mix them: fixed for the POC, capped time and materials for the pilot.
- **Payments tied to gates.** For example, a share on signing and a share at each passed gate. This links money to evidence, and a client who stops at gate 1 pays only for the work done.
- **Model and cloud costs.** Say who pays for model API calls and hosting. Often these run in the client's own cloud account and the client pays them directly. If you pay and pass them on, add a monthly cap that needs written approval to exceed. Usage can grow quickly once a pilot succeeds.
- **Intellectual property (IP).** Say who owns the code, the prompts, the golden sets and the eval harness (the scripts that run the tests). Golden sets are built from the client's data and experts' time, so clients often expect to own them. You may want to keep reusable tools you built before the project. Write both down.
- **Data protection.** Under India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, the client deciding why and how personal data is used is the **Data Fiduciary**. You, processing it on their behalf, are a **Data Processor**, and the Act says a processor may be engaged only under a valid contract. That contract is usually a data processing agreement (DPA). The DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025, and most duties apply from May 2027, so contracts signed now will run under them.
- **Confidentiality and liability.** These normally live in the MSA. For AI, check one point: who is responsible for a decision made using the system's output? The clean answer is to keep a human decision-maker in the loop and put AI-made decisions out of scope, as the worked example does.

### Keep it short, then test it on a stranger

The business owner should be able to read the main SOW in fifteen minutes. Put technical detail, such as field lists and test-set design, in an appendix.

Before signing, give the draft to someone who was not in the discovery meetings. Ask three questions: what exactly will be delivered, what will not, and how will each deliverable be accepted? Every hesitation marks an ambiguity, and each ambiguity is a future argument at acceptance time.

**Vague and testable wording:**

| Section | Vague | Testable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Objective | "Improve efficiency with AI" | "Median time per file from 41 to 22 minutes for 20 pilot officers" |
| Acceptance | "Works well" | "At least 95% on 10 critical fields across 200 held-out files, checked by two senior officers" |
| Model | Not mentioned | "Applies to the named model version; a provider change triggers a re-run of the test" |
| Out of scope | Not mentioned | "Credit decisions; customer-facing features; non-English documents" |
| Dependency | "Client provides data" | "Read access to two years of masked files by 10 July; delays move milestone 2 week for week" |
| Change process | Not mentioned | "Logged, sized within three working days, approved in writing by the head of operations" |
| Costs | "All inclusive" | "Model and cloud costs paid by the client in its Azure tenancy; we report them monthly" |

## A worked example

Extract from the SOW for the Chennai lender's credit-file assistant. It is signed by the lender's IT head, who holds the vendor budget, and names the head of operations as acceptance authority.

> **Objective.** Reduce median officer time per loan file from 41 minutes (baseline, June) to 22 minutes for the 20 pilot officers, without raising credit committee rework above 7%.
>
> **In scope.** Extraction of 24 agreed fields from bank statements, salary slips and bureau reports (English, PDF and phone photos); a draft credit summary in the lender's template; a review screen for officers; deployment in the lender's Azure tenancy; eval and pilot reports; runbook; two training sessions.
>
> **Out of scope.** Credit decisions, which stay with officers and the credit committee; customer-facing features; documents in languages other than English; integration with the loan origination system beyond a file export.
>
> **Acceptance (proof of concept, week 4).** 300 masked past files, chosen by the lender's credit team. 100 are shared with the build team for development. 200 are held out: never shown to the build team, and used to score the gate. Ground truth for the held-out files is checked by two senior credit officers, with the credit manager settling disagreements. The bar is at least 95% field accuracy on the 10 critical fields, and every summary cites its source pages. Acceptance applies to the model version named in the appendix.
>
> **Dependencies.** Read access to two years of masked files by 10 July; one senior credit officer for 4 hours a week. Each week of delay moves milestone 2 by one week.
>
> **Change process.** New requests are logged, sized within three working days and approved in writing by the head of operations before work starts.
>
> **Commercial.** POC at a fixed fee; pilot on time and materials with a cap; payments split across signing and the two gates. Model and hosting costs run in the lender's Azure account and are paid by the lender. The lender owns the golden sets, prompts and project code; we keep our pre-existing tools. A data processing agreement under the DPDP Act is attached.

Notice what each clause prevents. The held-out split stops the team passing the gate by fitting the files it debugged on. The two senior officers stop the test from rewarding the old retyping errors. The cost clause stops a surprise bill when usage grows in production.

**How it paid off.** In week 7, during the pilot, the collections head asked for summaries of borrower correspondence. Because the SOW named the scope and the change process, the request was logged and sized at about four weeks. It needed new document types, new users and a new golden set. The head of operations approved it as phase 2, after the production gate, instead of letting it quietly delay the credit-file work. Phase 2 later became a second contract. The lesson on managing scope creep follows that week in detail.

## Common mistakes

- **Vague acceptance.** "Works well" invites endless debate. Name the test set, the bar, the judge and the model version.
- **Testing on the tuning set.** Acceptance measured on files the team debugged on overstates quality. Hold out a part the build team never sees.
- **Trusting the old process as the answer key.** Original entries carry the errors you are fixing. Have experts check the ground truth.
- **Silence on what is out.** Unwritten assumptions become disputes.
- **Undated dependencies.** Client delays then become your missed deadlines.
- **No plan for a missed gate.** Agree extend, narrow or stop before the work starts.
- **Ignoring model changes.** A provider update after acceptance can move quality. Pin the version and agree how re-acceptance works.
- **Fixed price on open-ended work.** A fixed fee for a pilot full of unknowns pushes you to cut corners or argue over every request.
- **Unowned model costs.** If nobody owns the API bill, the first big invoice becomes a dispute.
- **Silence on IP.** Clients assume they own everything, including golden sets you planned to reuse. Write ownership down.
- **The signer is not the acceptor.** Procurement signs, operations rejects. Name the acceptance authority and get their agreement to the criteria.

## Practice

Read Atlassian's guide to project scope and GOV.UK's guide to user stories. Then:

1. Copy the skeleton above and fill it in for the use case from your discovery and KPI exercises. Keep the main document to two pages.
2. Write acceptance criteria for three deliverables. Each must name the test set and how it is held out, the bar, the judge and the model version.
3. Write the commercial section: choose fixed price or time and materials for each stage and give one sentence on why. Say who pays model costs and who owns the golden set.
4. Swap with a friend who did not see your discovery notes. Ask them the three stranger questions and fix every ambiguity they find.

**You are done when you can** hand your SOW to someone who was not in the room and have them say exactly what will and will not be delivered, how each part will be accepted, and who pays for what.
