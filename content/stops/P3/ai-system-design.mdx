## What it is

**AI system design** is planning an AI product on paper before (or while) building it: who it serves, how success is measured, what components it has, how a request flows through them, where it can fail, how it is evaluated and how it is run in production. A short design document lets a team agree on decisions, spot risks early and explain the system to clients and reviewers.

```text
   user (underwriter)
        |
        v
   [web app] --> [API service] --> [guardrails: input]
                      |
                      v
              [hybrid search + department filter + effective date] --> [reranker] --> top 5
                      |
                      v
              [model in the insurer's cloud region]  -- outage --> [fallback model]
                      |
                      v
              [guardrails: output] --> answer with citation links
                      |
   tracing (masked) | evals in CI | dashboards: latency, cost, feedback, refusals
```

This lesson follows a design for an internal underwriting assistant at a general insurer in Mumbai.

## How it works

A practical design document, two to four pages, covers:

**1. Users and goals.** Who uses it, for what task, and the success metric with a baseline (for example, "reduce median time to answer policy questions from 6 minutes to under 2").

**2. Requirements and constraints.** Volume and peak load, latency budget, cost per request, languages, data residency, privacy and compliance rules, who may see which data.

**3. Components.** Interface; API service; retrieval (document ingestion, parsing, chunking, embeddings, index, hybrid search, reranking); prompt and model choices (and a fallback model); tools; guardrails; human review points; logging, tracing and evaluation.

**4. Request flow.** Step by step, what happens to one request, with the data passed at each step. A simple diagram helps.

**5. Failure modes and guards.** For each component, what can go wrong and what catches it:

- retrieval misses → hybrid search, metadata filters, evaluation of recall;
- hallucination → grounding, citations, refusal when sources are silent;
- injection → least privilege, data marking, approvals;
- provider outage → retries, fallback model, graceful error;
- runaway cost → caps, budgets, alerts;
- stale data → re-indexing pipeline, freshness alerts.

**6. Evaluation.** The test set (size, sources, categories), metrics for retrieval and answers, the launch bar, and how regressions are caught in CI.

**7. Operations.** Deployment, monitoring signals, alerts, on-call, release process, and data retention.

**8. Open questions and trade-offs.** What you have not decided yet and the options.

**A failure-mode table:**

| Component | What can go wrong | Guard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parsing | Scanned tables garbled | Table extraction; flagged pages reviewed |
| Retrieval | Superseded circulars ranked first | Effective-date filter |
| Retrieval | Right passage missed | Hybrid search, reranker, recall tracked |
| Generation | Unsupported claims | Citations required; refusal when sources are silent |
| Documents | Injection in uploaded files | No write tools; content treated as data |
| Provider | Outage or rate limits | Retries, fallback model, clear message |
| Cost | Runaway usage | Per-user caps, daily budget alert |

**Reviewing a design.** Walk through one request from the user's tap to the answer, and at each box ask three questions: what data is here, who may see it, and what happens if this part fails or is slow. Invite a security or compliance reviewer to the walk-through early. Questions such as "where is this data stored?" are cheap in week one and expensive in week ten.

**Keeping it current.** Treat the document as living. When a decision changes, update the page and note the date and reason in a short decision log at the end. New team members and client reviewers then read one accurate page instead of reconstructing history from chat threads.

## A worked example

Design summary for an internal assistant at a Mumbai general insurer, answering underwriters' questions from 2,000 policy wordings, circulars and underwriting guidelines:

- *Goal*: underwriters' lookup time from 6 minutes to 2; launch bar 85% correct with citations on a 120-question test set, 100% of "not covered by documents" questions refused.
- *Constraints*: 300 users, peak 40 requests a minute, first token under 2 s, data in India, access by department.
- *Components*: layout-aware parsing with OCR for old circulars; 400-token chunks with section paths; hybrid search with department filters and a reranker; a mid-sized model through the insurer's cloud region with a fallback; citations rendered as links; tracing with masked logs.
- *Failure modes*: superseded circulars (effective-date filter), scanned tables (table extraction, flagged pages), injection via uploaded documents (no write tools), outage (fallback, then a clear message).
- *Evaluation*: recall at 5, answer correctness by a calibrated judge plus underwriter review of 30 answers a month; fast checks in CI.
- *Operations*: container service, dashboards for latency, cost, feedback and refusal rate, re-indexing nightly.

The document took two days and prevented a month of rework when the compliance team asked about data residency in week one.

| Design element | Decision |
| --- | --- |
| Goal | Lookup time from 6 minutes to 2 |
| Launch bar | 85% correct with citations on 120 questions; 100% refusal on out-of-scope ones |
| Constraints | 300 users, 40 requests a minute at peak, first token under 2 s, data in India |
| Payoff | Compliance's week-one residency question answered from the document, avoiding a month of rework |

## Common mistakes

- **Starting from the model instead of the user and metric.**
- **No failure analysis.** Every component fails somehow.
- **No evaluation plan.** "We'll see how it does" is not a plan.
- **Ignoring constraints.** Latency, cost and data rules shape the design.
- **Long documents nobody reads.** Two to four clear pages.
