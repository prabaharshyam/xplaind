## What it is

Every LLM app trades three things against each other:

- **Quality**: how often answers are correct, complete and safe.
- **Cost**: money per request and per month.
- **Speed**: how long users wait.

Bigger models, more retrieved context, rerankers, reflection steps and agent loops usually raise quality and also raise cost and latency. Good engineering makes these trade-offs visible and decides them on purpose, with the people who own the product.

```text
   quality ^
       89% |                         * large + reranker (₹0.64, 4.3 s)
       86% |              * router (₹0.24, 2.8 s)
       85% |                      * large (₹0.60, 3.9 s)
       76% |  * small (₹0.08, 1.6 s)
           +-------------------------------------------> cost and latency
   chosen: router + reranker for exclusions and claims = 88%, ₹0.30, 3.1 s p95
   + streaming: first words in under 1 s
```

This lesson follows a health-insurance company in Hyderabad whose call-centre agents use an assistant during live calls.

## How it works

**Measure all three on the same test set.** For each option, record:

- quality from your eval (by category, not only overall);
- cost per request (tokens times prices);
- latency at the median and the 95th percentile, and time to first token.

**Put the options in a table.** For example: small model, large model, large model plus reranker. Then the trade-off becomes a business conversation: "the reranker improves accuracy from 85% to 89% and adds 400 ms per request at p95; worth it?"

**Ask what quality matters.** For a casual chat, 85% may be fine; for medical or financial answers, quality and safety dominate. Weight errors by their consequences.

**Look for free wins first.** Some changes improve speed or cost without hurting quality:

- streaming (perceived speed);
- prompt caching (cost and speed for repeated prefixes);
- shorter outputs and prompts;
- running independent steps in parallel;
- removing unnecessary steps.

**Route by difficulty.** Send simple requests to a fast, cheap model and hard ones to a strong model. Measure how often the router gets it wrong.

**Set budgets.** Agree a latency budget (for example, first token under 1.5 s at p95) and a cost per request ceiling, then maximise quality within them.

**Revisit.** Prices fall, models improve and usage grows; the best trade-off changes. Re-run the table every few months.

**Levers and their effects:**

| Lever | Quality | Cost | Speed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Larger model | Up | Up | Slower |
| Reranker | Up | Slightly up | Slightly slower |
| More retrieved context | Often up, then down | Up | Slower first token |
| Router | Near the large model | Down | Faster on average |
| Streaming | Same | Same | Feels much faster |
| Prompt caching | Same | Down | Faster first token |
| Shorter outputs | Same or better | Down | Faster |

**Weighting by consequence.** Split the test set into categories and give each a weight from the business: how costly is a wrong answer here? For the insurer, exclusions and claims carried the most weight, general questions the least. A weighted score can rank options differently from plain accuracy, and it makes the reason for the choice clear to everyone.

**Writing the recommendation.** One paragraph for the product owner: the options considered, the chosen one, its quality, cost and latency, what it gives up compared with the most accurate option, and when to revisit. For example: "We recommend the router with a reranker on exclusions and claims: 88% accuracy at about ₹0.30 per request and 3.1 s at p95. The large model with reranker everywhere is 1 point better but twice the cost and slower on live calls."

**Measuring the router.** A router saves money only if it sends the right requests to the right model. Label a sample of test questions as easy or hard, run the router, and count two kinds of mistake: hard questions sent to the small model, which hurt quality, and easy ones sent to the large model, which waste money. The first kind matters more. If it is common in a high-risk category, route that whole category to the strong model, as the insurer did for exclusions and claims.

## A worked example

A Hyderabad health-insurance company's assistant answers policy questions for its call-centre agents.

The team compares four options on 150 test questions:

```text
option                         accuracy   cost/request   p95 latency
small model                      76%        Rs 0.08        1.6 s
large model                      85%        Rs 0.60        3.9 s
large model + reranker           89%        Rs 0.64        4.3 s
router (small, hard -> large)    86%        Rs 0.24        2.8 s
```

The product owner's view: agents are on live calls, so latency matters a lot; wrong coverage answers cause complaints and refunds, so accuracy matters more. They choose the router plus the reranker for questions about exclusions and claims (the riskiest categories), reaching 88% overall at about ₹0.30 per request and 3.1 s at p95. Streaming makes the first words appear in under a second.

| Decision factor | How it shaped the choice |
| --- | --- |
| Agents on live calls | Latency matters, so streaming and the router |
| Wrong coverage answers cause complaints and refunds | Reranker for exclusions and claims |
| Cost at call-centre volume | Router keeps most traffic on the small model |

## Common mistakes

- **Optimising one dimension silently.** Show all three.
- **Overall accuracy only.** Weight by error consequences and categories.
- **Ignoring tail latency.** Users remember slow requests.
- **Missing free wins.** Streaming and caching first.
- **Never revisiting the decision.**
- **Choosing without the product owner.** The trade-off is a business decision; show the table and let them weigh it.
