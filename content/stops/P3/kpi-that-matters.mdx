## What it is

A **KPI** (key performance indicator) is the one number that says whether the project worked. For an AI deployment, a complete KPI plan has six parts:

- **One primary KPI** tied to the business result: minutes per loan file, first-response time, claims processed per officer per day, cost per resolved ticket.
- **A baseline**: the KPI measured before your system, in the same way, for the same kind of people and cases.
- **A target with a date**, and bands agreed in advance for what counts as met, close and missed.
- **Guardrail measures**: things that must not get worse, such as error rate, customer complaints or compliance exceptions.
- **Adoption**: whether people actually use the system, because a tool nobody uses cannot move the KPI.
- **A method**: where each number comes from, which cases count, which weeks count and who reports it.

Model metrics such as accuracy or faithfulness are inputs, not the KPI. A 95%-accurate extractor that officers do not trust, and so retype anyway, saves nobody a minute.

| KPI plan, signed in the scope of work | |
|---|---|
| **Primary** | Median minutes per loan file, from opening the file to submitting the credit note (loan-system timestamps) |
| **Baseline** | 41 min lender-wide (four weeks in June, 6,200 files); 41 min for the 20 pilot officers alone (about 690 of those files) |
| **Target** | 22 min within ten weeks of pilot start, judged on pilot weeks 7 to 10 |
| **Bands** | Met: 22 or less. Close: above 22, up to 26. Missed: above 26 |
| **Guardrails** | Credit committee rework not above 7%; no rise in audit policy exceptions; weekly spot check of drafts accepted without edits |
| **Adoption** | Credit note submitted from the draft on at least 80% of pilot files |
| **Comparison** | The lender's other branches over the same ten weeks |

This lesson follows the KPI agreement for a credit-file assistant at a fictional lender in Chennai: 180 credit officers, about 6,000 loan files a month. It is the same case used in the other lessons on this path, and all its figures are made up to show the method.

### Define the number exactly

Start from the problem discovery found. If officers spend about 40 minutes per file, the KPI is time per file, not "AI accuracy". Then pin it down, because a vague KPI lets each side read the result its own way:

- **Start and end events.** "From opening the file to submitting the credit note", both stamped by the loan system.
- **Which cases count.** Every file handled by a pilot officer, whether or not they used the draft. People try a new tool on easy cases first. If you count only files where the tool was used, it looks faster than it is.
- **Which statistic.** The median, not the mean. A file left open over lunch or overnight shows as hours. One such file moves the mean a lot and the median hardly at all. Report the share of files over two hours separately, as a check on the data.
- **Elapsed time is not working time.** Timestamps measure the clock, not effort. That is acceptable as long as the baseline and the pilot are measured the same way.

Prefer numbers that systems already record (timestamps, ticket close times) over surveys. If nothing records the number, set up measurement first: a simple log of start and end times for a sample of cases, started before the pilot.

### Measure the baseline on the right people

Use the same team and kind of cases, enough volume (a few hundred cases, over at least two to four weeks), and an ordinary period. A month-end rush, a festival season or a new credit policy all distort it.

Measure two baselines: the whole organisation and the pilot group alone. Pilot groups are often chosen because they are keen, well run or close to head office. If the pilot officers already took 35 minutes, comparing them with the lender-wide 41 would credit the tool with six minutes it never saved. At the Chennai lender both baselines came out at 41 minutes, so the pilot comparison is fair. The pilot branches were still keener than average, which is why the business case later planned for a more cautious figure at full rollout (see the lesson on talking to executives).

### Set the target from evidence, not ambition

"Halve it" is a wish. A defensible target comes from the work itself. In discovery, the team timed a small sample of files while shadowing officers (the case's figures): about 16 minutes went on reading documents and judging the applicant, and about 25 on retyping figures and writing the credit note. The assistant drafts the figures and the note, but officers still check flagged and critical fields, which the team estimated at about 6 minutes. So the target was 16 + 6 = 22 minutes.

A target built this way does two jobs. The client can see where it came from. And if the pilot misses, you know which step to look at: here, the checking took longer than estimated.

### Agree in advance what met, close and missed mean

Weekly medians move by chance. In the June baseline weeks the pilot group's weekly median moved by one to two minutes with nothing changed, on about 170 files a week. So a result of 24 against a target of 22 needs a rule agreed before anyone sees it.

| Band | Range | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Met | 22 minutes or less | Recommend rollout |
| Close | Above 22, up to 26 minutes | Recommend rollout, with a cautious planning figure and a fix plan for the gap |
| Missed | Above 26 minutes | Fix plan with a short extension, a narrower scope, or stop |

Judge on the later weeks. In the first weeks, results are distorted both ways: officers are still learning the tool, and some are excited by something new and use it more than they will later (the novelty effect). The lender judged on pilot weeks 7 to 10, after the first problems were fixed.

To see how much of a gap is chance, compute a likely range for the median. Resample officers, not single files, because one officer's files are alike:

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("pilot_files.csv", parse_dates=["opened_at", "submitted_at"])
df["minutes"] = (df["submitted_at"] - df["opened_at"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60

# Weekly median per branch: the numbers behind the weekly line
print(df.pivot_table(index="pilot_week", columns="branch",
                     values="minutes", aggfunc="median").round(1))

# Judge on pilot weeks 7 to 10, using every file, draft used or not
end = df[df["pilot_week"].between(7, 10)]
officers = [g.to_numpy() for _, g in end.groupby("officer_id")["minutes"]]

# Resample officers, not files: one officer's files are alike
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = len(officers)
medians = [np.median(np.concatenate([officers[i] for i in rng.integers(0, n, n)]))
           for _ in range(2000)]
low, high = np.percentile(medians, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"median {end['minutes'].median():.1f} min, likely range {low:.1f} to {high:.1f}")
```

If the target sits outside the likely range, the gap is real, not noise. Say so plainly.

### Choose guardrails, including one against gaming

Faster processing with more errors is not a win. Pick one or two quality measures the client's risk owners already care about, such as credit committee rework and audit exceptions. Rework is a lagging measure: the committee sends files back a week or two after submission, so report it with that lag and do not judge it on the last week.

Add one guardrail against gaming. Goodhart's law, named after the economist Charles Goodhart, is often put as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure". Under pressure to hit 22 minutes, an officer can accept the draft without reading it. The number improves; the real result does not. So track the share of drafts accepted with no edits, and have a senior officer spot-check 20 random files a week. A zero-edit share rising towards 100% while spot checks find missed errors means rubber-stamping.

### Compare fairly

Something else always changes during a pilot: a new policy, staff on leave, a seasonal rush. A comparison group catches it. Track the KPI for similar people without the system over the same weeks; at the lender, the other branches stayed at about 41 minutes throughout, so the drop was not a lender-wide change. If everyone gets the tool at once, compare the same people before and after, and write down everything else that changed. Put the method in the measurement plan before the pilot starts, so nobody can choose a favourable comparison afterwards.

### Turn the KPI into rupees

Executives fund rupees, not minutes. Using the lender's own HR figure of about ₹600 for a loaded officer hour, one minute saved on every file is 6,000 files ÷ 60 = 100 officer hours a month, worth about ₹60,000 a month. Fifteen minutes saved is about ₹9 lakh a month. That single conversion lets anyone translate each weekly result into money.

| Input (model or system) | Outcome (business) | How it becomes rupees |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Extraction accuracy | Minutes per loan file | Minutes saved × files × cost per minute |
| Faithfulness score | Rework rate | Files not sent back × hours to redo × hourly cost |
| Retrieval recall | Time to answer a policy question | Minutes saved × questions × cost per minute |
| Latency | Calls handled per agent per hour | Extra calls handled without extra staff |

Once the running cost is known, work out the break-even: the smallest saving per file at which value equals cost. At the lender, the full-volume cost model came late, in contract week 10 (see the case study lesson). It gave a break-even of about 13 minutes: the saving must stay at 13 minutes or more, which means 28 minutes per file or less. Build the cost model in the first weeks, so the floor is known before the pilot is judged.

### Report every week, split by group

Send one line every week with the KPI, the guardrail and adoption. In pilot week 2 it read: "Median 27 minutes per file (baseline 41, target 22); rework 5.4% against 7%; draft used on 78% of files."

Split it by branch too. A week later the split showed what the blended line did not: the draft was used on 92% of files in Anna Nagar but only 41% in Tambaram, where the median was 37 minutes. Officers there feared blame for AI errors and retyped everything. That split led to the branch visit that fixed it (see the lesson on change management). A blended number tells you whether there is a problem. A split tells you where it is.

## A worked example

After discovery at the Chennai lender, you propose the plan in the table above. The head of operations first wants "90% AI accuracy" as the target. You agree to track accuracy on the golden set as a quality input. Then you explain that an accurate draft officers do not trust saves nothing. The lender pays for officer time and rework, so those are the KPI and the guardrail. She agrees, and the plan goes into the scope of work.

Before the pilot, you pull the June timestamps twice: lender-wide (41 minutes, 6,200 files) and for the 20 pilot officers alone (41 minutes, about 690 files). You set the target from the timed steps (22 minutes) and agree the bands.

During the pilot, the weekly line flags Tambaram in pilot week 3, and adoption there recovers to 85% two weeks later. Pilot weeks 7 to 10 then give the verdict:

| Measure | Baseline | Target | Pilot weeks 7 to 10 | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Median minutes per file | 41 | 22 | 24 (likely range 23 to 25) | Close |
| Rework rate | 7% | Not above 7% | 5.1% | Held, and improved |
| Adoption | Not applicable | 80% of files | 88% | Met |
| Other branches, same weeks | 41 | No change expected | About 41 | No outside change |

The honest summary is: "Close, not met. Time per file fell 17 minutes, from 41 to 24. The target of 22 is outside the likely range, so the gap is real. Checking flagged fields took longer than the 6 minutes we estimated." Because the bands were agreed in advance, nobody argues about whether 24 is a success. The end-of-pilot meeting is about rollout and the checking step, not about what success meant.

## Common mistakes

- **Model metrics as the KPI.** Accuracy is a means; time, cost or errors in the business are the end.
- **No baseline, or the wrong group's baseline.** "Officers feel it's faster" will not survive a budget review, and a lender-wide baseline can flatter a keen pilot group.
- **Counting only the files where the tool was used.** People use it on easy cases first. Count every pilot file.
- **Judging on the first weeks.** Learning and the novelty effect distort them. Judge on the later weeks, as agreed.
- **Arguing about a narrow miss after the fact.** Agree met, close and missed bands before the pilot starts.
- **A target picked for sounding good.** Build it from timed steps, so a miss tells you where to look.
- **Guardrails that cannot catch gaming.** Rework alone lags; add spot checks of drafts accepted without edits.
- **Only the blended number.** Split by branch or team every week, or a stalled group hides inside the average.
- **Too many KPIs.** Ten numbers and nobody knows which matters. One primary, a few guardrails, one adoption measure.
