## What it is

A **design round** gives you an open problem, such as "design a support assistant for a bank" or "design question answering over 10 million documents", and one interviewer, often for 45 to 60 minutes. After introductions and your questions at the end, about 35 minutes are usually left for the design. You draw on a whiteboard or a shared document, and the interviewer interrupts with "what if...?".

The content of a good design is covered in the lesson "Design an AI system": components, request flow, failure tables, latency budgets. This lesson is about the interview itself: how the time is spent, what the interviewer writes down, and what a strong answer sounds like, numbers included.

### What the interviewer scores

Many companies use structured interviews: the same kind of question for every candidate, rated on a written rubric, with notes that back each rating. Google's public re:Work guides describe this approach. So the interviewer is not forming a vague impression. They are filling in a scorecard, and they need evidence, often your own words, under each heading. The headings vary by company, but most look like this:

| Heading | Evidence for a strong rating | Evidence for a weak rating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scoping | Asked about users, scale, cost of a wrong answer and data rules before drawing | Started naming tools in the first minute |
| Design | Whole system on the board early; each part justified for this problem | Listed technologies; could not say why each one is there |
| Depth | Went deep on the riskiest part and knew its trade-offs | Equal, shallow time on every box |
| Production thinking | Numbers for load, cost and latency; failures; a test set and launch bar | Stopped at the happy path |
| Communication | Signposted, checked in, adapted calmly to "what if" | Long silences, or defended the first design rigidly |

The practical lesson: say things the interviewer can write down. "I am assuming 2 million consumers, so about 33 model calls a second at peak" is evidence. Thinking it silently is not.

### How rounds differ by company type

- **Large product companies** tend to set generic, scale-heavy prompts and rate against a fixed rubric. Clear structure and numbers matter most.
- **Startups** care whether you could build version one next month. Expect "what would you cut?" and questions about cost. Some replace the round with a take-home or a live build.
- **Forward deployed and client-facing roles** often run the round as a customer conversation. Candidate reports on Palantir's loop, for example, describe a "decomposition" round in which the interviewer plays a customer with a vague problem and scores how you break it down. Ask more questions, and propose a small first deliverable.
- **IT services firms and global capability centres in India** often frame prompts around a client's or parent company's rules: data residency, an existing vendor stack, a fixed budget. Treat those constraints as the core of the problem, not noise.

Ask the recruiter which kind of round it is. It is a normal question.

## How it works

### One time plan, and how to resize it

Use the same plan every time, so the clock runs in the background and your attention stays on the problem.

| Minutes | Phase | Done when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 to 5 | Clarify | Users, scale, cost of a wrong answer, languages, data rules and success metric are written on the board |
| 5 to 10 | High level | Boxes and arrows from user to reply, agreed with the interviewer |
| 10 to 28 | Deep dives | Two or three risky parts covered, with a quick estimate |
| 28 to 31 | Failures | The three most likely failures, each with its guard |
| 31 to 34 | Evals and ops | Test set, launch bar, rollout, the one metric you would watch |
| 34 to 35 | Trade-offs | What you would change at ten times the scale, or with more time |

If you get only 30 minutes for the design, cut the deep dives to 13 minutes and keep every other phase. If you get 45, add the time to deep dives and failures, never to clarifying. If you reach minute 20 and are still on the first deep dive, say "I'll park chunking there and move to evaluation" and move. Covering every phase briefly beats covering one perfectly.

### The first five minutes

Ask questions that change the design, not questions that fill time. A useful test: if the answer would not change a box or a number, do not ask it. The strongest ones are:

- **Who gets the answer, and what does a wrong one cost?** A wrong answer read by staff is a nuisance. A wrong answer sent straight to a customer or a doctor can be a legal problem.
- **How much traffic, and when?** Daily users, and the peak pattern: bill day, exam results, festival sales.
- **What must come from live systems?** Balances, order status and outages should come from a tool call, never from the model's memory.
- **Languages, channel and data rules.** Tamil and English on WhatsApp is a different design from English on a web app. Ask where data may be stored.

Then state your assumptions as numbers and ask the interviewer to correct them. They often reply "fine, go ahead", which is your licence to move on.

### Estimates you should not have to think about

Interviewers do not want exact figures. They want to see you reason in orders of magnitude, out loud, in under a minute.

| Quantity | Rule of thumb |
| --- | --- |
| English tokens | About 4 characters or three quarters of a word per token; 1,000 words is roughly 1,300 tokens |
| Indian languages | Often need more tokens for the same meaning, depending on the tokenizer; state a multiple and say you would measure it |
| Seconds in a day | 86,400, call it 100,000 for quick sums |
| Peak model calls per second | Daily conversations divided by busy hours, times a peak factor (often 2 to 3), times turns per conversation, divided by 3,600 |
| Cost per conversation | Tokens in times input price plus tokens out times output price, per million tokens, times turns |
| Model weights in memory | About 2 bytes per parameter at 16-bit, so a 7-billion-parameter model needs about 14 GB, plus memory for the conversation it is processing |

Always say the price is an assumption with a date. Prices change often, and a candidate who knows that sounds experienced.

### Driving the conversation

- **Signpost.** "I'll do the high level first, then go deep on retrieval and on outages, since that is where it can go wrong." The interviewer now knows your plan and can redirect you early.
- **Check in at each phase change.** "Does this match what you had in mind, or should I go deeper somewhere?" Hints from the interviewer are instructions. If they ask about evaluation twice, go there.
- **Think aloud, but in sentences.** Ten seconds of silence while you write is fine. Thirty seconds of silence loses the room.
- **Answer "what if" in four moves.** Restate it as a number, say which part breaks first, change only that part, name the cost of the change.
- **Recover from mistakes openly.** "I said one model call per conversation; it is one per message, so four times that. Let me redo the number." Catching your own error is a good signal.
- **Say "I don't know" usefully.** "I don't know that provider's exact rate limit, so I would check it in week one; if it is below our peak, the fix is a queue or a second region."

## A worked example

Prompt: "Design a WhatsApp assistant for a state electricity board that answers billing and outage questions in Tamil and English." The numbers below are the candidate's stated assumptions, not facts about a real board.

**Minutes 0 to 5, as spoken.**

> **Candidate:** Before I draw anything, a few questions. How many consumers does the board serve, and does traffic spike?
>
> **Interviewer:** About 2 million. It spikes after bills go out and during outages.
>
> **Candidate:** Do answers go only to consumers, and can they ask about their own account?
>
> **Interviewer:** Yes. They should be able to check their bill.
>
> **Candidate:** Then I need an identity check, so I'll verify the consumer number with a one-time code. Outage status is in an internal system I can call? And must data stay in India?
>
> **Interviewer:** Yes to both.
>
> **Candidate:** Let me state assumptions. On a normal day, 1% of consumers message us, so 20,000 conversations. On a bill or outage day, five times that, 100,000. Four messages from the consumer per conversation. Success means accurate tariff answers and fewer helpline calls. Shall I go with those?

**Minutes 5 to 10: the high level.** WhatsApp Business Platform through a provider, then an API service, then intent routing into four paths: bill lookup and outage status (tool calls, after the code check), tariff questions (retrieval over tariff orders and FAQs in both languages, with citations), and complaints (logged with a reference number and handed to staff). A model hosted in an Indian region writes the reply.

**Minutes 10 to 28: estimate, then deep dives.**

> **Candidate:** Quick numbers. A peak day is 100,000 conversations over about 10 busy hours, and the worst hour is about three times average. That is 30,000 conversations in an hour, times four messages, so about 33 model calls a second. Each call is about 3,000 tokens in, which allows for Tamil taking more tokens, and 250 out. That is roughly 6.5 million tokens a minute at peak. I would check the provider's rate limit against that first.

| Item | Assumption | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Model | Suppose $0.10 and $0.40 per million input and output tokens, ₹94 to the dollar | About ₹0.15 per conversation |
| WhatsApp | Third-party reports, not Meta's rate card, give about ₹0.115 per service message for India from 1 October 2026 (some say up to ₹0.145), after 1,000 free a month per number, before GST (checked November 2026) | About ₹0.46 per conversation, four replies |
| Month | 26 normal days and 4 peak days, so 9.2 lakh conversations | About ₹1.4 lakh model, ₹4.2 lakh WhatsApp, before GST and provider fees |

> **Candidate:** So the channel may cost about three times the model, if that reported rate holds. Rates change often, so I'd confirm on Meta's rate card. Two things follow. Shorter conversations save more than a cheaper model. And outage questions need no model at all: the tool returns the status and a fixed Tamil or English reply fills it in.

The deep dives then cover bilingual retrieval (tariff tables extracted as tables, chunks in both languages, the tariff order date on every chunk), and outage answers taken only from the live tool, cached per area for a couple of minutes because thousands of people ask at once. The latency budget is for the whole reply, since each reply goes out as one complete message: about 5 seconds for 95 out of 100 replies.

**Minutes 28 to 35.** Failures: a wrong tariff (citations, refusal when no source matches, weekly review of flagged answers), injection (no write tools except complaint logging), provider outage (a fixed message with the helpline number). Evaluation: 300 real questions in both languages, scored by intent, with a launch bar agreed with the board; launch in one district first and watch helpline call volume there.

**The interruptions.**

> **Interviewer:** What if the outage system is down?
>
> **Candidate:** Then outage answers are the part that breaks. I would show the last known status with its timestamp, give the helpline number and alert on-call. I would not let the model guess a restoration time.
>
> **Interviewer:** What if traffic grows ten times?
>
> **Candidate:** That is about 330 calls a second and 65 million tokens a minute at peak. The rate limit breaks first. If most peak traffic is outage questions, the fixed-reply path removes those model calls. Then I'd cache the fixed part of the prompt, and queue the rest with a "we are replying shortly" message. The cost is slower replies at the very top of the peak.

| Phase | What earned the scorecard evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Clarify | Asked who receives answers and what must come from live systems; stated numbers |
| Estimate | 33 calls a second at peak, and a cost split that changed the design |
| Deep dive | Outage path with no model; bilingual retrieval with dated tariff chunks |
| What if | Named the part that breaks, changed only that, gave the cost |

## Common mistakes

- **Never asking who receives the answer.** Staff-facing and customer-facing systems need different guards, and the interviewer is waiting for the question.
- **Designing an agent when a lookup would do.** Bill amount and outage status are database reads. Adding a model there adds cost, delay and a way to be wrong.
- **Numbers with no assumptions behind them.** "About ₹5 lakh a month" means nothing until you say the volume, tokens and prices that produced it.
- **Pricing only the model.** The channel, the one-time codes, human review and hosting can cost more than tokens.
- **Treating hints as noise.** When the interviewer asks the same thing twice, they are telling you where the missing evidence is.
- **Reciting a memorised architecture.** Every prompt has one or two constraints that should bend the design. If yours looks the same for a bank and an electricity board, you missed them.
- **Arguing with a "what if".** It is a new requirement, not a challenge to your judgement. Adapt, then name the cost.

## Practice

Read the parts of Chip Huyen's ML interviews book linked below on how interview rounds run, and revise the content in the lesson "Design an AI system" before your first timed run.

This lesson's check is a project. Practise these ten prompts out loud. Do two as full 35-minute designs, and the rest as five-minute spoken answers.

1. Design a support assistant for a bank's credit card customers, in Hindi and English. (design)
2. Design question answering over 10 million internal documents with per-team access rights. (design)
3. How would you evaluate a switch to a newer, cheaper model?
4. Our AI feature's cost doubled this month. How do you find out why and cut it in half?
5. Prompting, RAG or fine-tuning for sorting support tickets into 40 categories?
6. How do you stop prompt injection in an assistant that reads customer emails?
7. Design an agent that books train tickets. What may it do without asking?
8. How would you summarise 200-page tender documents for a construction firm?
9. Tell me about an AI project where the first version failed.
10. How do you decide that an assistant is ready to launch?

Record one design answer with a timer. Score it out of six, one point each: clarified before drawing; stated numbers; high level before deep dives; named failures with guards; gave a test set and launch bar; answered a "what if" with its cost. Note where your minutes actually went. Practising with a partner who interrupts twice is better still, and playing the interviewer for them teaches you what a clear answer sounds like.

**You are done when you can** design an unseen AI system in 35 minutes, out loud, scoring at least five of six on the rubric, with every number tied to a stated assumption.
