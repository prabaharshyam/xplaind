## What it is

This is one engagement told whole: the running case from the other lessons on this path, put in order, with the parts those lessons leave out. The client is a fictional mid-sized non-banking lender in Chennai with 180 credit officers in Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, handling about 6,000 loan files a month. All figures are the case's own, made up to show the method. Read it as the model for your capstone write-up.

Week 1 is the first week of the contract. Discovery came before it.

| Weeks | Stage | Key decision | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 weeks before | Discovery, KPI, scope | A credit-file assistant, not a chatbot; credit decisions out of scope | About 40 min a file, 6,000 files a month, 7% rework; baseline 41 min |
| 1 to 4 | Proof of concept | Image clean-up and a low-confidence flag | 93% to 96.4% on critical fields; gate passed |
| 5 to 14 | Pilot | Accountability note in Tambaram; cheaper model routing | 24 min, 5.1% rework, 88% adoption; ₹233 to ₹150 a file |
| 15 to 26 | Production | Three monthly waves, approved on net value | About ₹16 lakh a year net at the planning figure |
| 23 to 26 | Handover | Reverse shadowing, then escalation-only support | Client engineers did every core task themselves |

## How it works

### Discovery: the ask was wrong

The ask was "a chatbot for customers". Nine interviews and half a day watching the loan team showed the call centre already handled customer queries well. The pain was inside: officers spent about 40 minutes per file reading bank statements, salary slips and bureau reports, retyping figures and writing a credit note. At 6,000 files a month, that is about 4,000 officer-hours. Retyping errors sent 7% of files back from the credit committee.

The reframe worked because it came with numbers the head of operations recognised. Discovery numbers are estimates, so before the contract the team measured the real baseline from loan-system timestamps: 41 minutes median over four weeks and 6,200 files.

### KPI and scope

- **KPI**: median officer time per file, 41 to 22 minutes within ten weeks of the pilot start.
- **Guardrail**: rework must not rise above 7%. **Adoption**: officers using the draft on at least 80% of their files.
- **In scope**: 24 fields, 10 of them critical, from English PDFs and phone photos; a draft credit note; a review screen.
- **Out of scope**: credit decisions, customer features, other languages.

### Proof of concept: what the average hid

The golden set was 300 masked past files from six branches, labelled by two senior officers working separately. Where they disagreed, the credit head decided. Ten critical fields on 300 files gives 3,000 values to score.

Each document was read by one structured-output model call, and every value had to pass a schema. This is an excerpt:

```python
from decimal import Decimal
from typing import Literal
from pydantic import BaseModel, Field, model_validator

class Source(BaseModel):
    page: int = Field(ge=1)
    text: str = Field(min_length=1)  # the exact words the value was read from

class Amount(BaseModel):
    rupees: Decimal | None  # None if the document does not show it
    source: Source | None
    confidence: Literal["high", "low"]

class SalarySlip(BaseModel):
    employer: str
    month: str = Field(pattern=r"^\d{4}-\d{2}$")  # for example 2026-06
    gross_pay: Amount
    net_pay: Amount  # critical field

    @model_validator(mode="after")
    def net_not_above_gross(self):
        g, n = self.gross_pay.rupees, self.net_pay.rupees
        if g is not None and n is not None and n > g:
            raise ValueError("net pay above gross pay: re-read or flag")
        return self
```

Three choices are worth copying. Every value carries its page and the words it came from, so an officer can check it in seconds. A missing value is `None`, never a guess. And cross-checks such as "net pay cannot exceed gross pay" catch misreadings that a format check misses. "Low" confidence was not the model's opinion of itself, which is unreliable. It was set by rules: a poor image-quality score, a failed cross-check, or two reads that disagreed.

The first result was 93%, under the 95% gate. Splitting the golden set by document source showed why:

| Slice | Values scored | First run | After image clean-up |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PDFs from net banking and HR systems (180 files) | 1,800 | 98.0% | 98.0% |
| Phone photos (120 files) | 1,200 | 85.5% | 94.0% |
| All files | 3,000 | 93.0% | 96.4% |

Most misses were phone photos with dark shadows across the page. An image clean-up step (straighten, even out the light) and the low-confidence flag fixed most of them. Notice the expert's point: the overall figure passed, but photos alone still did not. So the gate note said so:

> **Gate 1, week 4.** Criteria: at least 95% on the 10 critical fields across 300 files; every summary cites its source pages. Result: 96.4% overall (PDFs 98.0%, photos 94.0%); citations on every summary. Decision by the head of operations: go to pilot, with two conditions. The low-confidence flag is always on, and the photo slice is reported separately every week.

### Engineering for the pilot

The pilot system was built in weeks 3 and 4, alongside the end of the proof of concept. The lender's risk team had already approved its own Azure tenancy in an Indian region, so the models were used through that tenancy, behind a gateway with a fallback, instead of starting a new vendor approval. The service ran in containers with evals in CI, sign-on through Entra ID, documents filtered by each officer's branch, an audit log of every view and edit, and personal data masked in traces.

The risk team's questions are a useful list for your own work. Where is the data stored and processed? Can the provider use it to train models? Who can see which files? What is logged, and for how long? How is a customer's data erased everywhere, including logs and the eval set? The FDE answered each one in writing, quoting the provider's published data-privacy terms with a link, never from memory.

### Pilot: people, scope and a late review

Twenty officers in two branches, Anna Nagar and Tambaram, used it for ten weeks, with a weekly demo and a risk log.

- **Week 6.** The production security review had not been scheduled. Escalated to the CIO's office the same day, it was booked for week 9, three weeks before it would have blocked the production gate.
- **Week 7.** Adoption was 92% in Anna Nagar and 41% in Tambaram. The branch manager had said any figure error would be the officer's fault, "AI or not", so officers retyped everything to be safe. The head of operations wrote down what officers are accountable for: reviewing flagged and critical fields, using the side-by-side review screen added in week 8. With repeat training and a regional summary template, Tambaram reached 85% in two weeks.
- **Week 7.** Three change requests came in one week. Collections summaries went to phase 2. Tamil salary slips were swapped in for PDF export and shipped in week 9. Approve-or-reject recommendations were declined, because credit decisions were out of scope.

Result: 24 minutes median, 5.1% rework, 88% adoption. The 22-minute target was narrowly missed. Because the measures were agreed in advance, the client discussed rollout, not what success meant.

### The wrong turn that cost two weeks

In the proof of concept the largest available model read every page, because it scored best. The POC report showed cost per file on 300 files, which looked small. Nobody multiplied it out to 6,000 files a month, with retries and the long fixed instructions sent with every page.

In week 10 the FDE built the full-volume projection for the business case and found it: about ₹14 lakh a month, or ₹233 a file, against about ₹173 a file of value. At that price the rollout would lose money.

She told the head of operations in that week's update, as a risk with a fix plan and a date, before any memo was written. In weeks 11 and 12 the team sent typed PDFs to a smaller model and kept the large one for photos and low-confidence pages. They cached the fixed instructions and stopped re-reading pages already extracted. A rerun of the golden set showed no loss of accuracy. Cost fell to about ₹9 lakh a month, ₹150 a file. The price was two engineer-weeks, borne by the FDE's side, and an audit-report export that slipped to the first rollout wave.

### The business case

The memo to the CEO and CFO compared value and cost in the same unit, at a cautious planning figure of 26 minutes per file. Officer time is valued with the lender's own HR figure of about ₹600 an hour, loaded.

| Line | Per month |
| --- | --- |
| Time saved: 6,000 files × 15 min = 1,500 hours × ₹600 | ₹9.0 lakh |
| Fewer sent-back files: 114 × 2 hours × ₹600 | ₹1.4 lakh |
| Running cost, all in | ₹9.0 lakh |
| Net value | ₹1.4 lakh a month, about ₹16 lakh a year (about ₹31 lakh at the pilot's 24 minutes) |
| Break-even | About 13 minutes saved per file |

This is a modest case, and the memo said so: time saved barely covers the running cost at Indian salaries, and the margin comes from quality. The first draft had compared ₹50 lakh a year of value with ₹9 lakh a month of cost, which is ₹1.08 crore a year. The CFO's analyst caught it the day before the meeting. The CFO approved three monthly waves, on the condition that the break-even line goes into the monthly report.

### Production and handover

Waves ran in weeks 15 to 18, 19 to 22 and 23 to 26, with the FDE supporting the last two. In weeks 23 to 26 the lender's engineers, Karthik and Divya, first watched, then did each core task while she watched: a release, a rollback, a prompt change through CI with evals, two simulated incidents. Then came sign-off, removal of her access, rotated gateway keys and three months of escalation-only support.

Three months after the last wave, the numbers across all 180 officers were: 25 minutes median, 5.3% rework, 84% adoption and ₹8.7 lakh a month running cost. That is about ₹25 lakh a year net. The two photo-heavy rural branches sat at 29 minutes, the next piece of work.

## A worked example

Here is the engagement told in under two minutes, for a portfolio or an interview:

"A Chennai lender asked for a customer chatbot. Discovery showed the real cost was document work: about 40 minutes per loan file, 6,000 files a month, with 7% sent back for errors. We agreed one KPI, median minutes per file, from a measured 41 to a target of 22, with rework as a guardrail. My proof of concept missed the 95% accuracy gate at 93%; splitting the golden set showed phone photos were the problem, and image clean-up took it to 96.4%. In a ten-week pilot with 20 officers, time fell to 24 minutes, just short of target, with 5.1% rework and 88% adoption, after we fixed a fear of blame in one branch. My mistake: I priced the model at full volume only in week 10, and it cost more per file than it saved. Routing easy pages to a smaller model cut cost by more than a third in two weeks. Three months after rollout to 180 officers: 25 minutes, about ₹25 lakh a year net. Next time I would start the security review and the cost model in week one."

Why each part is there:

| Part of the story | What it shows the listener |
| --- | --- |
| The ask and the reframe | You find the real problem before building |
| One KPI with a measured baseline | You measure business results, not model scores |
| The missed gate and the slice | You read evals properly instead of trusting the average |
| "Just short of target" | You report honestly, which makes the other numbers believable |
| The mistake and its cost | You notice your own errors and say so early |
| Net value after rollout | You think about money the way the client does |

## Common mistakes

These sink portfolio case studies, and interviewers spot them fast:

- **Invented numbers presented as results.** With an imagined client, your discovery figures are assumptions. Label them and give a source. Only numbers from your own golden set are results.
- **"Improved efficiency by 40%".** Of what, from what baseline, measured how, on how many cases? Write the number with its unit, baseline and sample.
- **Only the average.** One accuracy figure hides the slice that fails. Show the breakdown by document type or source.
- **No failure, or a failure solved in one sentence.** Say how you found it, what it cost in time or money, and what you told the client.
- **Value without cost.** If your system costs ₹150 a case to run, say so next to what it saves, in the same unit.
- **No code link, or code nobody can run.** A reviewer should be able to rerun your evals from the README.
- **Retelling every stage at equal length.** Spend your words on the decisions and the evidence, not on describing the process.

Read the a16z essay on the FDE role for the business context.

This lesson's check is the capstone write-up. Choose a public dataset and an imagined client in an Indian industry you know, such as a hospital chain, a logistics company, a state department or a retailer. Write two or three pages in the shape of this lesson:

1. Discovery findings with numbers, each labelled as an assumption with its source, such as a published guide, an annual report or an interview with someone who does the work.
2. KPI, baseline, target, guardrail and scope with exclusions.
3. Your golden set and how you labelled it, results split by at least one slice, and the gate it had to pass.
4. Architecture with its governance choices: access, logging, masking and where data goes.
5. Cost per case at the assumed monthly volume, against the value per case.
6. One thing that went wrong in your build, how you found it and what it cost you.
7. How someone else would run it: a README, a runbook and one command that reruns the evals.

**You are done when you can** tell your case study in two minutes, point to which numbers you measured and which you assumed, and answer "what would you do differently?" honestly.
