## What it is

**Discovery** is the first phase of an engagement, typically one to three weeks, before anything is built. Its goal is shared understanding:

- What problem matters, to whom, and how much?
- How is the work done today, step by step?
- Where do time, cost and errors actually go?
- What data exists, where, and in what condition?
- What constraints apply: rules, systems, budgets, people?
- Is AI the right tool, or would a form, a report or a process change do?

The output is short and concrete: a process map, a problem statement with numbers, a shortlist of use cases ranked by value and feasibility, and the risks and open questions.

```text
   DISCOVERY (two weeks)
   week 1: interviews (5-10, past tense) + shadowing (half a day)
             |
             v
   process map: trigger -> step -> step -> outcome
                with who, system, minutes, failure points, judgement vs rote
             |
   week 2: rough numbers (volume, time per item, rework) -> score use cases
             |
             v
   play-back:  "Here is what we heard"  -->  client corrects  -->  agreed first use case + baseline
```

This lesson follows a non-banking lender in Chennai that asked for a customer chatbot and needed something else.

## How it works

**Talk to the people who do the work.** Leaders describe the process as designed; front-line staff describe it as it is. Interview both. Aim for five to ten conversations of 30 to 45 minutes across roles.

**Ask about the past, not opinions about the future.** "Walk me through the last claim you processed" beats "Would an AI assistant help you?" People are poor at predicting what they would use and good at describing what they did.

**Watch the work.** Sit with someone for an hour. Note every system they open, every copy-paste, every question they ask a colleague, every wait. Shadowing finds what interviews miss.

**Map the process.** Draw the steps from trigger to outcome, with who does each, the systems used, time taken and failure points. Mark where judgement is needed and where it is rote.

**Measure, roughly.** Volume per month, time per item, error or rework rates, backlog. Ask for real data where possible; estimates from staff are a start.

**Score use cases.** For each candidate: value (hours or errors saved, revenue), feasibility (data available, AI good at this kind of task, tolerance for mistakes), risk (regulatory, reputational) and effort. Favour frequent, painful, data-rich tasks where a person can check the output.

**Play it back.** Present what you heard to the client, in their words, and let them correct you. This is where trust forms.

**Scoring use cases:**

| Use case | Value | Feasibility | Risk | Effort | Rank |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Document extraction and draft credit summary | High: about 4,000 officer-hours a month, 7% rework | High: two years of files with decisions | Medium: officer reviews every figure | Medium | 1 |
| Collections call notes summary | Medium | High | Low | Low | 2 |
| Customer chatbot | Low: call centre already handles queries well | Medium | Medium: customer-facing | Medium | 3 |

**Good interview questions:**

| Instead of | Ask |
| --- | --- |
| "Would AI help you?" | "Walk me through the last application you processed." |
| "Is this process slow?" | "How long did the last one take, and where did you wait?" |
| "What features do you want?" | "What did you copy from one system to another last week?" |
| "Do errors happen?" | "When was something last sent back, and why?" |

**Writing the play-back.** One page: the problem in the client's words, the process map, three numbers that matter, the ranked use cases with reasons, risks and open questions, and a proposed first step with its baseline. Send it before the meeting, then walk through it and invite corrections. A play-back the client edits is one they own.

## A worked example

A Chennai-based non-banking lender asks for "an AI chatbot for customers". You propose a two-week discovery first.

Interviews with the head of operations, two team leads, four credit officers and two collections agents, plus half a day shadowing the loan-processing team, show:

- Customer queries are mostly handled well by the existing call centre.
- The real pain is in loan processing: credit officers spend about 40 minutes per application reading bank statements, salary slips and bureau reports, retyping figures into a spreadsheet and writing a summary note. With 6,000 applications a month, that is roughly 4,000 officer-hours.
- Errors in retyped figures cause 7% of applications to be sent back by the credit committee.
- Documents arrive as PDFs and phone photos; the lender has two years of past applications with final decisions.

Your play-back recommends starting with document extraction and a draft credit summary, with the officer reviewing every figure, not with a customer chatbot. The baseline is 40 minutes and 7% rework. The head of operations agrees, and the chatbot idea moves to a later list.

| Finding | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Customer queries handled well already | Call-centre interviews and data |
| Officers spend about 40 minutes per application | Shadowing and team-lead estimates |
| 6,000 applications a month, about 4,000 officer-hours | Operations data |
| 7% of applications returned for retyping errors | Credit committee records |
| Recommendation | Start with extraction and a draft summary; chatbot moved to a later list |

## Common mistakes

- **Solutioning in the first meeting.** Listen first; propose after you have evidence.
- **Only talking to managers.** The process on paper and the process in practice differ.
- **Asking hypothetical questions.** Ask about the last real case.
- **No numbers.** Without volume and time per item, you cannot rank use cases or prove value later.
- **Ignoring non-AI fixes.** Sometimes a template or a database view solves it. Saying so builds credibility.

## Practise

Read the GOV.UK guides to discovery and user research.

1. Pick a process you can observe (a college admission office, a shop's billing, a friend's job). Interview two people who do it using only past-tense questions.
2. Draw the process map with steps, people, systems, time and failure points.
3. List three use cases, score each for value, feasibility, risk and effort, and write a one-page play-back.

**You are done when you can** present a process map and a ranked use-case list with at least one number per use case, and explain why the top one is first.
