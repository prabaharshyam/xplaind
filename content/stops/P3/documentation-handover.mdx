## What it is

**Handover** is the planned transfer of a system to the people who will own it. It has three parts:

- **Documentation** for each audience: users, operators, developers and owners.
- **Knowledge transfer**: training, pairing and a period where the client's team runs the system while you are still available.
- **Formal close**: agreed criteria that handover is complete, access removed, and a support arrangement if any.

AI systems need extra handover material, because their behaviour depends on prompts, models, data and evals, not only code.

```text
   HANDOVER (four weeks)
   week 1  SHADOW          client engineers watch you operate; read docs; questions -> doc fixes
   week 2  REVERSE SHADOW  they change a prompt (CI evals), handle a simulated incident; you watch
   week 3  RUN ALONE       they run KPI report and cost review; champion trains new users
   week 4  CLOSE           checklist sign-off | your access removed | secrets rotated | support terms

   documents:  users: guide  | operators: runbooks | developers: how-tos + architecture
               owners: KPI, costs, risks | everyone: decision records
```

This lesson follows the handover of a credit-file assistant at a lender in Chennai.

## How it works

**Write for each reader.** A useful split (from the Diátaxis framework): tutorials to learn, how-to guides for tasks, reference for facts, and explanations for reasons.

- *Users*: a short guide to using the system well, what it is good and bad at, how to check outputs and give feedback.
- *Operators*: **runbooks** for routine tasks and known incidents, such as "model provider returns errors", "answers reference outdated documents", "costs spike", "connector stopped". Each runbook: symptoms, checks, fix, escalation.
- *Developers*: architecture overview, how to run it locally, how to deploy, how to change a prompt safely, where configuration lives.
- *Owners*: what the system does, its KPI and current numbers, risks, costs, and decisions made with reasons.

**AI-specific essentials.** The golden sets and how to run the evals; the prompt and model versions in use and how to change them; the data pipeline and how to re-index; the guardrails and their test sets; the audit log schema; the cost dashboard; known failure modes with examples.

**Decision records.** Short notes for important choices: what was decided, alternatives, and why. They stop the next team from undoing good decisions or repeating bad experiments.

**Shadow, then reverse shadow.** First the client's engineers watch you operate and change the system. Then they do it while you watch: a deploy, a prompt change through CI with evals, a simulated incident from a runbook. Handover is complete when they have done each core task themselves.

**Clean exit.** Remove your access, rotate shared secrets, transfer accounts and billing, and confirm the support terms.

**A runbook page:**

| Section | Example: "answers cite outdated documents" |
| --- | --- |
| Symptoms | Users report old rates; refusal rate normal; feedback mentions dates |
| Checks | Index freshness dashboard; last pipeline run; effective-date filter setting |
| Fix | Re-run the indexing job for the affected source; confirm superseded flags |
| Verify | Ask three test questions from the golden set about current rates |
| Escalate | If the source connector fails twice, contact the platform team lead |

**The handover checklist.** Before sign-off, tick each item with the name of the client engineer who did it: deployed a release; rolled one back; changed a prompt through CI with evals; re-indexed a source; handled two simulated incidents from runbooks; produced the monthly KPI and cost report; rotated a secret. Add the documents delivered and the accounts removed. A checklist signed by the client's team makes the end of the engagement clear and avoids disputes about what was handed over.

**Writing for people who were not there.** Assume the reader joins a year from now and has never met your team. Explain why things are the way they are, link to decision records, and keep examples real. Have someone outside the project follow each how-to guide without help, and fix every place they get stuck.

## A worked example

Handover of the Chennai lender's credit-file assistant takes four weeks.

Week 1: the lender's two engineers, Karthik and Divya, pair with you on daily operations and read the developer docs, raising 23 questions that become doc fixes.

Week 2: Divya changes the summary prompt for a new regional format. She runs the eval suite in CI, sees a 1.5-point drop on the golden set, fixes an instruction and merges. Karthik handles a simulated incident ("connector to the document store stopped") using the runbook, in 25 minutes.

Week 3: they run the monthly KPI report and the cost review themselves; the operations team's champion runs a training session for new officers using your user guide.

Week 4: sign-off against the handover checklist, removal of your accounts, rotation of the gateway keys you had used, and a three-month support agreement for escalations only.

Six months later, Karthik upgrades the model behind the gateway after the evals pass, without calling you. That is a successful handover.

| 1 | 23 questions from the client engineers turned into doc fixes |
| 2 | Divya's prompt change caught a 1.5-point drop in CI and fixed it; Karthik resolved a simulated connector incident in 25 minutes |

## Common mistakes

- **One giant document.** Different readers need different documents.
- **No runbooks.** Operators learn the system during their first incident.
- **Leaving evals out.** Without golden sets and eval scripts, the client cannot change prompts or models safely.
- **Handover in the last week.** Start in the pilot; it needs time and practice.
- **Forgetting access.** Your keys and accounts must be removed and secrets rotated.

## Practise

Read the Diátaxis overview and the first Google technical writing unit if you have not.

1. For a system you have built, write a one-page architecture overview, a how-to guide for changing a prompt safely and two runbooks for likely incidents.
2. Write two decision records for choices you made, with alternatives and reasons.
3. Ask a friend to follow your how-to guide and one runbook without your help; note every question they ask and fix the docs.

**You are done when you can** watch someone else change a prompt, run the evals and deploy, using only your documents.
