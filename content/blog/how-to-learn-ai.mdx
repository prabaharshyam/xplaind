Most people who ask "how do I learn AI?" get a list of fifty courses. That is the problem, not the answer. The fastest way to learn AI is to decide what you want to do with it, learn only what that needs, and build something real at each step.

This guide gives you that plan. It is free, it works from India on a normal laptop and internet connection, and every step links to a lesson or course you can start today.

## Step 1: Decide what "learning AI" means for you

AI is now three different skills, and they need very different preparation.

1. **Using AI well.** Getting real work done with assistants such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude: writing, research, analysis and everyday tasks, while knowing when not to trust them. No coding. A few weeks.
2. **Building with AI.** Writing software that uses language models: chatbots over company documents, agents that take actions, automations. This is the fastest-growing kind of AI job, with titles such as AI engineer, GenAI engineer and forward deployed engineer. Needs Python. Months.
3. **Building the models themselves.** Machine learning engineering and research: training, fine-tuning and evaluating models. Needs Python, maths and statistics. A year or more.

There are also roles that sit beside these: AI product managers decide what to build, and automation specialists connect AI to business tools with low-code platforms such as n8n.

If you are not sure, start with the first skill. It takes little time and helps whatever you choose next. Our [path chooser](/paths) describes nine roles, who each one suits and how long it takes.

## Step 2: Learn how AI actually works, in plain words

Before any tool or framework, spend a week on the ideas. You should be able to explain:

- what machine learning is, and how it differs from a program written with rules;
- what a large language model does: it predicts the next piece of text, one token at a time, from patterns it learned in training;
- why that means it can be fluent and wrong at the same time;
- what training data is, and how bias gets into a model.

Good free starting points: our lesson [What is AI](/stop/what-is-ai), Andrew Ng's [AI For Everyone](/go/dlai-ai-for-everyone), NVIDIA's two-hour [Generative AI Explained](/go/nvidia-genai-explained) and Google's [Introduction to Generative AI](/go/google-intro-genai-path). Pick one; you do not need all four.

## Step 3: Use AI every day, and learn to check it

Reading about AI is no substitute for using it. For two weeks, use an assistant for real tasks: draft an email, summarise a long document, plan a trip, explain a concept you are studying. Notice where it helps and where it gets things wrong.

Three habits matter more than any prompt trick:

1. **Give context.** Say who the answer is for, what you want and in what format. Our lesson on [prompting without jargon](/stop/prompting-without-jargon) shows how.
2. **Check what matters.** Numbers, names, dates, laws and anything medical or financial must be checked against a trusted source.
3. **Protect data.** Never paste customer details, passwords or confidential files into a tool your employer has not approved. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act makes organisations responsible for personal data.

If your goal is the first skill, using AI well, you can stop here and go deeper with the [AI for Beginners path](/roadmap/ai-for-beginners).

## Step 4: Learn Python, if you want to build

To build with AI you need to code, and the language is Python. You do not need to be an expert. You need to be able to:

- write functions, loops and conditions;
- work with lists and dictionaries, and read and write files;
- install packages in a virtual environment;
- call a web API and handle the JSON it returns;
- use Git to save and share your work.

That is roughly 60 to 100 hours of practice. The early levels of our [GenAI Engineer path](/roadmap/genai-engineer) cover exactly this, with the best free course for each topic. If you already code in another language, take the [level test](/start) and skip what you know.

## Step 5: Build with language models

This is where most AI engineering jobs are today. Learn in this order, and build a small project at each stage:

1. **Calling a model.** Send your first request to a model API, count tokens, control cost. Lesson: [Your first LLM API call](/stop/first-llm-api-call).
2. **Prompts and structured output.** Write prompts that work reliably and get answers back as JSON your code can use.
3. **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).** Let the model answer from your own documents: embeddings, chunking, vector search and citations. This is the most common thing companies ask AI engineers to build.
4. **Tools and agents.** Let the model call functions and take steps towards a goal, with limits and human approval for risky actions.
5. **Evaluation.** Build a test set and measure quality, so you know whether a change made things better or worse. This is the skill that most separates juniors from seniors.
6. **Deployment.** Serve your application, monitor it, and keep cost and latency in check.

Free courses worth your time here include DeepLearning.AI's short courses, Microsoft's [Generative AI for Beginners](/go/ms-genai-for-beginners), Anthropic's [Building with the Claude API](/go/anthropic-claude-api-course) and Hugging Face's [LLM Course](/go/hf-llm-course). Our [course directory](/resources) lets you filter every course we recommend by path, level and certificate.

## Step 6: Build projects that prove you can do the job

Certificates show you finished a course. Projects show you can do the work, and they are what hiring managers ask about. Good projects:

- solve a real problem for real users, even a small group such as your college or family business;
- use real, messy data, not a cleaned tutorial dataset;
- include an evaluation: how you measured quality and what you improved;
- are on GitHub with a README a stranger can follow, and ideally a short demo video.

Three strong first projects: a question-answering assistant over a set of public documents, such as government scheme guidelines, with citations; an automation that extracts fields from invoices or forms and checks them; and an agent that does a multi-step task with an approval step. Every stage of our paths ends with a project brief like these.

## Step 7: Get ready for interviews

AI interviews test fundamentals (how models, retrieval and evaluation work), system design (design a support assistant for a bank, say) and judgement (what you would do when the model is wrong). Reading answers is not enough: you need to say them out loud, clearly, in one to two minutes.

## Mistakes that slow people down

- **Collecting courses instead of finishing one.** Choose one course per topic and build something with it before moving on.
- **Starting with deep learning maths when you want to build apps.** You can build useful AI applications long before you can derive backpropagation. Learn the maths when your path needs it.
- **Following every new model release.** Fundamentals change slowly; tools change weekly. Learn the fundamentals and check tool docs when you need them.
- **Learning alone without feedback.** Share your projects, ask for reviews, and practise interviews out loud.

## A realistic timeline

At 10 to 15 hours a week:

| Goal | Rough time |
| --- | --- |
| Use AI tools well at work | 3 to 6 weeks |
| Automate business tasks with low-code tools and AI | 3 to 4 months |
| Build AI applications as a developer, starting from zero | 8 to 12 months |
| Build AI applications, already a developer | 4 to 6 months |
| Machine learning engineer | 9 to 12 months or more |

These are estimates, not promises. The level test on each path skips what you already know and gives you a personal estimate.
